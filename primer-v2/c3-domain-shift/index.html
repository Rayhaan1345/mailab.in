When the water changes

Why a model that works in clear water can fail 10 m down, and three ways to prepare for it or adapt: augmentation, new statistics and test-time training

A model is trained in one world and used in another. For SharkCam the world changes with depth: water removes red light first, darkens the picture and adds haze. This is domain shift. The chapter measures it on a pretrained ResNet-18 and on a small digit reader, then tries three defences. Augmentation trains on simulated water. Test-time adaptation re-estimates the stored normalisation statistics on unlabeled new images. Test-time training also updates a few weights while the model is used.

The results include a negative one. Re-estimating statistics rescued the digit reader. On ResNet-18 with 14 photos it collapsed the shark confidence, even where the water had not changed. The chapter says what is known and not known about why. The depth effect comes from a simple model of light in water with an assumed particle term. It does not measure Mumbai water. Photos are credited under the figures.

You will need: batch norm and its stored averages (B6), colour channels (C1), CNNs (C2) and the test set idea (A4).

The water changes the photo

Guess first. SharkCam photographs a shark 10 m under water, where most of the red light is gone and the picture is hazy. A network trained on clear photos was confident at the surface that it saw a shark. At 10 m, will its confidence be lower, the same or higher?

domain shift
the data met in use differ from the data the model was trained on
attenuation coefficient \(K\)
how fast light of one colour fades with depth, per metre; this \(K\) is a different quantity from the keys of attention (D4)
haze
light scattered by particles into the camera, which lowers contrast
shark-class confidence
here, the summed probability of three ImageNet classes: great white shark, tiger shark and hammerhead

Water changes a photo in a predictable way, and a network that never saw that change can lose its footing, gain false confidence, or both.

The photo at the chosen depth (depth.py, run in your browser)

For the Jarvis Island hammerhead the network stays above 0.85 down to 30 m. For the Kuala Lumpur milk sharks it falls from 0.97 at 5 m to 0.49 at 15 m. The three photos without a shark rise from an average of 0.06 at 5 m to 0.46 at 30 m. The coral dot is the slider position. Confidence is a number from 0 to 1. The curves were computed offline for 61 depths from 0 to 30 m.

The classifier is ResNet-18 trained on ImageNet, a set of web photos in 1,000 classes (He et al., 2016, for the architecture). It was not trained for SharkCam. Its three shark classes are summed, so the curve says how strongly the network sees some ImageNet shark. It does not say which species. The eight photos are examples, not statistics.

Move through the photos in turn. The curves show four things. The hammerhead photos keep a high confidence for a long way down, although the colours are far from the surface. The milk sharks of Kuala Lumpur lose confidence early. The spadenose shark on a white board gets more shark-like with depth, from 0.70 at the surface to 0.998 at 10 m, although the animal is the same. And the photos with no shark, a sea turtle and a clownfish, climb to 0.61 and 0.44 at 30 m. One possible reading is that blue haze is part of what the network learned to call a shark, since sharks are photographed under water and the water looks blue-green. This was not tested directly. A cue of this kind is the spurious cue of B9.

The light itself follows a simple law. A colour is multiplied by a factor that falls exponentially with depth, and the factor falls fastest for red.

\[ I_c(z) = I_c(0)\; e^{-K_c\, z} \]

  • \(c\) is a colour (red, green or blue) and \(z\) is the depth in metres.
  • \(I_c(z)\) is the light of colour \(c\) that is left at depth \(z\), and \(I_c(0)\) is its value at the surface.
  • \(K_c\) is the attenuation coefficient of colour \(c\) in units per metre: 0.54 for red, 0.256 for green and 0.209 for blue. Pure water absorbs 0.34, 0.0565 and 0.0092 per metre (Pope and Fry, 1997). The rest, 0.20 per metre for all colours, represents particles. This 0.20 is an assumption. It was chosen so that the mean of the three values, 0.335 per metre, matches the attenuation of the made-up dive log. No Mumbai measurement was found.

The boxes under the figure evaluate the law at the slider depth. At 10 m red keeps \(e^{-0.54 \times 10} = e^{-5.4} = 0.0045\) of its surface light, green \(e^{-2.56} = 0.077\) and blue \(e^{-2.09} = 0.124\). At 5 m the factors are 0.067, 0.278 and 0.352. Red is below 1% after \(\ln(100)/0.54 = 8.5\) m. Haze also lowers the contrast of the picture. depth.py combines the law with a veil of scattered light and a camera that brightens a dark scene.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{out}_c &= G\; e^{-K_c z}\Big( (1 - V)\, J_c + V\, W_c \Big) \\ V &= 1 - e^{-\kappa z} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(J_c\) is the colour of the scene at the surface (in linear light) and \(\text{out}_c\) what the camera records. \(W_c\) is the colour of the veil, a blue-green (0.08, 0.50, 0.55).
  • \(V\) is the share of haze, which grows from 0 as \(1 - e^{-\kappa z}\). The haze rate \(\kappa = 0.06\) per metre is chosen by eye, as is the veil colour.
  • \(G\) is the gain of the camera's automatic exposure. It gives back 60% of the darkening, another number chosen by eye.

This is the structure of the classic underwater image model (Jaffe, 1990), which depth.py cites. It is a simple model. Light travels from the surface to the animal and from the animal to the camera, and the second trip depends on the distance to the animal, which the code ignores. Akkaynak and Treibitz (2018) show that natural water needs different coefficients for direct and scattered light. Treat the pictures as an illustration of the kind of change, not as a prediction for Mumbai. Benchmarks such as ImageNet-C (Hendrycks and Dietterich, 2019) test models against many corruptions of this general kind, though not this one.

Common belief. A model that is right in clear water stays right a little deeper, with slightly lower confidence.

Why it fails. The curves are not smooth declines. One photo falls early, one keeps its confidence to 30 m, one rises, and the photos with no shark gain confidence at depth. The change moves the inputs into a range the network never saw, and what it does there is not under control.

Better account. Domain shift changes the statistics of the inputs, and the response of a model can be anything. Measure it under the shift you expect, on positives and on negatives.

Debug tip. When you test a model against a change, plot its score against the amount of change and include negatives. A curve of only the sharks would look reassuring here. The dashed line, the false alarms, shows that at 30 m the average for the non-sharks is 0.46, against 0.68 for the five sharks.

Training for water you can imagine

Guess first. You train a digit reader on pictures made at random depths between 0 and 15 m, with slightly different water in the pictures. Then you test it in a brown river water that it never saw. Will it do better than a reader trained on clear digits only, about the same, or worse?

data augmentation
training on altered copies of the examples, such as shifted, flipped or murky ones (B7 first meets it as noise on the inputs)
training range
the set of changes the augmentation can produce, here depths of 0 to 15 m in blue-green water
water type
one setting of the attenuation coefficients, the haze and the veil colour

Augmentation teaches a network to ignore the changes you can simulate, and it helps only as far as the simulation covers the water you meet.

Common belief. Augmentation makes a model safe against any change in the input.

Why it fails. In the brown water the augmented network is worse than the one that saw no murky images at all, from 4 m to 10 m. The training family changed the colours in a blue-green way, and the network learned to expect that family.

Better account. Augmentation buys reliability inside the family of changes you simulate. Outside the family it can be as bad as no augmentation, or worse. Choose the augmentation from the changes you expect, and test on a change you did not include.

Practical notes. Apply augmentation to training images only. Augmented copies of a test image in the training set are a leak, in the sense of A4. Typical image augmentations are flips, crops, small turns and changes of brightness, contrast and colour. Their strength is a setting to test on a validation set.

Debug tip. Look at a grid of augmented images before training, as in the panel above. If you cannot read the digit or see the shark, the network cannot either. Water so thick that no digit or shark can be seen teaches the network to guess.

import numpy as np
K = np.array([0.54, 0.256, 0.209])               # per metre for red, green, blue (assumed particle term included)

def murk(light, z, K=K, kappa=0.06, veil=(0.08, 0.50, 0.55), expo=0.6):
    """Linear-light colour (r, g, b) in 0..1 as seen at depth z metres (the model of depth.py)."""
    V = 1 - np.exp(-kappa * z)                   # share of haze
    mixed = light * (1 - V) + np.array(veil) * V
    return mixed * np.exp(-(K - K.min()) * z) * np.exp(-K.min() * z) * np.exp(expo * K.mean() * z)

white = np.ones(3)
for z in (0, 5, 10, 20):
    print(z, murk(white, z).round(3))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                   # augmentation: a random water for each training image
for _ in range(3):
    z = rng.uniform(0, 15); K2 = K * np.exp(0.2 * rng.normal(size=3)); kappa = 0.06 * rng.uniform(0.6, 1.4)
    print(f"depth {z:4.1f} m  K {K2.round(3)}  haze rate {kappa:.3f}  white becomes {murk(white, z, K2, kappa).round(2)}")

Quick quiz

Why is the augmented digit reader still at 97.4% at 20 m, when it was trained only up to 15 m?

Water at 20 m is a stronger version of the same change: less red, more haze and the same colours. The network learned to handle that direction of change. At 30 m it starts to fall (93.7%), because the change is further out.

Name one case where the augmentation of this section would not help SharkCam.

Water with different colours, like the brown water B, or a camera whose lens is fogged or scratched. Both change the pictures in ways the training family never produced.

Adapting to water you cannot predict

Guess first. A batch-norm layer stores a mean and a variance per channel, measured on the training photos. In murky water the numbers that flow into the layer change. To put the network back on track without any labels, could you change only the stored statistics, or must you change weights too? And how many new images would you need: a handful, or thousands?

test-time adaptation
changing a model while it is used, with unlabeled inputs
stored statistics
the running mean and variance per channel that batch norm keeps for use after training (B6)
pool
the unlabeled new images that the new statistics are computed from

Re-estimating the stored statistics on unlabeled new images needs no labels and no gradient steps. It rescued a digit reader whose shift is mostly in colour and contrast. It collapsed the shark confidence of a pretrained network when the pool was only 14 photos.

Mean per channel

Variance per channel (log axes)

In the first layer of ResNet-18 the median channel variance on the murky photos falls from 81% of the stored value at 0 m to 7% at 10 m. In the last layer it falls from 90% to 61%. A dot is one channel. Dots on the dotted line would mean no change. Click a dot to see its numbers below.

This is what the layer sees now, against what it stored. The images are 14 photos with 16 random crops per photo (the pool of the next figures), passed through ResNet-18 with its stored statistics. The first layer reads pixels, so its variance follows the contrast of the picture, and haze removes contrast: the cloud of dots moves below the line. The last layer reads features of the whole picture. Its dots scatter around the line even at 0 m, probably because 14 photos are not a sample of ImageNet. This difference will matter in a moment.

Batch norm normalises a value with a mean and a variance, and its layers keep a stored pair for a channel. Re-estimation replaces the stored pair by the pair measured on the new images.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{x} &= \gamma\,\frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\mathrm{var} + \epsilon}} + \beta \\ \mu_{\text{new}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i, \qquad \mathrm{var}_{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(x_i - \mu_{\text{new}}\big)^2 \\ \mu &= (1-\alpha)\,\mu_{\text{train}} + \alpha\,\mu_{\text{new}} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(x\) is one value of one channel. The \(N\) values in the sums are those of that channel over all new images and all positions in them.
  • \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) are the learned scale and shift of the layer. They stay as trained.
  • \(\mu_{\text{train}}\) is the stored mean, and the variance is mixed in the same way. \(\alpha\) is the share of the new statistics: 0 keeps the stored ones and 1 uses only the new ones. \(\epsilon\) is a small constant, \(10^{-5}\) in PyTorch.

A worked example with made-up numbers. A channel has stored mean 2 and variance 4, so a standard deviation of 2. In murky water the channel has mean 0.5 and variance 0.25, standard deviation 0.5. A value \(x = 1\) is normalised to \((1 - 2)/2 = -0.5\) with the stored statistics, and to \((1 - 0.5)/0.5 = +1\) with the new ones. The next layer receives numbers that differ by 1.5 standard deviations, from one value. With \(\alpha = 0.25\) the mean is \(0.75 \times 2 + 0.25 \times 0.5 = 1.625\). No labels are used anywhere, and no weight is learned by gradient descent.

Re-estimating the statistics on 256 unlabeled murky digits lifts the clean-trained reader from 68.6% to 98.3% at 2 m and from 10.6% to 87.2% at 10 m. In the brown water it also rescues the augmented reader, from 52.6% to 97.8% at 4 m. The teal line is the mean of 5 random pools, and the spread between pools is at most 0.3 points.

On the digit reader the result is strong. The statistics were re-estimated on 256 unlabeled murky training digits and the network was tested on the 10,000 test digits. In blue-green water the augmented reader gains no accuracy from it (98.0% against 98.0% at 10 m). In brown water adaptation works where augmentation failed, with no knowledge of the water. It does not cure all cases: at 20 m in blue-green water the clean-trained reader reaches only 36.2%. Here are four more measurements, all on this digit model at 10 m in blue-green water. Pools of 2, 8 and 256 images give 84.3%, 87.2% and 87.3% (single pools, while the chart shows the mean of five), so digits need almost no images. A pool of only the digit 1 gives 81.7%, against 86.9% for a fair mix. Mixing the new statistics with the stored ones gives 13.7% at \(\alpha = 0.25\), 39.2% at 0.5, 80.4% at 0.75 and 87.3% at 1. There is no safe halfway when the stored values are far off. And on clean digits, re-estimating changes the accuracy from 98.56% to 98.60%.

Now the same idea on shark photos. The switch below sets which statistics ResNet-18 uses. There are three choices, all computed offline. The first uses the stored statistics. The second mixes in 25% new ones. The third uses the statistics of a pool of 14 photos with 16 random crops per photo, made at the chosen depth.

The photo at the chosen depth

On these photos, re-estimating all statistics pushes the average shark confidence of the five sharks from 0.89 down to 0.08 at 15 m, and the non-sharks to about 0. Mixing in 25% of the new statistics changes little: 0.84. The adapted network is quieter and no better. At 0 m, where the world has not changed, re-estimating already lowers the average for the five sharks from 0.91 to 0.32.

This is a negative result, and the number at 0 m shows it before any depth is involved. In the code below, re-estimating on a pool made at 0 m takes the milk sharks of Kuala Lumpur from 0.97 to 0.00. It takes the Jarvis Island hammerhead from 1.00 to 0.70. What was measured: ResNet-18 and a pool of 14 photos with 16 random crops per photo, which makes 224 images. The test photos are among them. The test images were five shark photos and three non-shark photos, and there was one run. A likely reason is that 14 photos are not a fair sample of the world that ResNet-18's stored statistics describe, which has 1,000 classes. The cause was not isolated. The collapse stays the same under three variations. At 0 m the average shark confidence falls from 0.91 to 0.15 with a shuffled pool. It falls to 0.11 with the pool passed as one batch of 224 and to 0.06 with the 14 photos alone and no random crops. So the order of the batches and the random crops are not the cause.

Several remedies are known, including mixing and larger and more varied pools (Schneider et al., 2020; Nado et al., 2020). Mixing 25% was too weak to matter here. A larger pool was not tried, because the photo set has only 14 pictures. There is also a second reading, which was not tested. The stored network may have called murky blue pictures sharks partly because of the haze, the spurious cue of the last section. Normalising the haze away would then remove that cue along with the confidence.

Common belief. Adapting to the new images can only help, because the model gets to see them.

Why it fails. On the digits, adaptation was close to harmless on clean digits (98.56% to 98.60%) and gave large gains. On ResNet-18 with 14 photos it changed the answers at 0 m, where the stored statistics were right. A likely reason is that statistics from a small or odd pool replace good stored numbers with poor ones.

Better account. Re-estimating statistics helps under three conditions. The shift is mainly in the statistics. The new images are a fair and large enough sample of the new world. A check on source-like images shows no harm. The method was developed and tested with large batches of varied images (Li et al., 2016; Schneider et al., 2020).

Debug tip. Before you trust an adaptation method, run it where the world has not changed. Adapt on clean images and compare the answers with the stored model on clean images. The digit reader passes (98.56% to 98.60%). ResNet-18 on 14 photos fails (milk sharks 0.97 to 0.00). The check is short and would have saved a dive.

import copy, torch, torch.nn as nn

def reestimate(model, images, alpha=1.0):
    """Replace the stored batch-norm mean and variance by those of unlabeled new images (no labels, no weight updates)."""
    new = copy.deepcopy(model)
    for m in new.modules():
        if isinstance(m, nn.BatchNorm2d):
            m.reset_running_stats(); m.momentum = None      # None: a plain average over all batches
    new.train()                                             # in train mode batch norm uses and records batch statistics
    with torch.no_grad():
        for i in range(0, len(images), 64): new(images[i:i + 64])
    for a, b in zip(new.modules(), model.modules()):        # alpha = share of the new statistics
        if isinstance(a, nn.BatchNorm2d):
            a.running_mean.mul_(alpha).add_((1 - alpha) * b.running_mean)
            a.running_var.mul_(alpha).add_((1 - alpha) * b.running_var)
    return new.eval()

def make_pool(z):                                           # 14 photos x 16 random crops, all at depth z
    pool = []
    for photo in photos:
        d = degrade(photo, z)
        for k in range(16):
            torch.manual_seed(100 + k); pool.append(aug(d))
    return torch.stack(pool)

for z in (0, 10):
    adapted = reestimate(model, make_pool(z))

        print(f"{z:2d} m  {f:13s} shark confidence: stored {before:.2f}, re-estimated {after:.2f}")

The code assumes model, photos, files, tf, aug and degrade from the previous block. Here aug is a random resized crop, a flip and normalisation. Setting momentum = None makes PyTorch use a plain average of all batches, instead of the exponential average that stays near the old values. Calling .train() is the step that makes the layer use batch statistics. Forgetting torch.no_grad() only wastes memory here. Forgetting to switch back to .eval() means that the answer for an image depends on the other images of its batch.

Quick quiz

A channel has stored mean 3 and variance 9. On new images its mean is 1 and its variance 1. What does the value x = 2 become under the two, ignoring \(\epsilon\), \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\)?

Stored: \((2 - 3)/3 = -0.33\). New: \((2 - 1)/1 = +1\). The two normalised values differ by 1.33 standard deviations.

Why does a pool of 14 photos fail on ResNet-18 but a pool of 8 digits work on the digit reader?

Digits are a small, simple world: ten classes, one size, centred, and nearly the same statistics from any handful. The stored statistics of ResNet-18 describe a world of 1,000 classes of photos, and 14 photos are not a fair sample of it. This reason was not tested directly.

Test-time training: update some weights

Guess first. Re-estimating statistics changes only averages. Suppose the network may also change a few of its weights while SharkCam dives, with no labels. What could it use as a loss? And how often should it update: after a single frame, after a block of frames, or never during the dive?

test-time training
updating some weights while the model is used, with a loss computed from the inputs alone
entropy \(H\)
how spread out the predicted probabilities are: 0 for one sure class, \(\ln 10 = 2.30\) for ten equal classes (0.7)
chunk
a block of frames that is read before one update
fast weights
weights that change during use, while the others stay fixed

Test-time training turns use into a loop: read a chunk, update a few weights with a loss that needs no labels, then predict with the updated model.

On a stream of 8,000 digits that get deeper from 0 to 12 m, the frozen network falls from 99% on the first chunk to 11% on the last. Updating the statistics alone keeps the average at 92.6%, and also updating the scale and shift with one entropy step per chunk lifts it to 97.3%. Press Play: the three stages light up in turn and the chart grows one chunk at a time. The digits are the test digits of the last figure, in blue-green water.

The stream has 40 chunks of 200 digits. Chunk \(t\) is made at depth \(12\,t/39\) metres. The network is the digit reader with batch norm, trained on clean digits. The frozen copy never changes. The other two copies follow the loop. They predict the next chunk with what they learned from the earlier chunks, so no chunk helps to label itself. Then they read the new chunk. The first moves the stored statistics toward the chunk's own statistics (an exponential average with weight 0.5). The second does that and also takes one Adam step on the scale and shift of the batch-norm layers. These are 96 of the network's 20,874 numbers. The step lowers the entropy of its predictions on the chunk.

\[ \begin{aligned} H(\mathbf{p}) &= -\sum_{k} p_k \ln p_k \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial \theta}, \qquad \theta = \{\gamma,\ \beta\} \ \text{of the batch-norm layers} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathbf{p}\) is the vector of ten class probabilities for one frame, and \(H\) is averaged over the frames of the chunk.
  • \(\theta\) is the set of weights that are allowed to change, and \(\eta\) is the step size (0.01, used with Adam, B5).
  • Low entropy means a confident prediction. The loss needs no label, which is why it can run during use.

Worked values. For probabilities (0.9, 0.05, 0.05) the entropy is \(-(0.9 \ln 0.9 + 2 \times 0.05 \ln 0.05) = 0.095 + 0.300 = 0.394\). A one-hot answer has entropy 0, and ten equal probabilities have \(\ln 10 = 2.303\). The step moves the scale and shift so that predictions become more confident. This is the idea of Tent (Wang et al., 2021). It does not know whether the confident answer is right. Tent as published normalises a batch with its own statistics and predicts in the same pass. The loop here predicts a chunk with what earlier chunks taught, which is a stricter test. Sun et al. (2020) use a different label-free loss: they train on a self-supervised task using the test input itself before predicting.

Read the chart with two cautions. First, the step size matters. Two values were tried, 0.001 and 0.01, and the larger one is shown. With 0.001 the average over the stream is 93.8% and the last 10 chunks average 87.4%. With 0.01 they are 97.3% and 96.8%. The value was chosen by looking at this same stream, so 97.3% is optimistic. No other values were tried, and in the field there is no labelled stream to tune on. Second, one stream and one trained network is one trial.

Common belief. A deployed model keeps learning from the images it sees.

Why it fails. Normally the weights do not change after training. The frozen line in the chart is what that looks like: it does not recover as the water gets deeper. The other two lines change only because someone designed a loop with a loss, a step size and a rule for which weights may move.

Better account. Learning during use is a design choice with risks. Entropy minimisation rewards confidence, so a wrong but confident answer can be reinforced. A sensible loop keeps a frozen copy to compare against, updates few weights, and starts afresh for a new dive.

Bridge to my project. My work at MBZUAI, in Salman Khan's group, uses a loop of the same kind on a language model. In-Place TTT, from ByteDance Seed, treats the down projection of selected MLP blocks of a language model as fast weights. It updates them chunk by chunk as the model reads, with a target built from the input, and generation never pauses. I am porting the method from text models to a Qwen vision-language model so that it can adapt to an input after deployment. My motivation is the question of this chapter: recognising a shark when the water, the light and the animals change between dives. The update differs from the entropy step above, and the weights that move are the down projections of selected MLP blocks. My primer explains the update step by step, and the project page has the study. Test-time training for long text comes back in D6.

Debug tip. Keep two predictions for all frames, one from a frozen copy and one from the updating model, and log how often they disagree and how the entropy moves. A sudden rise of disagreement or a fall of the entropy to nearly 0 means the loop is locking onto its own answers. Reset the model at the start of a dive.

# the core of the script that made the chart
def run(mode, lr=0.01):
    m = copy.deepcopy(clean_model)                        # a fresh copy for a new dive
    for mod in m.modules():
        if isinstance(mod, torch.nn.BatchNorm2d): mod.momentum = 0.5
    for p in m.parameters(): p.requires_grad_(False)
    for p in bn_params(m): p.requires_grad_(True)         # only the scale and shift may move
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(bn_params(m), lr=lr)
    acc = []
    for t in range(40):
        x, y = chunk(t)                                   # 200 frames at depth 12 * t / 39
        acc.append(accuracy(m, x, y))                     # predict with what earlier chunks taught
        if mode == "frozen": continue
        m.train(); p = m(x).softmax(1)                    # read: batch statistics, stored ones move toward them
        if mode == "tent":
            entropy = -(p * torch.log(p + 1e-8)).sum(1).mean()    # a loss that needs no labels
            opt.zero_grad(); entropy.backward(); opt.step()       # update a few weights
    return sum(acc) / 40

Quick quiz

Why does the loop predict the next chunk with the model from before the chunk was read?

If a chunk helped to set the weights that label it, a wrong confident answer could reinforce itself immediately. Predicting first keeps the test fair. The same order is used in In-Place TTT, where a chunk is read with the weights from before its own update.

Entropy minimisation made the digit reader better. Name one way it could make a reader worse.

If the first answers are wrong but confident, lowering the entropy makes the network more sure of its mistakes. Confidence is not accuracy.

Come back later

In B6, which statistics does batch norm use in training mode and which in evaluation mode, and which line of reestimate changes the mode?

In training mode it uses the mean and variance of the current batch and records them. In evaluation mode it uses the stored ones. new.train() switches to batch statistics for the pass over the new images. The final .eval() switches back, so that predictions use the new stored values.

A shark classifier scores 95% on its test set. Why can that number mislead about its performance 10 m down, and what test would you add? (A4)

The test set was drawn from the same conditions as the training set, so it cannot show shift. Add a test set that applies the change you expect, such as the depth model, and report the score for sharks and non-sharks as the change grows.

The loop used Adam with a step size of 0.01 and a new optimiser for a dive. Why do the first updates of a fresh Adam move a weight by about the step size? (B5)

With Adam's \(m\) and \(v\) both empty, bias correction makes the first update \(\eta\,\hat{m}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon) \approx \eta \cdot\) sign of the slope. So the 96 numbers move by about 0.01 in the first step, whatever their slopes.
