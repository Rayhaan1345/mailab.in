Primer

Questions instead of answers.

A student learns a method by doing its steps. A chatbot that hands over the answer does the steps for them. The Socratic Tutor plugin makes Claude ask the question that lets the student take the next step.

Where the idea comes from

The method is named after Socrates, the Athenian philosopher who appears in Plato's dialogues. Socrates rarely told people what to think. He asked questions, and the person answering found the gaps in their own ideas.

In the dialogue called Meno, Socrates asks a boy with no training in geometry to double the area of a square. He asks questions and draws lines, and the boy works out the answer. Step through the scene below.

The same idea in a tutoring session

A student in grade 8 asks whether x = 11/2 solves 2x + 4 = 7. Here are two ways to reply. The first fixes the answer. The second follows the plugin's rules for checking an answer.

The questioning has limits. A date, a definition or a formula gets a plain answer, because those are things to look up. Questions are for reasoning. A student can also ask for the answer at any point, and the tutor gives it.

What the studies show

Four sources the plugin is built on.

The plugin's README cites four sources. Here is what they found, in plain terms, and what the plugin takes from them.

Field experiment · mathematics

A chatbot can help practice and hurt the exam

Nearly 1,000 high school students in Turkey practised mathematics in one of three ways. One group used a plain GPT-4 chat. A second group used a GPT-4 tutor designed to give hints instead of answers. A third group used their notes and textbook. Then all three sat an exam without AI.

The plain chat raised practice scores and lowered exam scores. The authors conclude that students used it as a crutch. The hint-giving tutor raised practice scores further, and its students scored about the same as the control group on the exam.

What the plugin takes from it. Hints come before answers, and the student makes an attempt first.

Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı, Mariman. Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning. PNAS, 2025. Percentages as reported in the working paper.

Randomised trial · physics

A well-built AI tutor can beat a good class

In a Harvard introductory physics course, 194 students took two lessons in two weeks. One lesson was an active-learning class. The other was an AI tutor at home, built on the same teaching principles. The two groups had the lessons in opposite orders.

After the AI lesson the median post-test score was 4.5, against 3.5 after the class, from a pre-test median of 2.75. The median time on the AI lesson was 49 minutes. The authors count the class as 60 minutes of learning. Students also reported more engagement and motivation with the AI tutor.

The tutor was designed to limit how much it said at once and to guide students through a problem in order. It gave feedback whenever a student asked. The paper leaves open when an AI tutor should give an answer and when it should guide. The plugin takes a position on that question, set out under When the tutor gives the answer.

What the plugin takes from it. Short turns, one step at a time, and support that responds to the student.

Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne, Ponti. AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning. Scientific Reports 15, 17458, 2025.

Laboratory experiment · memory

Recalling beats rereading, a week later

College students learned a short prose passage in four 5-minute periods. One group read it four times. A second group read it three times and then wrote down what they remembered. A third group read it once and then recalled it three times.

The group that reread the passage the most was also the most confident it would remember it. A week later it remembered the least. Recalling feels harder than rereading, and it led to better memory a week later.

What the plugin takes from it. The quiz-me skill asks before it tells, and a tutoring session ends with the student's own summary.

Roediger and Karpicke. Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention. Psychological Science 17(3), 249–255, 2006. Figures from Experiment 2.

Design framework · Google

Five principles for AI that teaches

Google's learning team wrote a framework for building AI tools that support learning. It starts from a problem the plugin also targets. A chatbot is trained to complete the task for the user, and that skips the effort learning needs. The framework names a typical failure in chat, the "wall of text", where a long explanation overloads the reader.

It sets out five principles. The plugin's README names four of them as the basis for its rules. The table maps all five to the plugin.

PrincipleIn the plugin
Encourage active learningThe student does the steps. The tutor asks one question per turn and gives the answer after an attempt.
Manage cognitive loadA turn is at most three sentences and one question. The tutor works on the one thing blocking the student.
Stimulate curiosity and motivationThe README does not claim this one. The closest rules: the tutor starts from the student's own attempt, and praise names the step that worked.
Adapt to the learnerHints rise only when the student stalls and fade as the student gets steps right alone. Questions match the student's class or year.
Deepen metacognitionThe session ends with the student stating the method in two sentences of their own.

The framework also lists the behaviours an AI tutor should show. They include withholding direct solutions, giving graduated hints tied to the student's error and fading support as the student improves. It cites a review of 225 studies in college science and mathematics, where active learning raised exam scores by about half a standard deviation.

Wilkowski. Google's Pedagogical Framework for Generative AI to Support Human Learning. Google, 2026.

How a Claude plugin works

Skills, plugins and what Claude reads, and when.

The plugin contains no code. It is a folder of written instructions that Claude reads when a conversation calls for them.

Skills

A skill is a folder of instructions for one kind of task. Its main file is called SKILL.md. The file opens with a short header that gives the skill a name and a description of when to use it. The rest of the file is the instructions. A skill can also keep longer reference files beside SKILL.md.

Plugins

A plugin packages skills so that people can install them in one step. It can also bundle connectors to outside services, commands, agents and hooks. A small file, .claude-plugin/plugin.json, gives the plugin its name and version. Socratic Tutor contains two skills and no other parts. Open the files below to see what Claude reads.

When does a skill load?

Claude does not read a skill in full at the start of a conversation. Anthropic's documentation describes three stages.

  1. It knows what is available. Claude sees the name and description of a skill that is turned on.
  2. It loads the one that fits. When a request matches a description, Claude reads that skill's SKILL.md.
  3. It opens extra files when needed. If the instructions point to a reference file, Claude opens it then.

So the description decides when the tutor appears. In Claude Code, a loaded skill's instructions stay in the conversation for later turns. Pick a message below to see which skill the plugin's test cases expect.

The design of the tutor

The rules in SKILL.md and how they fit together.

How a session runs

  1. Find out where they are. Ask what the task is and what they have tried.
  2. Diagnose before teaching. Find the one thing blocking them: a missing idea, a misread question, a slip or a wrong plan.
  3. Ask one question at a time. A turn has at most three sentences and one question.
  4. Climb the ladder. Move through six kinds of question, from clarifying the problem to generalising the method.
  5. Raise hints when they stall. A nudge, then the concept, then a worked step, then the full solution.
  6. Confirm and move on. A right step gets a few words, then the next question.
  7. Fade the support. After two right steps in a row without a hint, ask with less guidance.
  8. Finish with their summary. The student states the method in two sentences.

One question per turn

The rule the skill repeats most often is one question per turn. Two questions at once split the student's attention. The skill tells Claude to read the last line of a turn before sending it and to cut any second ask, in English or any other language.

The question ladder and the hint scale

Two scales work together. The question ladder sets the kind of question: clarify the problem, recall an idea, try a simpler case, apply it, check the result, generalise. The hint scale sets how much help comes with the question when the student is stuck. Play the student below and watch both scales move.

    When the tutor gives the answer

    The method is a default the student can switch off. The skill gives the answer in four cases.

    • The student has made at least two attempts and asks for it.
    • The student says "just tell me" or that they are out of time.
    • The question is a lookup: a fact, a definition, a formula or syntax.
    • The person asking is a teacher or parent preparing to help someone else.

    After a full answer the tutor asks one short question to check the student can redo the key step. The skill tells Claude never to refuse an answer as a matter of principle and never to pretend not to know it. The one refusal is a graded test in progress. The tutor offers to go through the method afterwards.

    Checking an answer and correcting errors

    When a student brings an answer, the tutor says first whether it is right. If it is wrong, the tutor asks about the step where it first goes wrong and leaves the correct answer out of that turn. The tutor exposes an error with a question, for example about a sign that flipped when a term moved across. If the same error remains after two questions, the tutor states it plainly and shows the corrected step. The skill also forbids calling a wrong answer "close".

    Subject notes

    Students get stuck in familiar places. The reference file subject-notes.md lists, for eight subject areas, what to ask first, common blockers with the question that moves a student past them, and what to give plainly. Contributors improve the plugin mostly by adding to this file.

    Quiz me

    The second skill runs retrieval practice, the method tested by Roediger and Karpicke above. It confirms the topic and level, then asks five questions unless the student asks for a different number. It asks one question at a time and grades the answer before the next. A wrong answer gets a question that exposes the error. The quiz ends with the score, the two weakest ideas and one thing to do before the next quiz.

    Testing the plugin

    The repository includes twelve test cases for Claude Code's claude plugin eval command. A case is a student's message plus checks on the reply. Most cases have two checks: a rubric that a second model grades, and a check that the right skill loaded.

    CaseWhat the reply must do
    Homework helpAsk a question without giving the roots
    Hinglish homeworkReply in Hinglish, with one question
    Check a wrong answerSay it is wrong, then ask, without correcting it
    Answer when askedAfter two attempts and "out of time", give the roots
    Debugging codePoint at the bug with a question, never paste a fixed program
    Essay introductionAsk about the claim, never rewrite
    Proof by inductionStart at the base case, without the full proof
    Exam in progressDecline briefly and offer help afterwards
    Quiz meAsk one quiz question and wait
    DefinitionAnswer plainly in a line or two
    Teacher askingExplain directly, with a worked example
    Unrelated questionAnswer directly in the first sentence

    The eval command also runs the same cases with the plugin switched off and reports the difference. That tells us what the plugin adds, since Claude might pass some cases on its own.

    Privacy

    The plugin is Markdown files. It runs no code, connects to no server and collects no data. The tutor may ask a student's class or year to pitch its questions, and it asks for no other personal detail. Conversations are handled by Anthropic under the terms of the Claude product in use, as they would be without the plugin.

    Why the Lab built it

    From our sessions to a default setting.

    Mumbai AI Lab runs sessions for school students in Navi Mumbai. We teach them to use AI as a tutor that asks questions, so they keep doing the reasoning themselves. The studies above show why. The same chatbot can raise learning or lower it, depending on how it is used.

    Students do most of their studying at home, between sessions. That is when a student with a deadline is most tempted to ask for the answer. The plugin makes the questioning approach the default inside Claude, so the method from the sessions is there when the student studies alone.

    The rules follow the method we teach in sessions. The tutor replies in the language the student writes in, including Hinglish, which many students in Mumbai use. When a student needs the answer, the tutor gives it.
