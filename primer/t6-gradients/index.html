Slopes and gradients

How much does the output move when you nudge the input?

Training changes a model's weights so that its error falls. To know which way to change a weight, you need the slope of the error with respect to that weight. This chapter builds that slope from nudging. It extends the slope to many weights at once (the gradient), passes it through chained steps (the chain rule) and adds how fast the slope itself changes (curvature).

You do not need limits or integrals. A slope here is what you measure by nudging an input a tiny amount and watching the output.

You will need: functions, exponents and logarithms (chapter 0.3), and for the gradient section vectors and dot products (chapter 0.5).

Slope as sensitivity

Guess first. A shark of 80 cm grows by 1 cm. A shark of 50 cm also grows by 1 cm. Weight grows with the cube of length. Does the longer shark gain about the same weight as the shorter one, about 1.5 times as much, or about 2.5 times as much?

slope
rise divided by run: how far the output moves per unit step of the input
derivative
the slope of a curve at one point, written \(f'(x)\) or \(df/dx\)
tangent
the straight line that touches the curve at a point and has the same slope there
nudge
a small change \(h\) in the input, used to measure a slope

The derivative says how much the output moves for a small nudge of the input, and on a curve it changes from point to point.

Make the nudge large and the chord tilts away from the tangent. Make it small and the two agree. Mathematicians define this with limits. In this primer a nudge small enough that the curve looks straight is good enough.

\[ f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \qquad\text{so}\qquad f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h \]

  • \(f(x)\) is the output of the function at input \(x\). Here \(x\) is a length and \(f(x)\) a weight.
  • \(f'(x)\), read "f prime of x", is the slope at \(x\). It is itself a function: give it an \(x\), it returns a slope.
  • \(h\) is the nudge. The sign \(\approx\) means "close to, when \(h\) is small".

The second form is the one that predicts. At 80 cm the slope is 0.0768 kg per cm, so a shark that grows 2 cm gains about \(2 \times 0.0768 = 0.154\) kg. The true gain is 0.157 kg. The prediction is a little low because the slope keeps rising during the 2 cm.

Where does the rule for powers come from? Nudge \(L\) by \(h\) in \(W = 4\times10^{-6}L^3\). Expanding \((L+h)^3 = L^3 + 3L^2h + 3Lh^2 + h^3\) shows that the rise is \(4\times10^{-6}(3L^2h + 3Lh^2 + h^3)\). Divide by \(h\) and the chord slope is \(4\times10^{-6}(3L^2 + 3Lh + h^2)\). As \(h\) shrinks the last two terms vanish and \(1.2\times10^{-5}L^2\) remains. At 80 cm with \(h=1\) the chord slope is \(4\times10^{-6}\times(19{,}200 + 240 + 1) = 0.0778\), a little above the slope of 0.0768.

A second forecast, at 50 cm. The slope is \(1.2\times10^{-5}\times 2500 = 0.03\) kg per cm, so 2 cm of growth should add about 0.06 kg. The true gain is \(4\times10^{-6}(52^3-50^3) = 0.0624\) kg, which is 4% more. For this curve the forecast is low by about \(h/L\): 2.5% at 80 cm and 4% at 50 cm. A slope is a promise about tiny nudges, and it loosens as the nudge grows relative to the curve.

Common belief. A curve has one slope, like a line.

Why it fails. Only a straight line has the same slope at all points. On this curve the slope at 80 cm is 2.56 times the slope at 50 cm.

Better account. The derivative is a function. It takes a position and returns the slope there.

Debug tip. A slope has units: output units per input unit. The values 0.0768 kg per cm and 76.8 g per cm are the same slope. If a slope looks absurdly large or small, check that the input and output use the units you think they do.

# a made-up law, W = 4e-6 * L^3, in kg

    return 4e-6 * L ** 3

print(round(by_formula, 4), "kg per cm, by formula")
print(round(weight(82) - weight(80), 4), "kg gained going from 80 to 82 cm")

In practice. Libraries find slopes by rule. A nudge test is still the standard check for a slope you wrote by hand. With 64-bit numbers a nudge near \(10^{-5}\), taken on both sides of the point, works well. Much smaller nudges lose digits to rounding, as the practice problems show.

Used in: the slope of an error is what gradient descent follows (A2), and the nudge test is the basis of gradient checking (B4).

Quick quiz

What does a derivative of 0.03 kg per cm mean for a shark of 50 cm?

A small extra length adds about 0.03 kg per cm: growing 1 cm adds about 30 g. The statement is accurate for small nudges only.

Why does the chord slope change when you change the nudge h?

The chord joins two points on a curve, and a bigger gap lets the curve bend away from the tangent. For tiny h the curve looks straight and the chord matches the tangent.

What is the slope of the weight law at 100 cm, and how does it compare with the slope at 50 cm?

\(1.2\times10^{-5}\times 100^2 = 0.12\) kg per cm. That is 4 times the slope at 50 cm, because the slope goes with \(L^2\) and doubling \(L\) quadruples it.

The slopes this primer uses

Guess first. The sigmoid curve climbs from 0 to 1 and is steepest in the middle. Is its steepest slope 1, 0.5 or 0.25?

rule
a formula that gives the slope without any nudging
sigmoid \(\sigma(z)\)
a smooth curve that climbs from 0 to 1 in an S, built from \(e^{-z}\) (A3, B1)
ReLU
\(\max(0, z)\): zero for negative \(z\), equal to \(z\) for positive \(z\) (B1)
saturated
stuck in a flat tail of a curve, where the slope is close to zero

A handful of rules give the slope of all the functions in this primer. The sigmoid's slope is \(\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), never above 0.25, and ReLU's slope is 0 or 1.

Function

The sigmoid's slope peaks at 0.25 at z = 0 and drops to about 0.007 at z = 5, where the curve is almost flat.

Slide \(z\) to the right and watch the coral slope curve. In the tails the output hardly reacts to a nudge of \(z\), so anything feeding that neuron learns slowly. B4 and B6 return to this as vanishing gradients. The table lists the rules you will meet. All the rules here follow from the power rule, the exponential and the logarithm, combined with the chain rule in a later section.

ReLU has a corner at \(z = 0\), where no single slope exists. Most libraries use 0 there. It rarely matters, because inputs seldom hit the corner.

The table in use. Take \(f(x) = 3x^2 + 2\ln x + 5\) at \(x = 2\). The constant 5 drops out, \(3x^2\) has slope \(6x\) and \(2\ln x\) has slope \(2/x\). The slope is \(6\cdot2 + 2/2 = 13\), and a nudge of \(10^{-6}\) gives 13.0000. Now compare activations. Tanh has slope \(1-\tanh^2 z\), which is 1 at \(z=0\) and 0.071 at \(z=2\). The sigmoid has 0.25 and 0.105 at the same points. At its peak tanh passes on four times as much of a nudge, and it falls off faster in the tails.

The peak of 0.25 matters in a stack. With weights near 1, a sigmoid layer passes on at most a quarter of a nudge, so ten of them pass on at most \(0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}\). This is one reason deep networks avoid sigmoids in their hidden layers (B4).

Common belief. Where the output is large, the slope is large.

Why it fails. A sigmoid output of 0.999 has a slope near 0.001. The value says where you are on the curve. The slope says how much a nudge matters there.

Better account. Track the value and the slope separately. A saturated unit can have a big output and a tiny slope.

import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

h = 1e-6
for z in [0.0, 2.0, 5.0]:
    nudged = (sigmoid(z + h) - sigmoid(z)) / h
    formula = sigmoid(z) * (1 - sigmoid(z))
    print(f"z = {z}: nudged {nudged:.4f}, formula {formula:.4f}")

relu = lambda z: np.maximum(0, z)
for z in [-2.0, 3.0]:
    print(f"ReLU slope at {z}:", round((relu(z + h) - relu(z)) / h, 3))

In practice. Deep-learning libraries store one rule per operation and combine them with the chain rule, so you rarely write a slope by hand. You still need the table to explain what training does. ReLU became a common default in hidden layers partly because its slope is 1 when the unit is on, so it does not shrink the signal.

Used in: the sigmoid and ReLU slopes feed neurons (B1) and backpropagation (B4); flat tails lead to vanishing gradients (B6).

Quick quiz

What is the sigmoid's slope at z = 0, and why is that its maximum?

It is \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\). The product \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\) is largest when \(\sigma = 0.5\), and \(\sigma = 0.5\) at \(z = 0\).

A ReLU unit receives \(z = -3\). What does a nudge of \(z\) do to its output?

No change at all: the slope is 0 there. A unit stuck in that region passes no learning signal back (a "dead" ReLU, discussed in B8).

What is the slope of \(5x^4\) at \(x = 2\)?

The rule for powers gives \(5\times4x^3 = 20x^3\), which is \(20\times8 = 160\).

Partial derivatives

Guess first. A model has two dials, \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), and an error \(L\) that depends on both. You turn only \(w_1\) and leave \(w_2\) alone. Does the answer to "how fast does \(L\) change?" depend on where \(w_2\) is set?

loss \(L\)
one number that scores the model's error: lower is better (A2 gives it a formula)
partial derivative
the slope of \(L\) along one weight while the other weights are frozen, written \(\partial L/\partial w_1\)
slice
the curve you get by freezing all weights but one
contour line
a line joining all points that have the same loss

A partial derivative is an ordinary slope on a slice: freeze all the other weights and nudge one.

Click the contour map to move the point.

Freezing \(w_2\) turns the map into a curve in \(w_1\) alone, and the tangent to that curve has slope \(\partial L/\partial w_1\). Move the point and both slices change.

The map is a view from above, like a hill drawn with contour lines. The numbers on the contour lines give the loss. The horizontal navy line is the slice where \(w_2\) is frozen; the sand vertical line is the slice where \(w_1\) is frozen. Both slices appear on the right as ordinary curves with ordinary tangents. Move the point up and down: the top-right curve shifts and its slope changes, even though you never touched \(w_1\). The partial derivative depends on where all the weights are, including the ones you did not nudge.

…∂L/∂w1: slope along w1and…∂L/∂w2: slope along w2at…(w1, w2)

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} \approx \frac{L(w_1 + h,\; w_2) - L(w_1,\; w_2)}{h} \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} \approx \frac{L(w_1,\; w_2 + h) - L(w_1,\; w_2)}{h} \]

  • \(\partial\) is a curly "d". It says other variables are frozen while this one is nudged.
  • \(L(w_1 + h,\; w_2)\) is the loss after nudging only \(w_1\).

A worked slice. Take the bowl of the next section, \(L = 0.5(w_1-0.5)^2 + (w_2+0.5)^2 + 0.6(w_1-0.5)(w_2+0.5)\). Freeze \(w_2 = 1\), so \(w_2+0.5 = 1.5\). Then \(L = 0.5(w_1-0.5)^2 + 2.25 + 0.9(w_1-0.5)\), a curve in \(w_1\) alone. Its slope is \((w_1-0.5) + 0.9\), which is 2.4 at \(w_1 = 2\). Freeze \(w_2 = -0.5\) instead. The cross term vanishes and the slope at \(w_1 = 2\) is 1.5. The same weight gives two slopes, because the term \(0.6(w_1-0.5)(w_2+0.5)\) ties the slope along \(w_1\) to the value of \(w_2\).

Analogy. On a hillside, the slope towards the east and the slope towards the north are two different numbers. You measure either one by walking along a single compass direction.

Where it breaks. A hillside has two directions to check. A model can have millions of weights, so it has millions of partial derivatives, and you cannot draw the hill. The idea extends to any number of weights. The picture does not.

Debug tip. If a hand-written derivative disagrees with a nudge test, check which variable you nudged. Swapping \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) in a formula gives a perfectly smooth, wrong answer.

def L(w1, w2):
    a, b = w1 - 0.5, w2 + 0.5
    return 0.5 * a ** 2 + b ** 2 + 0.6 * a * b

h = 1e-6
for w2 in [1.0, -0.5]:
    slope = (L(2 + h, w2) - L(2 - h, w2)) / (2 * h)     # nudge w1 only
    print(f"w2 frozen at {w2}: slope along w1 = {slope:.3f}")
w2 frozen at 1.0: slope along w1 = 2.400
w2 frozen at -0.5: slope along w1 = 1.500

In practice. A model with a million weights has a million partial derivatives. Nudging the weights one at a time would cost a million evaluations of the loss. Backpropagation (B4) gets all of them from one forward and one backward pass, at a cost of a few forward passes. That is why training large networks is possible.

Used in: a model has one partial derivative per weight, which together form the gradient in the next section (A2, B3).

Quick quiz

What does the symbol \(\partial L/\partial w_2\) ask you to do?

Nudge \(w_2\), keep \(w_1\) frozen, and measure how much \(L\) changes per unit nudge.

On the map, where is a slice with \(w_2\) frozen?

It is a horizontal line through the point, because \(w_2\) is the vertical axis and stays fixed along it.

For \(L = 3w_1^2 + w_1w_2\), what is \(\partial L/\partial w_2\)?

Freeze \(w_1\). The term \(3w_1^2\) is a constant and drops out, and \(w_1w_2\) has slope \(w_1\). So \(\partial L/\partial w_2 = w_1\).

The gradient

Guess first. You are on a foggy hillside and can feel only the ground under your feet. The slope towards the east is +2 (uphill) and towards the north is +3 (uphill). In which direction does the ground rise fastest: east, north or somewhere between? And how steep is it there?

gradient \(\nabla L\)
the vector of all partial derivatives; it points uphill
steepest descent
the direction in which the loss falls fastest, which is minus the gradient
learning rate \(\eta\)
how far one downhill step goes (full story in A2)

The gradient is the vector of partial derivatives. It points straight uphill, crosses the contour lines at right angles, and its length is the steepness.

Surface

Drag the 3D view to rotate it. Click the contour map to move the point.

At the point, the coral arrow is the gradient: it crosses the contour line at a right angle and points to higher loss. The teal arrow, minus the gradient, points downhill.

The arrows are long where the surface is steep and short where it is flat. They always cross the contour lines at right angles. Press "Step downhill" to move the point a distance \(\eta\) times the gradient, against it. On the bowl, a press lowers the loss. Try the narrow valley: the steps zig-zag across the valley instead of running along it.

…∂L/∂w1,…∂L/∂w2→…length of the gradient: the steepness,…loss at the point

\[ \nabla L = \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1},\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}\right) \qquad \|\nabla L\| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}\right)^2} \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla L \]

  • \(\nabla L\), read "grad L" or "nabla L", lists one partial derivative per weight. With a million weights it has a million entries.
  • \(\|\nabla L\|\) is its length, the slope in the steepest direction. For a gradient \((2, 3)\) it is \(\sqrt{13} \approx 3.6\), the answer to the guess.
  • \(\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla L\) is the downhill step the button takes. A2 turns it into training.

Why does the gradient point to the steepest direction? The slope in a direction given by a unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) is the dot product \(\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{u}\) (see vectors). A dot product is largest when the two vectors point the same way, and then it equals the length of the gradient.

The foggy hillside in numbers. The gradient is \((2,3)\), with length \(\sqrt{13} = 3.6\). Its direction is the angle whose tangent is \(3/2\), 56 degrees above east. A unit step east rises 2 and a unit step north rises 3. A unit step along \((2,3)/\sqrt{13} = (0.555,\ 0.832)\) rises \(2\times0.555 + 3\times0.832 = 3.6\), more than either.

The gradient is zero at the bottom of the bowl, and the loss is flat there. Choose the saddle or the two dips and find other points where the gradient is zero but you are not at a lowest point. The next sections give the tools to tell them apart.

Common belief. Minus the gradient points at the minimum.

Why it fails. It points downhill at your feet, not at the lowest point. In the narrow valley it points mostly across the valley, and repeated steps zig-zag.

Better account. The gradient is a local compass. Take a small step, then read the compass again.

Debug tip. A gradient has one entry per weight. If its size differs from the size of the weights, or a small step along minus the gradient raises the loss, the gradient code is wrong. The nudge test in the code below catches both.

import numpy as np

def L(w):  # a tilted bowl, lowest at (0.5, -0.5)
    a, b = w[0] - 0.5, w[1] + 0.5
    return 0.5 * a**2 + 1.0 * b**2 + 0.6 * a * b

def grad(w):  # the two partial derivatives, by rule
    a, b = w[0] - 0.5, w[1] + 0.5
    return np.array([a + 0.6 * b, 2 * b + 0.6 * a])

def grad_by_nudging(w, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(w)
    for j in range(len(w)):  # nudge one weight, freeze the others
        step = np.zeros_like(w)
        step[j] = h
        g[j] = (L(w + step) - L(w - step)) / (2 * h)
    return g

w = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
print("rule    :", grad(w))
print("nudging :", np.round(grad_by_nudging(w), 4))
print("loss now:", L(w))
print("after one step of 0.3 downhill:", round(L(w - 0.3 * grad(w)), 4))

for step in range(4):  # four steps of size 0.3, always downhill
    print(step, w.round(3), round(L(w), 4))
    w = w - 0.3 * grad(w)

Follow the printed steps. From \((2,1)\) the gradient is \((2.4,\ 3.9)\), so a step of 0.3 moves the point to \((2-0.72,\ 1-1.17) = (1.28,\ -0.17)\). The loss falls from 4.725 to 0.5675. There the gradient is \((0.978,\ 1.128)\), much smaller, so the next step is shorter and ends at \((0.987,\ -0.508)\). The steps shrink by themselves because the gradient shrinks near the bottom.

In practice. Nobody prints a gradient with a million entries. People watch its length, called the gradient norm (B8), and the loss. A loss measured on a small batch gives a noisy gradient (B3). A single step may then fail to lower the loss even when the code is right.

Used in: one gradient entry per weight is what training computes (A2, B3), and the loss surface idea returns in B3.

The nudge uses both sides of the point (\(L(w+h) - L(w-h)\), divided by \(2h\)). That is more accurate than nudging one side. B4 uses this same check to test a backpropagation implementation.

Quick quiz

The gradient at a point is (3, 4). How steep is the steepest direction, and which way is downhill?

The steepness is \(\sqrt{9+16} = 5\). Downhill is the opposite direction, \((-3, -4)\).

A contour line runs north-east at the point. Which way does the gradient point?

At right angles to it: north-west or south-east, whichever side has higher loss.

The loss is \(w_1^2 + w_2^2\). What is the gradient at \((3,4)\), and where does a step with \(\eta = 0.1\) take the point?

The partial derivatives are \(2w_1 = 6\) and \(2w_2 = 8\), so the gradient is \((6,8)\), with length 10. The step moves to \((3-0.6,\ 4-0.8) = (2.4,\ 3.2)\).

The chain rule

Guess first. A SharkCam neuron turns a length \(x\) into a score \(z = 0.1\,(x - 70)\), and then into a probability of "adult" \(a = \sigma(z)\). You nudge \(x\) by 1 cm. The score moves by 0.1. Near this point the sigmoid has a slope of about 0.21. By roughly how much does \(a\) move?

composition
feeding the output of one function into the next (see 0.3)
local slope
the slope of one link in the chain, at the value that link receives now
chain rule
the slope of the whole chain is the product of the local slopes

When functions are chained, a nudge is multiplied by the local slope of the links it passes through, one after another. The slope of the whole chain is the product of the local slopes.

Chain

A 1 cm nudge in \(x\) becomes 0.1 in \(z\) and about 0.021 in \(a\), so the slope of the chain is \(0.1 \times 0.214 = 0.0214\). The answer to the guess is about 0.02.

Press "Nudge" to watch the change travel down the chain. The top slider moves by \(\Delta x\). The link below it multiplies the change by its local slope, so the second slider moves by \(0.1\,\Delta x\), and the link below that multiplies again. Use the two controls to move the starting point and to enlarge the nudge. A large nudge breaks the match between the product of slopes and the actual change, because the slopes change along the way. Add the loss link to see a longer chain.

\[ \frac{da}{dx} = \frac{da}{dz}\cdot\frac{dz}{dx} \qquad\qquad \frac{d\ell}{dw} = \frac{d\ell}{da}\cdot\frac{da}{dz}\cdot\frac{dz}{dw} \]

  • \(x \to z \to a\) is a chain: \(z = wx + b\) is a weighted sum and \(a = \sigma(z)\) is the neuron's output.
  • \(\ell\) is the loss for one example, here \((a - y)^2\) for a label \(y\).
  • In training you nudge the weight \(w\), not the input. The chain is the same, with \(dz/dw = x\) as the first link.

The rule follows from using the nudge idea twice. A nudge \(\Delta x\) moves \(z\) by about \((dz/dx)\,\Delta x\). That change in \(z\) moves \(a\) by about \((da/dz)\) times as much. So \(\Delta a \approx (da/dz)(dz/dx)\,\Delta x\).

The same 1 cm matters differently at other lengths. At \(x=60\) cm, \(z=-1\), the sigmoid slope is 0.197 and the chain slope is \(0.1\times0.197 = 0.0197\). At 78 cm it is 0.0214. At 90 cm, \(z=2\), it is \(0.1\times0.105 = 0.0105\). At 120 cm, \(z=5\), it is 0.00066. A 120 cm shark moves the output more than 30 times less per centimetre than a 78 cm one, because the sigmoid is saturated there. For a larger nudge the forecast drifts. From 78 cm, 10 cm of growth is forecast to raise \(a\) by \(10\times0.0214 = 0.214\). The true rise is \(0.858 - 0.690 = 0.168\), because the slope falls as the curve flattens.

B4 builds this into backpropagation: it walks the chain from the loss back to the weights and multiplies the local slopes on the way, once for all the weights.

Analogy. A train of gears. A 10-to-1 gear driving a 4-to-1 gear turns the last shaft 40 times slower than the first: the ratios multiply.

Where it breaks. A gear ratio is fixed. A local slope depends on where the signal is now, so you must measure it at the current point, and the product only predicts small nudges.

Debug tip. A product with one near-zero factor is near zero. If the gradient at a weight is tiny, list the local slopes along its chain. A saturated sigmoid (slope 0.001) or a ReLU receiving a negative score (slope 0) is often the culprit (B8).

import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

x, y = 78.0, 1.0  # a 78 cm shark, label 1 = adult
def loss(w, b=-7.0):
    z = w * x + b  # score
    a = sigmoid(z)  # probability of adult
    return (a - y) ** 2

w = 0.1
z = w * x - 7.0
a = sigmoid(z)
dl_da = 2 * (a - y)  # slope of the loss with respect to a
da_dz = a * (1 - a)  # slope of the sigmoid
dz_dw = x  # z = w*x + b, so nudging w moves z by x
chain = dl_da * da_dz * dz_dw
nudge = (loss(w + 1e-6) - loss(w)) / 1e-6

print("local slopes:", round(dl_da, 4), round(da_dz, 4), dz_dw)
print("chain rule  :", round(chain, 4))
print("by nudging  :", round(nudge, 4))
local slopes: -0.6201 0.2139 78.0
chain rule  : -10.3455
by nudging  : -10.3451
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

def a_of(x):
    return sigmoid(0.1 * (x - 70))      # length x -> score z -> probability a

for x in [60, 78, 90, 120]:
    a = a_of(x)
    chain = 0.1 * a * (1 - a)           # dz/dx times da/dz
    true = a_of(x + 1) - a              # the true change for a 1 cm nudge
    print(f"x = {x}: chain slope {chain:.5f}, true change {true:.5f}")
forecast = 10 * 0.1 * sigmoid(0.8) * (1 - sigmoid(0.8))
true_rise = a_of(88) - a_of(78)

In practice. Libraries keep \(z\) and \(a\) from the forward pass, because the local slopes need them. This is part of why training uses more memory than prediction.

Used in: backpropagation multiplies local slopes along a computation graph (B4).

The last local slope, \(dz/dw = 78\), is large because lengths are in centimetres. A weight attached to a big input moves the score a lot, which makes its gradient big. This is one reason A2 rescales features before training.

Quick quiz

The local slopes along a chain are 3, 0.5 and 0.1. What is the slope of the whole chain?

\(3 \times 0.5 \times 0.1 = 0.15\).

Why can the chain rule fail for a large nudge?

A local slope is measured at the starting point. A large nudge moves the signal to where the slopes are different, so the product no longer matches the true change.

One link of a chain has slope 0, such as a ReLU that received a negative score. What is the slope of the whole chain?

It is 0, whatever the other links are. A product with a zero factor is zero, so no nudge of the input reaches the output.

Curvature: the slope of the slope

Guess first. Two valleys have their lowest point in the same place. One is a narrow ravine and the other a wide dish. You move a weight 0.5 away from the bottom in both. In which does the loss rise more, and by how many times more?

second derivative
the slope of the slope, written \(f''(x)\)
curvature
how fast the slope changes: large for a sharp bend, small for a gentle one
sharp and flat valleys
a valley with large curvature, and one with small curvature
Hessian
the table of all second partial derivatives of a loss with many weights

The second derivative says how quickly the slope changes. A sharp valley has a large one, a flat valley a small one, and the same nudge costs the sharp valley far more loss.

At 0.5 from the bottom, the sharp valley (curvature 6) gains 12 times the loss of the flat one (curvature 0.5). With the same step size, the flat valley is slow and the sharp one bounces.

Slide the distance \(\delta\) from the bottom. Near the bottom of a smooth valley the loss rises like a parabola: half the curvature times \(\delta^2\). That is why the sharp valley is 12 times more sensitive at the same distance. Then press "Take 8 steps" to start from \(\delta\) and walk downhill with step size \(\eta\) in both valleys. A step shrinks the distance to the bottom by the factor \(1 - \eta k\), where \(k\) is the curvature. When \(\eta k\) is between 1 and 2 the walker overshoots the bottom and bounces across it, still getting closer. Above 2 it bounces further out at the next step.

\[ f''(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2} \qquad f(w^\ast + \delta) \approx f(w^\ast) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,f''(w^\ast)\,\delta^2 \qquad \eta < \frac{2}{k} \]

  • \(f''(x)\) is the second derivative: nudge the slope the way you nudged the function.
  • \(w^\ast\) is the lowest point and \(\delta\) the distance from it. The middle formula says the loss rises like \(\tfrac12 k\delta^2\) with \(k = f''\).
  • \(\eta < 2/k\) is the largest step size that keeps a gradient step from growing the distance, for a perfect parabola of curvature \(k\).

Two steps behind these formulas. Near a smooth bottom the loss is about \(f(w^\ast) + f'(w^\ast)\delta + \tfrac12 f''(w^\ast)\delta^2\). The slope \(f'\) is 0 at the bottom, so the middle term vanishes and \(\tfrac12 k\delta^2\) is the first term left. For that parabola the slope is \(k\delta\), so one step of size \(\eta\) gives a new distance \(\delta - \eta k\delta = (1-\eta k)\delta\). The distance shrinks when \(\lvert 1-\eta k\rvert < 1\), which means \(0 < \eta k < 2\).

With many weights, curvature is a table of second partial derivatives, the Hessian. Its eigenvectors (0.5) are the directions in which the valley is sharpest and flattest, and its eigenvalues are the curvatures along them. A saddle has positive curvature in one direction and negative in another: the gradient is zero there, yet it is no minimum. Positive curvature means a valley, negative means a hilltop.

A two-weight case: the bowl of the last section. Its second derivatives are 1 along \(w_1\), 2 along \(w_2\) and 0.6 for the mixed term, so the Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0.6&2\end{pmatrix}\). The trace is 3 and the determinant is \(2-0.36 = 1.64\), so \(\lambda^2-3\lambda+1.64=0\) and \(\lambda = (3\pm\sqrt{2.44})/2 = 2.281\) or \(0.719\). The sharpest direction has curvature 2.28, so the largest safe step is \(2/2.281 = 0.877\). The code runs 60 steps at 0.8 and at 0.9.

import numpy as np

H = np.array([[1.0, 0.6],
              [0.6, 2.0]])               # second derivatives of the bowl
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(H)
limit = 2 / vals.max()
print("curvatures:", vals.round(3), " step limit 2/max:", round(limit, 3))

def grad(w):                              # gradient of the bowl
    a, b = w[0] - 0.5, w[1] + 0.5
    return np.array([a + 0.6 * b, 2 * b + 0.6 * a])

for eta in [0.8, 0.9]:
    w = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
    for _ in range(60):
        w = w - eta * grad(w)
    dist = np.linalg.norm(w - [0.5, -0.5])
    print(f"eta = {eta}: distance from the bottom after 60 steps = {dist:.5f}")
curvatures: [0.719 2.281]  step limit 2/max: 0.877
eta = 0.8: distance from the bottom after 60 steps = 0.00002
eta = 0.9: distance from the bottom after 60 steps = 44.01931

Along the sharpest direction a step multiplies the distance by \(1-\eta\lambda\). At 0.8 that factor is \(-0.82\), so the distance shrinks. At 0.9 it is \(-1.05\), so the distance grows by about 5% per step and the walker leaves the bowl.

Curvature appears again in the optimiser chapter (B5), where Adam's running average of squared gradients is often read as a rough gauge of curvature. It also returns in B7: a weight whose loss is sharp (a large Fisher information) is one the model cannot afford to change.

Common belief. If the gradient is zero, training has found the lowest point.

Why it fails. A zero gradient also occurs at the top of a hill and at a saddle. Only the second derivative tells them apart: positive in all directions is a valley.

Better account. The gradient says where to go. The curvature says how far you can trust the step.

Debug tip. If the loss swings up and down between two values, or grows without bound, the step size is probably too large for the curvature. Halve the learning rate first and see whether the swing calms down.

# a valley with its bottom at w = 0
def f(w, k):
    return 0.5 * k * w ** 2

def second_derivative(f, w, k, h=1e-3):
    return (f(w + h, k) - 2 * f(w, k) + f(w - h, k)) / h ** 2

for name, k in [("sharp", 6.0), ("flat", 0.5)]:
    curv = second_derivative(f, 0.5, k)
    rise = f(0.5, k)
    w = 1.5
    for _ in range(8):  # eight gradient steps, step size 0.25
        w = w - 0.25 * (k * w)
    print(f"{name}: curvature {curv:.2f}, loss rise at 0.5 = {rise:.3f}, w after 8 steps = {w:.3f}")
sharp: curvature 6.00, loss rise at 0.5 = 0.750, w after 8 steps = 0.006
flat: curvature 0.50, loss rise at 0.5 = 0.062, w after 8 steps = 0.515

In practice. Nobody forms the Hessian of a large network, since a million weights give a table of \(10^{12}\) entries. People watch the loss for swings, or estimate the largest curvature by repeated multiplication (0.5). A step size that suits the sharpest direction crawls along the flat ones. Momentum and Adam (B5) are two remedies.

Used in: step size and curvature in optimisers (B5), and the Fisher information in B7.

Both walkers started at 1.5. The sharp one bounced from side to side (the factor is \(1 - 0.25 \times 6 = -0.5\)) and ended near the bottom. The flat one shrank its distance by only 12.5% per step.

Quick quiz

A valley has curvature 4. How much does the loss rise when you move 0.3 from the bottom?

\(\tfrac12 \times 4 \times 0.3^2 = 0.18\).

For a curvature of 10, what is the largest step size that does not make the distance to the bottom grow?

\(2/10 = 0.2\). Above it, a gradient step overshoots by more than the distance it started from.

At a point the gradient is 0 and the curvature is \(-2\). Is it a valley bottom or a hilltop?

A hilltop. Negative curvature means the slope falls as you move, so the loss drops on both sides of the point.

Why does a flat sigmoid tail make learning slow? Name the two ideas from this chapter that your answer uses.
