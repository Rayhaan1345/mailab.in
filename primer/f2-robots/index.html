AI on a robot

Sense, decide, act, on a small computer with a camera and a battery.

SharkCam is a robot, and a robot has a deadline. It has to see a shark, decide and turn its camera before the shark has moved on. A model that is fine on a laptop may not fit in the memory of a small computer, or may run too slowly for the loop. This chapter follows one frame through camera, model, decision and motor, adds up the times, and works out frames per second and memory from the size of the model.

Then it shrinks the model. You will store the weights of the digit reader from C2 in 8, 4 and 2 bits and measure what the cuts cost on the 10,000 test digits.

You will need: the digit reader and its parameter counts from C2, the KV cache of D5 for one remark about language models, and the rare classes of A4 for the last exercise. The size of GPT-2 comes from D2 and D5.

Sense, decide, act

Guess first. SharkCam's camera makes 30 pictures a second. The model needs 160 ms for one picture. The camera then needs 60 ms to turn. When the camera finishes turning, how old is the picture it turned for: about 30 ms, about 100 ms, or about 250 ms?

sense, decide, act
the loop of a robot: a sensor reads the world, a program decides, a motor changes the world, and the loop starts again
latency
the time from the light entering the lens to the motor moving, which is the age of the information the motor acts on
update period
the time between two commands; one divided by it is the frames per second the robot uses
pipelining
working on several frames at once, one per stage, so that the robot's updates come faster

The model is one stage of a loop. The time it may take comes from how fast the shark moves, and the model does not set it.

0latency L: light in to motor moving
0update period T
0worst age of a command, L + T
0pointing error behind the shark

With ResNet-18 in 8 bits on a Pi 4, the model takes 159 ms and the whole loop 257 ms. A shark that crosses the view at 1 m/s from 3 m away is then followed with an error of 9.8 degrees, against 5 allowed. MobileNetV2 brings it to 4.7. The model times come from the calculator of the next section. The camera interval, the decision and the motor time are assumed values, and the shark's speed and distance are made up for the story.

Press Step to follow one frame. The four boxes are the stages, and the bar below shows them to scale. The camera stage is the frame interval (33 ms at 30 frames per second) plus 3.5 ms to read and resize the picture. The 3.5 ms is the figure the PyTorch tutorial gives for a Pi 4. The model stage comes from the calculator of the next section. The decision is a few lines of code, which we assume takes 1 ms. A small hobby servo is assumed to need 60 ms for a short turn. The sum is the latency L. It is the age of the information in the command when the motor has finished moving.

Now switch on "pipelined". The camera no longer waits for the model, so the robot gets a new command after 159 ms instead of 257 ms. The latency stays at 257 ms. Pipelining raises the rate of updates and leaves the age of an update unchanged. For a shark that keeps moving, both matter: the command is L old when it arrives, and the next one is T later.

What the lag costs

The second figure shows the cost. The camera points where the shark was at the time of the last frame it used. The shark moves on, and the gap between the dashed coral line to the shark and the teal line of the aim is the pointing error. At the middle of the pass, a shark at distance \(d\) moving across the view at speed \(v\) sweeps the angle at \(\omega = v/d\) radians per second. A lag of \(L+T\) puts the camera behind by about \(\omega\,(L+T)\).

The camera points where the shark was one latency ago, so the pointing error grows with the shark's speed and shrinks as the shark swims farther away. The wedge is a 60-degree field of view. The pass starts with the camera on target, and the animation is slowed when the pass would be too quick to watch.

0camera, ms+0model, ms+0decision, ms+0motor, ms=0latency L, ms→0L + T, ms (T = 0)×0degrees per second=0pointing error, degrees

\[ L = t_{\text{cam}} + t_{\text{model}} + t_{\text{dec}} + t_{\text{motor}} \]

\[ \theta_{\text{err}} \approx \omega\,(L + T) \qquad \omega = \frac{v}{d} \qquad L + T \le \frac{\theta_{\text{allowed}}}{\omega} \]

  • \(t_{\text{cam}}\), \(t_{\text{model}}\), \(t_{\text{dec}}\) and \(t_{\text{motor}}\) are the times of the four stages. \(L\) is their sum, the latency. \(T\) is the update period: \(L\) when the robot finishes a frame before it takes the next, and the larger of the camera interval and the model time when it is pipelined.
  • \(v\) is the shark's speed across the view in m/s and \(d\) its distance in m. \(\omega\) is the angle it sweeps per second (in radians; the figure shows degrees). The formula is for the middle of the pass, where the angle changes fastest, and it assumes small turns.
  • The last relation is the budget. The budget line on the bar is the longest \(L+T\) that keeps the error within the allowed angle. For 5 degrees at 19.1 degrees per second it is 262 ms.

A second case with the figure. MobileNetV2 in 8 bits needs 26 ms in the model. The loop takes 124 ms, the update period is 124 ms when frames are taken one at a time, and the worst command age is 248 ms. That is inside the 262 ms budget, with an error of 4.7 degrees. In pipelined mode the period falls to the camera's 33 ms and the error to 3.0 degrees. The smaller model buys the whole difference between failing and keeping up. The decision and the motor cost the same in both cases.

Bridge to Rayhaan's robot. Rayhaan has built a small robot that ran a vision model on a Raspberry Pi. The questions of this chapter are the ones such a build raises. How large a model fits in the memory? How many frames per second does it give? What does the delay from camera to motor do to how the robot behaves?

Common belief. If the model runs at 30 frames per second, the robot reacts in 33 ms.

Why it fails. Frames per second says how often an answer arrives. Latency says how old the answer is. The camera's exposure, the read, the motor and the wait for the next update all add to the age of a command.

Better account. Add up the stages from the light entering the lens to the motor moving. Compare that sum, and the update period, with a budget that comes from how fast the world changes.

Debug tip. Put a timestamp before and after the stages in the robot's code, and print the stage times. The stage you expect to be slow is not always the slow one. Fix the biggest bar first. Repeat the measurement on the robot, not on a laptop.

import numpy as np

stages = {"camera": 36.8, "model": 159.3, "decision": 1.0, "motor": 60.0}   # milliseconds
L = sum(stages.values())
v, d = 1.0, 3.0                                   # shark speed in m/s, distance in m
omega = np.degrees(v / d)                         # degrees per second, at the middle of the pass

for name, T in [("one frame at a time", L), ("pipelined", max(33.3, stages["model"]))]:
    lag = L + T                                   # age of the information plus the wait for the next update
    print(f"{name}: latency {L:.0f} ms, new command after {T:.0f} ms, "
          f"pointing error {omega * lag / 1000:.1f} degrees")
one frame at a time: latency 257 ms, new command after 257 ms, pointing error 9.8 degrees
pipelined: latency 257 ms, new command after 159 ms, pointing error 8.0 degrees

Quick quiz

Pipelining halves the update period of a robot. Does it halve the latency?

No. A frame still passes through all four stages in order, so the time from light in to motor moving is the same. Only the rate of updates changes.

The shark swims twice as fast. By what factor does the pointing error change, and by what factor must the loop's total time shrink to keep the same error?

The error doubles, because \(\omega\) doubles. To keep it the same, \(L+T\) must halve.

Frames per second and memory

Guess first. ResNet-18 needs about 1.8 billion multiply-adds for one photo. A Raspberry Pi 4 does about 11 billion a second when the weights are stored as 8-bit integers. How many photos per second does the Pi manage: about 6, about 60, or about 600?

multiply-add
one multiplication and one addition, the unit of work in a network; a profiler may count the two separately and report twice the number
effective speed
the multiply-adds per second a program reaches, which is well below the peak of a spec sheet
bits per weight
how many binary digits store one weight: 32, 16, 8 or 4
fits
the weights take no more memory than the computer has (the weights are not all of the memory a program uses)

Two sums decide whether a model can live on a robot. Weights times bytes must fit in memory. Multiply-adds per frame divided by speed must fit in the time budget.

0memory for the weights,
0time for one frame
0frames (or tokens) per second,
0billion multiply-adds per second at this precision

On a Pi 4, ResNet-18 in 8 bits needs 11.7 MB of weights and gives about 6 frames per second, MobileNetV2 gives about 38, and the 70-billion-parameter model does not fit in memory at any precision on the page. The speeds are estimates from one published measurement (below). The calculator gives 4-bit weights no speed-up, because these processors have no 4-bit multiply. That is an assumption, and software can still gain from reading fewer bytes.

Choose a model and a number of bits. The dot of the chosen model is ringed. Its position on the left to right axis is the memory of its weights. The dashed coral line is the computer's memory, and any dot to its right does not fit. Its position on the bottom to top axis is the frames per second. The dashed sand line is the rate the robot needs, and any dot below it is too slow. Lower the bits and all the dots move left. With 8 bits instead of 32 it also moves up, by the speed-up factor you set.

0weights, N×0bits ÷ 8 = bytes per weight=0memory M | 0multiply-adds, O÷0billion per second, R=0time t→0per second, 1/t

\[ M = N\cdot\frac{b}{8} \ \text{bytes} \qquad t = \frac{O}{R_b} \qquad f = \frac{1}{t} \]

\[ R_b = R_8 \ \text{ for } b \le 8 \qquad R_b = R_8 / s_8 \ \text{ for } b > 8 \]

  • \(N\) is the number of weights (the parameters of A1) and \(b\) the bits per weight, so \(b/8\) is the bytes per weight. \(M\) is the memory for the weights alone.
  • \(O\) is the multiply-adds for one frame (one token for a language model). \(R_b\) is the effective speed in multiply-adds per second when the weights have \(b\) bits. \(t\) is the time per frame and \(f\) the frames per second.
  • \(R_8\) is the speed for 8-bit weights. \(s_8\) is how many times slower 32-bit arithmetic is. The page assumes 2.5. The LiteRT documentation lists 2 to 3 times for dynamic range quantisation and more than 3 times for full integer quantisation. The actual value depends on the chip.

Where the speeds come from

The speed of a computer in multiply-adds per second is not on its box, and the peak in the spec sheet is not what a program reaches. The page uses one measurement. The PyTorch tutorial for the Raspberry Pi runs MobileNetV2, quantised to 8 bits, on a Pi 4 with 4 GB. It reports 26.4 ms per frame in the model and 29.7 ms in all, about 33.7 frames per second. MobileNetV2 needs 300.8 million multiply-adds (counted for this chapter from the network's definition). Dividing gives 11.4 billion per second. For the Pi 5 the tutorial reports about 41 frames per second. Remove the 3.3 ms that the Pi 4 spends outside the model, and the estimate is 14.3 billion per second.

Treat these as first estimates and measure on your own robot. The software changes the answer as much as the hardware. Ultralytics reports a 2.4-million-parameter detector (YOLO26n) at 640 pixels on a Pi 5 in 32-bit arithmetic: 299 ms with PyTorch, 126 ms with ONNX and 67 ms with NCNN. The same network and the same computer give a factor of 4.5.

The table in the figure shows a second difference. Memory follows the number of weights. Time follows the number of multiply-adds. A convolutional network reuses a weight at all positions of the picture, so the C2 digit reader does 32 multiply-adds per weight, MobileNetV2 86 and ResNet-18 155. A language model uses a weight about once per token. The first group is limited by arithmetic and the second is usually limited by how fast the memory can deliver the weights. The calculator counts arithmetic only. For GPT-2 and the 70-billion-parameter model its frames per second are therefore the best case.

Common belief. A model with fewer parameters is a faster model.

Why it fails. Parameters set the memory. Time depends on the multiply-adds, and the ratio between the two depends on the design. The C2 digit reader has 3,730 weights and does 117,912 multiply-adds. A model with a few weights and a large picture can still be slow.

Better account. Count both numbers. Memory follows the parameters, and time follows the multiply-adds divided by the speed you measure.

Debug tip. If your measured time is far from the calculator's, check three things. Is the program using all the cores of the computer? Did you time a warm run, not the first one? Does the time include copying the picture, which the model does not? Then compare the multiply-adds per second you measured with the speed you assumed.

Quick quiz

A model has 25 million weights stored in 16 bits. How much memory do the weights need?

\(25\times10^6 \times 16/8 = 50\) million bytes, or 50 MB.

A model does 600 million multiply-adds per frame on a computer that reaches 12 billion per second. How many frames per second, and what happens to the time if the speed halves?

\(12\times10^9 / 600\times10^6 = 20\) frames per second, or 50 ms per frame. At half the speed the time doubles to 100 ms and the rate halves to 10 per second.

Fewer bits per weight

Guess first. The C2 digit reader stores 3,702 weights as 32-bit numbers and reads 98.89% of the 10,000 test digits correctly. Round the weights to 256 levels, which is 8 bits. How many of the 10,000 digits change their answer: none, about 6, about 600, or about 6,000?

quantisation
storing numbers with fewer bits by rounding them to the nearest of a fixed set of levels
scale \(s\)
the step between two neighbouring levels
zero point \(z\)
the integer code that means the value 0.0
rounding error
the weight minus its rounded value, never more than half a step

Rounding the weights to 8 bits cuts the memory by four and changes almost no answers. Below 4 bits, the answers fall apart.

0memory for the weights (biases kept in 32 bits)
0accuracy on the 400 digits in your browser
0accuracy on all 10,000 test digits, measured offline
0digits that change their answer

At 8 bits the digit reader keeps 98.87% of the 10,000 test digits, and 6 digits change their answer. At 4 bits it keeps 97.67%, at 3 bits 92.16% and at 2 bits 20.9%. Only the weights are rounded. The layers have their own scales, and the biases stay in 32 bits. The weights are those of C2, trained on MNIST; the 10,000 test digits were run offline, and the 400 digits in your browser are the first 400 of them.

The left plot shows a layer's weights. The coral lines, drawn when there are 32 levels or fewer, are the only values a quantised weight can take. At 8 bits there are 256 levels, so close together that they cannot be drawn. The right plot shows the rounding errors, weight minus stored value. They are spread evenly between minus half a step and plus half a step. When you remove a bit the step doubles. The mean absolute error of all the weights is 0.0023 at 8 bits and 0.040 at 4 bits. That is 17 times larger, close to the 16 times that four doublings of the step give.

Why is 8 bits harmless? The weights of the dense layer run from −1.46 to 0.98, and the 8-bit step is 0.0096. The typical weight is 0.13, and the error is at most half a step, 0.005, which is 4% of a typical weight. An output of the network sums 300 weights, and rounding errors that are independent partly cancel when they are summed. The network also has margin: for most digits the right answer wins by far more than a rounding error. Only 6 of 10,000 digits are close enough to flip. At 3 bits the step is 0.35, more than twice the typical weight, and many small weights are rounded to the same level or to zero. At 2 bits there are four levels, and almost all information about the small weights is gone.

The dotted line shows what happens when the activations, the numbers flowing between layers, are rounded to the same number of bits too. The ranges of the activations were taken from 500 training digits, as the LiteRT documentation advises (it asks for 100 to 500 samples). At 8 bits the result is 98.85%, within two digits of the weights-only result. This is one small network. The LiteRT page warns that there could be an accuracy loss, "particularly for smaller networks", and says to check it after conversion.

Compare the two accuracy readings at 8 bits. The 400 digits in your browser give 99.3%, above the 99.0% of the 32-bit network. The 10,000 test digits give 98.87% against 98.89%. Rounding cannot teach a network anything. The gap on 400 digits is one digit, and it is noise. A small test set cannot show a change of a fraction of a point.

0weight w÷0step s+0zero point z≈0stored code q→0s × (q − z)→0error, at most 0 here

\[ s = \frac{w_{\max} - w_{\min}}{2^{b} - 1} \qquad z = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{-w_{\min}}{s}\right) \]

\[ q = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{w}{s}\right) + z,\ 0,\ 2^{b}-1\right) \qquad \hat{w} = s\,(q - z) \qquad |w - \hat{w}| \le \frac{s}{2} \]

  • \(w\) is a weight and \(w_{\min}\), \(w_{\max}\) the smallest and largest weight of its layer. \(b\) is the bits per weight, so there are \(2^b\) codes, from 0 to \(2^b-1\).
  • \(s\) is the scale (the step between levels) and \(z\) the zero point. \(q\) is the integer that is stored. \(\hat w\) is the value the network uses when it computes; the program rebuilds it from \(q\).
  • The rounding step of the formula is why the error is at most \(s/2\), for any weight inside the range. The relation \(\hat w = s(q - z)\) is the one in the LiteRT quantisation specification and in Jacob and colleagues (2018). Hardware often fixes \(z = 0\) for weights (symmetric quantisation) and uses a scale per output channel. This page uses one scale and one zero point per layer, which is the simplest case.

For the dense layer at 8 bits the example reads: \(s = 0.0096\), \(z = 153\), and the first weight, \(-0.4328\), is stored as 108 and used as \(-0.4307\). The error is 0.0021, under half a step (0.0048). A dense weight needs one byte instead of four. A running network multiplies the integers and applies the scale once at the end of a sum. That is what makes 8-bit arithmetic fast on a chip with integer instructions.

Common belief. A bit removed always costs the same share of accuracy, so half the bits is half the quality.

Why it fails. The rounding error halves with an added bit. The accuracy does not follow it. Down to 5 bits the accuracy stays within 0.1 points of the 32-bit value. It then falls to 97.67% at 4 bits and 92.16% at 3 bits, and collapses at 2.

Better account. Measure the accuracy at several bit widths on data like the data the robot will see. Pick the smallest width where no important answer changes, and keep a margin.

Debug tip. After quantising, run the 32-bit and the quantised model on the same inputs and count the answers that differ, as the "digits that change their answer" box does. Accuracy can stay the same while some digits swap in and out. Then look at the classes that matter most. A model can keep its average and lose the rare class (A4) or the murky-water frames (C3). If one outlier weight sets a layer's range, the step is large for all the others; a scale per output channel avoids this.

import numpy as np

def quantise(w, bits):
    s = (w.max() - w.min()) / (2 ** bits - 1)     # scale: the step between levels
    z = int(round(-w.min() / s))                  # zero point: the code that means 0.0
    q = np.clip(np.round(w / s) + z, 0, 2 ** bits - 1).astype(int)
    return q, s, z

def dequantise(q, s, z):
    return s * (q - z)

w = np.array([-0.43, 0.12, 0.88, -1.46, 0.00])    # five made-up weights
for bits in (8, 4):
    q, s, z = quantise(w, bits)
    err = np.abs(w - dequantise(q, s, z))
    print(f"{bits} bits: step {s:.4f}, zero point {z}, codes {q.tolist()}, largest error {err.max():.4f}")
8 bits: step 0.0092, zero point 159, codes [112, 172, 255, 0, 159], largest error 0.0013
4 bits: step 0.1560, zero point 9, codes [6, 10, 15, 0, 9], largest error 0.0560

Quick quiz

A layer has weights from −2 to 6. What is the step at 8 bits, and the largest rounding error?

\(s = 8/255 = 0.0314\). The largest error is \(s/2 = 0.0157\).

Why can a network lose almost no accuracy at 8 bits and lose most of it at 2?

At 8 bits the error is a small share of a typical weight, and the errors of many weights partly cancel in a sum. At 2 bits there are four levels, the step is larger than most weights, and small weights lose their values.

The ladder of model sizes

Guess first. A language model with 70 billion weights stores a weight in 2 bytes (16 bits). How much memory do the weights need: about 14 GB, about 140 GB, or about 1,400 GB?

model-size ladder
the models of this primer in order of weights: the digit reader, MobileNetV2, ResNet-18, GPT-2 and a large language model
smaller by design
a network built to need fewer weights and multiply-adds for the same job, such as MobileNetV2
precision
the bits per weight; the same model at a lower precision is a smaller file

From the digit reader to a 70-billion-parameter model the weights grow by a factor of about 19 million. Going from 32 bits to 8 buys back a factor of four.

Green figures fit in 4 GB, the memory of the Pi 4 above. Weights only: the picture, the activations, the program and (for language models) the KV cache take more.

ResNet-18 needs 46.8 MB at 32 bits and 11.7 MB at 8 bits. GPT-2 small needs 498 MB at 32 bits and 62 MB at 4 bits. The 70-billion-parameter model needs 280 GB at 32 bits and 35 GB even at 4 bits, which is more than the largest Raspberry Pi 5 has. The sizes are the number of weights times the bytes per weight. The 70 billion is the size of the largest Llama 2 model (Touvron and others, 2023).

The bars are the memory for the weights. A model has four bars, one per precision. The dashed lines are the memory of a small computer. The digit reader needs 15 KB. MobileNetV2 and ResNet-18 need tens of MB in 32 bits, which any Pi can store easily. GPT-2 small fits in 4 GB at all four precisions. The large model does not fit anywhere on the page. It is a model for a data centre, or for a robot that sends its pictures to a computer on shore.

The second way to a smaller model is to choose a smaller network. MobileNetV2 has 3.5 million weights and needs 301 million multiply-adds. ResNet-18 has 11.7 million weights and needs 1,814 million: 3.3 times the memory and 6 times the arithmetic. Sandler and colleagues (2018) built MobileNetV2 for mobile devices, where this trade matters. Which model is better at finding sharks depends on the data, and the chapter does not compare them. Test both on murky-water frames before you choose (C3). Other ways to shrink a model exist, such as removing weights or training a small model to copy a large one, and this chapter does not cover them.

The weights are not the whole memory. A running network also needs memory for its activations, the feature maps between layers, and the program that runs it. A language model also keeps a KV cache that grows with the text: for GPT-2 small, 37.7 MB at 1,024 tokens in 16 bits (D5). Leave headroom and measure the memory of the running process.

11,689,512ResNet-18 weights×4, 2, 1, 0.5bytes per weight at 32, 16, 8 and 4 bits=46.8, 23.4, 11.7, 5.8MB

Common belief. A model that fits in memory will run well on the robot.

Why it fails. Fitting is the first test only. The model must also meet the time budget of the loop, leave memory for the rest of the program, and keep its accuracy at the lower precision.

Better account. Run the three checks in order: memory, time per frame, accuracy on the robot's own pictures. Fail fast on the cheapest.

Quick quiz

GPT-2 small has 124,439,808 weights. How much memory in 16 bits, and does it fit in 1 GB?

\(124{,}439{,}808\times 2 = 248.9\) MB, about a quarter of 1 GB. The weights fit. The KV cache and the program need space too.

Why is a 4-bit model smaller, and why is it not necessarily faster on a Pi?

The memory per weight falls to a half of the 8-bit size. A CPU without a 4-bit multiply must unpack the weights to 8 bits before the arithmetic, so the number of multiply-adds and their speed stay the same.

Try after two days. In C2 the digit reader had 3,730 numbers and a plain network on the same task had 101,770. Which of the two sums of this chapter does that change, memory or time, and which one depends on weight sharing?

Both change, in different ways. Memory follows the number of weights, so the plain network needs 27 times more. The time follows the multiply-adds. In a CNN a shared weight is used at all positions, so the multiply-adds per weight are large (32 for the digit reader). See C2.

Try after a week. A4 says a model that never flags a Ganges shark is 99.5% accurate. Why does that matter when you quantise the SharkCam model?

Rounding the weights can change a few answers. If they are the rare ones, overall accuracy hardly moves and the model stops finding the rare shark. So check recall for the rare class before and after, as well as accuracy (A4).

Try after a week. D5 gave the KV cache size as 2 × layers × tokens × width × bytes. Which bit width would you use to shrink it on a robot, and what would you check?

The cache is numbers too, so fewer bits per number shrink it in the same way, with the same trade: less memory, more rounding. Check the model's answers on long inputs with the smaller cache against the full one (D5).
