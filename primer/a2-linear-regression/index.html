Linear regression

Predict a shark's weight from its length by dragging a line, by a formula and by gradient descent.

Linear regression predicts a number from a weighted sum of the inputs. This chapter fits the simplest case, a shark's weight from its length, in three ways: by dragging a line and watching squared errors, by a formula, and by gradient descent. It then covers the learning rate and why inputs need scaling. After that you see how residuals show what a model misses, how a log-log plot straightens the length-weight curve, and how a second input turns the line into a plane.

All the data is made up: 268 spadenose shark records generated from a published length-weight relationship, not field data. The figure that starts the chapter follows the house-price figure in Jay Alammar's guide to neural networks.

You will need: A1 (model, parameter, lazy model), slopes and the gradient, the dot product, standardising and logarithms.

A line with two dials

Guess first. Twelve made-up spadenose sharks are drawn below. You can tilt a line through them and move it up and down. Two people drag the line to two different places, and both say theirs is best. What single number could settle the argument?

linear regression
predicting a number with a weighted sum of the inputs; with one input, a straight line
slope \(w\)
kg gained per extra cm. Machine learning calls it a weight. It is a different quantity from the shark's weight in kg, so this chapter says "slope" when the two could be confused
bias \(b\)
the height of the line at length 0, in kg
residual \(r\)
truth minus prediction, \(y - \hat y\): how far the line is from a shark
MSE
mean squared error: the average of the squared residuals

A line with slope \(w\) and bias \(b\) predicts weight from length. The mean squared error scores any line with one number, and the best line has the smallest one.

Why squares and not plain gaps? A square never goes negative, so misses above and below the line cannot cancel. A big miss costs more than several small ones: doubling a residual quadruples its square. And the total changes smoothly as the dials move, which lets us use slopes in the next sections. The average of plain gaps (the average miss of A1) is a fine score too, but its slope jumps at zero.

\[ \hat y = w\,x + b \qquad r_i = y_i - \hat y_i \qquad L = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i^{2} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(y_i - w x_i - b\bigr)^{2} \]

  • \(x\) is the input (length, cm) and \(\hat y\) the predicted weight. \(w\) and \(b\) are the two dials, the parameters \(\theta = (w, b)\).
  • \(r_i\) is the residual of shark \(i\), and \(m\) is the number of sharks. \(L\) is the MSE, the loss of A1 with a formula.
  • The unit of \(L\) is kg squared. Its square root, \(\sqrt{L}\), is a typical miss in kg.

Common belief. The best line passes through as many dots as possible.

Why it fails. A line through two dots can leave large squares at the others. The best line often passes through no dot at all.

Better account. The best line has the smallest average square. It does pass through one point, the average shark, as the next section shows.

Debug tip. Squared units are hard to read. An MSE of 0.0022 kg2 means a typical miss of \(\sqrt{0.0022} = 0.047\) kg, about 47 g. Quote the square root when you tell a person how far off a model is.

import numpy as np

# 12 made-up spadenose sharks: length (cm) and weight (kg)
x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])

def mse(w, b):
    r = y - (w * x + b)             # one residual per shark
    return (r ** 2).mean()          # the average of the squares

w, b = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)          # the best line (the next section shows how)
print("always the average:", round(mse(0, y.mean()), 5))
print("a line by eye     :", round(mse(0.015, -0.38), 5))
print("the best line     :", round(mse(w, b), 5), "with w =", round(w, 4), "and b =", round(b, 4))

the best line     : 0.00219 with w = 0.0149 and b = -0.3562

Quick quiz

A shark is 0.1 kg heavier than the line predicts. What is its residual, and what is the area of its square?

The residual is +0.1 kg (truth minus prediction). The square has area \(0.1^2 = 0.01\) kg2.

Line A misses two sharks, both by 0.1 kg. Line B misses one shark by 0.2 kg and the other by 0. Which has the larger MSE?

Line B. Its squares add to \(0.2^2 = 0.04\), against \(0.1^2 + 0.1^2 = 0.02\) for line A. Squaring punishes one large miss more than two small ones.

The best line, by hand

Guess first. The best line passes through one special point of the data. Is it the first shark, the longest shark, or the point at the average length and the average weight? And if you could compute only averages and sums, how would you get the slope?

deviation
a value minus the mean of its column (0.7)
least squares
choosing \(w\) and \(b\) to make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible
closed form
a formula that gives the answer in a few steps, with no searching

For a line, the best slope is the sum of products of deviations divided by the sum of squared length deviations. The best line passes through the point (average length, average weight).

usetagx, cmy, kgx − meany − meanproductsquare

Six sharks give w = 0.01535 and b = −0.3729, close to the 268-shark values 0.01545 and −0.3789. Switch off the longest shark (Tag 9585) and the slope falls to 0.01114.

Tick and untick sharks. The table redoes the sums, the coral dashed lines mark the average shark, and the teal line is the best line through the ticked sharks. Notice how much one long, heavy shark can tilt the line. A point far from the average pulls on the slope more than a point near the average, because its deviation is large.

\[ w = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^{2}} \qquad b = \bar{y} - w\,\bar{x} \]

  • \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) (read "x bar" and "y bar") are the average length and the average weight.
  • The top of the first fraction adds, over the sharks, the length deviation times the weight deviation. It is large and positive when long sharks are heavy. The bottom adds the squared length deviations.
  • The second formula says \(\bar{y} = w\bar{x} + b\): the line passes through the average shark.

These two formulas come from asking where the loss stops falling, that is where its slopes are zero. The next section finds those slopes.

Common belief. If a formula gives the best line, gradient descent is pointless.

Why it fails. The formula exists for this model. For a logistic model (A3) or a network (B3) no such formula exists. Even for a line with many inputs, the formula needs a matrix inverse, whose cost grows fast with the number of inputs (0.2).

Better account. The formula is the answer key. Gradient descent is the method that transfers to other models, and it must reproduce this answer here.

Debug tip. A fitted line with a bias must give residuals that average to zero. If yours do not, the bias is wrong or a mean was left out of the calculation. It is a free check on any least-squares code.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])

xbar, ybar = x.mean(), y.mean()
w = ((x - xbar) * (y - ybar)).sum() / ((x - xbar) ** 2).sum()
b = ybar - w * xbar
print("by formula :", round(w, 5), round(b, 4))
print("np.polyfit :", np.polyfit(x, y, 1).round(5))
print("mean residual:", abs(round((y - (w * x + b)).mean(), 10)))

Quick quiz

The average length is 40 cm and the average weight 0.25 kg. The slope is 0.015 kg per cm. What is the bias?

\(b = \bar{y} - w\bar{x} = 0.25 - 0.015 \times 40 = -0.35\) kg.

Why does a shark far from the average length tilt the line more than a shark near it?

Its length deviation is large, so it contributes a large term to both sums. A square grows fast, so a far point also contributes more to the denominator, and the line has to move toward it to keep that square small.

Gradient descent

Guess first. At the dial setting \(w = 0\), \(b = 0.55\), the loss changes by −0.27 per unit of \(w\) and by +0.65 per unit of \(b\). To lower the loss, should \(w\) go up or down, and should \(b\)? Which dial will move more if both take the same step size?

gradient descent
repeat: find the slope of the loss along the dials, then move the dials a little against their slopes
step
one such update of all the dials
learning rate \(\eta\)
the step size: how far to move per unit of slope (0.6 previewed it)
converged
the loss no longer falls by a useful amount

Gradient descent starts from any dial setting and repeats one move: compute the slope of the loss along the dials, then change the dials a little against their slopes.

In this figure the length is measured in spreads from the average, \(x = (L - 39.1)/8.6\), a z-score (0.7). So \(w\) is the kg gained per 8.6 cm. Section 5 shows why. The loss uses all 268 spadenose records.

From the start, with a step size of 0.1, ten steps cut the loss from 0.125 to 0.0036, and twenty steps reach within 1% of the best possible loss, 0.00218. The path runs straight down the bowl.

Press "Step" and watch three views of the same event. In the 3D bowl, the coral point moves downhill. In the data panel, the teal line turns from the dashed starting line toward the dotted best line. In the loss curve, the dot drops. The boxes show the arithmetic: the new dial value is the old value minus the step size times the slope of the loss along that dial. Where do the slopes come from? The derivation below finds both of them, one line at a time, with Tag 3305 as the worked example.

Step 1. The loss. Write \(r_i\) for the residual of shark \(i\), so the loss is the average of the squares.

\[ r_i = y_i - (w x_i + b) \qquad L = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i^{2} \]

Worked at \(w = 0\), \(b = 0.55\). Tag 3305 is 41.0 cm long, so \(x = \) 0 spreads. Its residual is \(0.228 - 0.55 = \) 0 kg.

Step 2. The slope of one square. The rule for a power (0.6) gives the slope of \(r^2\) with respect to \(r\).

\[ \frac{d\,(r^{2})}{d\,r} = 2r \]

For Tag 3305: \(2r = \) 0.

Step 3. How the residual moves with a dial. Freeze one dial and nudge the other (partial derivatives, 0.6). Since \(r_i = y_i - w x_i - b\):

\[ \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial w} = -x_i \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial b} = -1 \]

Raising \(w\) by one unit lowers the residual by \(x_i\), so a longer shark responds more to \(w\). Raising \(b\) lowers all the residuals by the same amount.

Step 4. Chain the links. The loss of one shark is a chain: dial, then residual, then square. Multiply the local slopes (0.6).

\[ \frac{\partial\,(r_i^{2})}{\partial w} = 2r_i \cdot (-x_i) = -2\,r_i\,x_i \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial\,(r_i^{2})}{\partial b} = 2r_i \cdot (-1) = -2\,r_i \]

For Tag 3305: \(-2 r x = \) 0 and \(-2r = \) 0.

Step 5. Average over the sharks. The slope of an average is the average of the slopes.

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = -\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i\,x_i \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = -\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i \]

Over all 268 sharks at the start: \(\partial L/\partial w = \) 0 and \(\partial L/\partial b = \) 0. These are the two numbers from the guess.

Step 6. Check by nudging. The nudge test of 0.6 estimates a slope with no formula: change a dial by \(\pm h\) and divide the change in loss by \(2h\).

With \(h = 0.00001\): along \(w\) the estimate is 0, and along \(b\) it is 0. They match the formula.

\[ w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = w + \eta\,\frac{2}{m}\sum_i r_i x_i \qquad\quad b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = b + \eta\,\frac{2}{m}\sum_i r_i \]

  • \(\partial L/\partial w\) is the slope of the loss along \(w\) with \(b\) frozen. Together with \(\partial L/\partial b\) it forms the gradient \(\nabla L\).
  • \(\eta\) (eta) is the learning rate. The arrow \(\leftarrow\) means "replace the old value by the right-hand side".
  • The last form shows what an update does. If the residuals are mostly positive (the line is too low), \(b\) rises. If long sharks have positive residuals, \(w\) rises.

Setting \(\partial L/\partial b = 0\) gives \(\sum_i r_i = 0\): the residuals average to zero. That is the line through the average shark from the last section.

Analogy. Gradient descent is a ball rolling down a bowl, or a walker feeling the slope in fog.

Where it breaks. A ball has momentum, and a walker can look around. Gradient descent sees only the slope under its feet. The bowl of a line has one bottom, so it cannot get stuck. The loss of a neural network can have many dips and flat patches (0.6, B3), and there the walk can end somewhere other than the lowest point.

Debug tip. Print the loss as you train. It should fall from one step to the next. If it rises, the learning rate is too big or a slope has the wrong sign. Compare the formula's slopes with the nudge test of step 6: they disagree when the formula has a bug.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])
z = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()        # length in spreads from the average

w, b, eta = 0.0, 0.55, 0.1          # start, and the learning rate
for step in range(101):
    r = y - (w * z + b)             # one residual per shark
    loss = (r ** 2).mean()
    if step in (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 100):
        print(f"step {step:3d}: w = {w:.4f}, b = {b:.4f}, loss = {loss:.5f}")
    grad_w = -2 * (r * z).mean()    # the two partial derivatives
    grad_b = -2 * r.mean()
    w -= eta * grad_w               # move each dial against its slope
    b -= eta * grad_b

print("closed form :", np.polyfit(z, y, 1).round(4))

Quick quiz

The slope of the loss along \(w\) is +2 and \(\eta = 0.1\). How does \(w\) change in one step?

\(w\) falls by \(0.1 \times 2 = 0.2\). A positive slope means the loss rises as \(w\) rises, so the step goes the other way.

Why does the update subtract \(\eta\,\partial L/\partial w\) and not add it?

The slope points uphill. The aim is a lower loss, so the dials move against their slopes.

The learning rate

Guess first. A larger learning rate takes bigger steps. Is it always faster? Predict what happens to the loss for \(\eta = 0.02\), \(0.3\), \(0.9\) and \(1.05\) on the bowl above, then test them with the buttons.

overshoot
a step so long that it crosses the bottom and ends higher on the far side
diverge
the loss grows from step to step instead of falling
hyperparameter
a setting you choose before training, such as \(\eta\), as opposed to a parameter that training sets

A learning rate that is too small wastes steps, a moderate one converges fast, and one that is too large overshoots so far that the loss grows.

Loss now: 0Steps to reach within 1% of the best loss: 0

At \(\eta = 0.02\) the loss is still three times the best after 40 steps. At 0.3 it is within 1% after 5 steps. At 0.9 it bounces across the bowl and needs 20. Above 1 it diverges.

Slide the learning rate, or press a preset, and then press "Run" to replay the path. At 0.5 the first step reaches the bottom, which happens only because this bowl is perfectly round. Past 0.5 the steps overshoot. At 0.9 a step crosses the bottom and ends 80% of the way back up the far side, so the path zigzags while it settles. At 1 it bounces between two points forever. Above 1 an overshoot is larger than the distance before it, and the loss grows.

The numbers follow from the form of this bowl. The loss here is a sum of two squared distances from the bottom \((w^{*}, b^{*})\), so a slope is twice the matching distance. One step then multiplies both distances by \(1 - 2\eta\).

\[ L(w, b) = L_{\min} + (w - w^{*})^{2} + (b - b^{*})^{2} \qquad w_{t+1} - w^{*} = (1 - 2\eta)\,(w_t - w^{*}) \]

  • \(w^{*}\), \(b^{*}\) are the best dial values, and \(L_{\min}\) is the loss there (0.00218).
  • \(1 - 2\eta\) is the factor per step. It must lie between −1 and 1 for the distance to shrink, so \(\eta < 1\). A negative factor means the step overshoots.
  • The 2 is the curvature of this bowl (0.6). The general limit is \(\eta < 2/k\) for curvature \(k\).

Common belief. A bigger learning rate always trains faster.

Why it fails. Speed rises with \(\eta\) up to the bounce point and then collapses into divergence. In a bowl that is steep in one direction, the steep direction sets the limit.

Better account. There is a usable range. Too small wastes time. Too large fails. The best value lies well inside the range and depends on the bowl.

Debug tip. If the loss rises or turns into nan, cut the learning rate by a factor of 10 and run again. If the loss falls smoothly but slowly, raise it by a factor of 3. Try values spaced by factors of 3 or 10, never by 10%. B8 adds a learning-rate range test.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])
z = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()

for eta in [0.02, 0.3, 0.9, 1.05]:
    w, b = 0.0, 0.55
    for step in range(20):
        r = y - (w * z + b)
        w += eta * 2 * (r * z).mean()
        b += eta * 2 * r.mean()
    loss = ((y - (w * z + b)) ** 2).mean()
    print(f"eta = {eta:4.2f}: loss after 20 steps = {loss:.5f}")

eta = 0.30: loss after 20 steps = 0.00219

Feature scaling

Guess first. Lengths run from 18 to 60, while the bias is multiplied by 1. A change of 0.01 in the slope moves the line by 0.4 kg at length 40. A change of 0.01 in the bias moves it by 0.01 kg. Will one learning rate suit both dials?

feature scaling
rewriting an input in other units so its values are of order 1
centring
subtracting the mean, so the new input averages 0
standardising
centring and then dividing by the spread (0.7)
curvature \(\lambda\)
how fast the slope of the loss changes along a direction (0.6)

Raw lengths make the loss bowl a long, narrow canyon. The steep direction limits the learning rate, and the flat direction then crawls. Standardised lengths make the bowl round, and one learning rate suits both dials.

At its best safe learning rate, gradient descent needs 130,658 steps to get within 1% of the best loss with raw lengths. Centred lengths need 313 steps and standardised lengths need 1.

The map shows the loss on a log scale, so the canyon is visible. Start on "Raw length", press "Best safe η for this scaling" and choose "1,000 steps". The path hardly moves along the canyon floor. Choose "100,000 steps" to watch it creep towards the bottom. Now press "Try η = 0.3", which suits the standardised bowl: on the raw bowl the loss explodes at once, because the steep direction cannot take a step that large. Switch to "Standardised length" and the same η converges in a handful of steps.

The boxes give the reason. The curvatures along the two directions of the bowl are \(\lambda_{\max}\) and \(\lambda_{\min}\). The learning rate must stay below \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) so the steep direction does not diverge. Progress along the flat direction then shrinks the distance by \(1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}\) per step. A big ratio \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) means a long wait. Standardising makes both curvatures 2, the ratio 1, and the bowl round.

\[ x_{\text{new}} = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s} \qquad \eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \qquad \text{progress per step along the flat direction: } 1 - \eta\,\lambda_{\min} \]

  • \(\bar{x}\) is the mean and \(s\) the spread (standard deviation) of the input column, computed on the training data.
  • \(\lambda_{\max}\), \(\lambda_{\min}\) are the largest and smallest curvatures of the bowl. For the line they are the eigenvalues of a 2 by 2 matrix built from the lengths (0.5).

Common belief. Scaling the inputs changes what the model learns.

Why it fails. The best line is the same line. In spreads it has \(w = 0.1331\) kg per spread and \(b = 0.2258\) kg. Divide the slope by 8.614 cm per spread to get 0.01545 kg per cm. The bias is \(0.2258 - 0.01545 \times 39.14 = -0.3789\) kg. These are the values from the unscaled fit.

Better account. Scaling changes only the units of the dials. It changes how fast gradient descent finds the answer, and it does not change the answer.

Debug tip. Work out the mean and spread on the training data only, and reuse the same two numbers on new data. Standardising new data with its own mean and spread would shift all the predictions (A4). Also check that the inputs have similar sizes before you tune \(\eta\). A learning rate that diverges on one data set and works on another often means that the inputs differ in scale.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])

def run(inputs, steps=1000):
    A = np.c_[inputs, np.ones_like(inputs)]               # columns: the input and a 1 for the bias
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(2 * A.T @ A / len(y))        # curvatures of the loss
    eta = 1 / lam.max()                                   # a safe learning rate
    p = np.array([0.0, 0.55])
    for _ in range(steps):
        p += eta * 2 * A.T @ (y - A @ p) / len(y)
    print(f"curvatures {lam.round(3)}, eta {eta:.5f}, loss after {steps} steps {((y - A @ p) ** 2).mean():.5f}")

run(x)                                   # raw length in cm
run((x - x.mean()) / x.std())            # standardised length
curvatures [9.800000e-02 3.196137e+03], eta 0.00031, loss after 1000 steps 0.03993
curvatures [2. 2.], eta 0.50000, loss after 1000 steps 0.00219

Used in: standardising inputs is the first step of nearly all models (A3, B1). Normalisation layers do the same job inside a network (B6).

A residual plot shows what a model misses. Random scatter around zero means the model has caught the pattern. A curve or a fan means something is missing.

Model

The straight line has an \(R^2\) of 0.89 and still leaves a U in its residuals, and it predicts a negative weight for 8 short sharks. The last two models are the power curve of the next section. Try them after you have read it.

Start on "Straight line". The coral line joins the average residuals in five bands of length. For a good model it would lie flat on the grey zero line. Here it bends: short sharks are heavier than the line says, middle sharks are lighter, and long ones are heavier again. The data is curved, and a line cannot follow a curve. The "Always the average" model shows the gap that the line closes. Because \(R^2\) is 1 minus the MSE over the lazy model's MSE, it compares a model with the lazy baseline of A1.

\[ r_i = y_i - \hat y_i \qquad R^{2} = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE of the model}}{\text{MSE of the lazy model}} \]

  • \(r_i\) is the residual, the same quantity that the loss squares.
  • The lazy model says the average weight for all sharks, so its MSE is the variance of the weights. \(R^2 = 0\) means no better than lazy, and \(R^2 = 1\) means no residuals.

Common belief. A high \(R^2\) means the model is right.

Why it fails. This line has \(R^2 = 0.89\), and its residuals still bend and it predicts negative weights.

Better account. \(R^2\) says how much of the spread the model explains. The residual plot says what remains, and a pattern there is a sign of a missing term.

Debug tip. Plot the residuals against the inputs, one at a time, and against the prediction. Look for four things. A curve means a missing bend. A fan means errors that grow with size. Clusters or stripes mean a hidden category. Single points far from zero mean a typo or a rare case. Check that the predictions also make sense: a negative weight cannot be right.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])

w, b = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
r = y - (w * x + b)
print("residuals, shortest to longest:")
print(np.round(r, 3))
print("signs:", "".join("+" if v > 0 else "-" for v in r))
print("R squared:", round(1 - (r ** 2).mean() / y.var(), 3))

Quick quiz

A residual plot shows points scattered evenly above and below zero, but the spread doubles from short sharks to long ones. What does that say?

The model is right on average at all lengths, and its errors grow with the size of the shark. That fan suggests that errors are proportional to size, which is the case for weights. Fitting in logs fixes it (next section).

A model has \(R^2 = 0\). What does it say about the model?

Its MSE equals the lazy model's. It explains none of the spread, and it is no better than always saying the average.

The cube law and the log-log line

Guess first. A shark twice as long weighs about eight times as much (A1). Draw both axes so that equal steps mean doubling. What will the curve \(W = aL^3\) look like on those axes, and what will its slope be?

power law
\(y = a\,x^{w}\): \(y\) grows like a power of \(x\)
exponent \(w\)
the power. On log axes it is the slope of the line
log-log plot
both axes use log scales (0.3)
log-bias \(b\)
\(\ln a\), the height of the line on the log axes at \(\ln L = 0\)

Taking logs of \(W = aL^{w}\) gives \(\ln W = w\ln L + \ln a\), a straight line with slope \(w\). A line-fitting method can fit it, and the fitted exponent for spadenose is 2.92, close to the cube.

On log axes the length-weight curve is a straight line with slope 2.92. In kilograms the power curve misses by 0.036 kg and the straight line by 0.047 kg, and the curve never predicts a negative weight.

Press "Fit the line in logs", then switch between linear and log axes. The same teal line is a curve in one and a line in the other. Now show the straight line from the first sections: on log axes it bends away from the data. The slope you read on log axes is the exponent \(w\). Doubling the length multiplies the weight by \(2^{w}\), which is 7.5 for \(w = 2.92\). The chip "b follows w" makes the line pivot about the average shark, the same point as in section 2. Switch to "Set b yourself" to move the two dials freely.

The fit in logs is the same machinery as before. The inputs are \(\ln L\) and the labels are \(\ln W\). The loss is the MSE of the logs, and gradient descent or the formula of section 2 finds \(w\) and \(b\). A residual of 0.14 in log units means the prediction is off by about 14% of the weight, whatever the size of the shark. In kilograms a straight line would give the same shark a bigger or smaller miss depending on its size.

\[ W = a\,L^{w} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \ln W = w\,\ln L + b \qquad\text{with}\quad b = \ln a \qquad \hat W = e^{\,w\ln L + b} \]

  • \(\ln\) is the natural logarithm, and \(e^{(\cdot)}\) undoes it (0.3).
  • \(w\) is the exponent, and \(a = e^{b}\) is the weight in kg of a 1 cm shark on this curve. The value has no physical meaning outside the data, as 1 cm is far below any length recorded.
  • The fitted line in logs is \(\ln \hat W = 2.915\,\ln L - 12.313\), so \(\hat W = 4.49\times 10^{-6}\,L^{2.915}\) kg. The published law for spadenose has \(b = 2.923\) (0.3).

Common belief. Linear regression can fit only straight lines.

Why it fails. "Linear" describes the dials: the prediction is a weighted sum of the inputs. The inputs and the label may be transformed first. Here the log of both columns turns a curve into a line.

Better account. Transform, then fit a line, then transform back. The model is linear in \(w\) and \(b\) and a power law in the length.

Debug tip. Say which units a miss is in. A typical miss of 0.14 in log units is about 14% of the weight, and 0.036 kg is a miss in weight. They are not comparable until you convert. After fitting in logs, predict with \(e^{(\cdot)}\) of the fitted value, and note that you cannot take the log of a weight of zero.

import numpy as np

# a straight line fitted to the logs: ln W = w * ln L + b
w, b = np.polyfit(np.log(x), np.log(y), 1)
print(f"exponent w = {w:.3f}, a = e^b = {np.exp(b):.2e}")

Quick quiz

On log axes a power law has slope 3. A shark becomes 3 times as long. By what factor does its weight grow?

\(3^3 = 27\). A slope of 3 on log axes means that multiplying the length by any factor multiplies the weight by that factor cubed.

Why is a fit in logs a poor choice if some weights in your data are zero?

The log of zero is undefined, and the log of a tiny positive number is a huge negative one. Such rows would break the fit or dominate it. You would drop them, add a small constant or choose another model.

Several inputs

Guess first. Girth is a second input. Does a model with two inputs need new machinery, or only one more dial and one more partial derivative? And will adding girth lower the typical miss by a lot, or a little?

one slope per input: \(w_1\) for length, \(w_2\) for girth

multiply matching entries and add (0.5)

the two-input version of a line: a flat sheet in 3D

With \(d\) inputs the model is a weighted sum, \(\hat y = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b\), with \(d + 1\) dials. Gradient descent is unchanged: one partial derivative per dial, found the same way.

Giving girth its best weight lowers the typical miss from 0.047 to 0.039 kg. Length and girth move together (correlation 0.93), so the two slopes trade off: as the weight on girth rises, the weight on length falls from 0.0155 to 0.0079.

The navy dots are the 268 sharks at (length, girth, weight). The teal sheet is the model. At \(w_2 = 0\) the sheet ignores girth and tilts only along the length axis, which is the straight line of section 1 extended into 3D. Slide \(w_2\) and the sheet tilts along the girth axis too. For any value of \(w_2\), the slider re-fits \(w_1\) and \(b\) so that the plane is the best one with that weight on girth. The chart on the right shows the typical miss for all values: it dips at the best plane and rises again beyond it.

The boxes under the figure work out the prediction for Tag 3305 as a dot product plus a bias. The partial derivative for a dial has the form you already know: the average of the residual times that dial's input. With \(d\) inputs the same formula gives \(d\) slopes plus one for the bias, and a program computes them all at once with a matrix.

\[ \hat y = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} = -\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i\,x_{ij} \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} L = -\frac{2}{m}\,X^{\top}\mathbf{r} \]

  • \(x_{ij}\) is input \(j\) of shark \(i\). The matrix \(X\) has one row per shark and one column per input (0.5), and \(\mathbf{r}\) is the vector of residuals.
  • \(X^{\top}\) is the transpose (0.5). The product \(X^{\top}\mathbf{r}\) gives all the sums at once. The bias has its own slope, \(-\frac{2}{m}\sum_i r_i\), as before.
  • The closed form is \(\mathbf{w} = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\mathbf{y}\), with a column of ones added for the bias. It needs the inverse of 0.5, which does not exist when an input is a copy of another.

Common belief. More inputs always make a better model.

Why it fails. On the training records, more inputs never raise the error, even when they are noise. Here girth helps for a particular reason: in this made-up table, girth was computed from weight, so it gives part of the answer away. A girth measured with a tape on an actual animal would help less.

Better account. Judge an extra input on records the model has not seen (A4), and ask where its information comes from.

Debug tip. When two inputs are nearly copies, the fitted weights can swing wildly if the data changes slightly, even though the predictions barely move. Check the correlation between inputs. Drop one, or combine them. Scaling helps gradient descent but cannot cure copies: the valley is long and flat along the direction that trades one input for the other.

import numpy as np

# length (cm), girth (cm) and weight (kg) of the same 12 made-up sharks
x = np.array([23.8, 27.8, 31.1, 33.9, 35.8, 37.9, 39.9, 41.0, 44.6, 46.8, 49.7, 55.4])
g = np.array([7.1, 7.8, 9.2, 9.7, 10.2, 11.9, 12.7, 11.5, 11.6, 12.3, 13.3, 16.2])
y = np.array([0.049, 0.068, 0.106, 0.122, 0.146, 0.207, 0.289, 0.228, 0.269, 0.288, 0.335, 0.578])

X = np.c_[(x - x.mean()) / x.std(), (g - g.mean()) / g.std()]   # d = 2 standardised inputs
w, b, eta = np.zeros(2), y.mean(), 0.3
for step in range(300):
    r = y - (X @ w + b)                  # the weighted sum is a dot product for each shark
    w += eta * 2 * X.T @ r / len(y)      # one partial derivative per weight, all at once
    b += eta * 2 * r.mean()
print("gradient descent:", w.round(3), round(b, 3))

A = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(y))]
print("least squares   :", np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0].round(3))
gradient descent: [0.026 0.11 ] 0.224
least squares   : [0.026 0.11  0.224]

Used in: a neuron computes \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b\) and then bends the result (B1). A layer does it for many neurons at once with a matrix (B2). Logistic regression adds a squash to the same sum (A3).

Quick quiz

A model has 5 inputs. How many dials does it have, and how many partial derivatives does one step of gradient descent need?

Six dials: five weights and one bias. One step needs six partial derivatives.

Two inputs are identical copies. What goes wrong with the closed form, and does gradient descent still work?

\(X^{\top}X\) has no inverse, because many weight pairs give the same predictions. Gradient descent still runs and finds one of them. Started from equal weights, it splits the weight between the copies equally.

The loss falls smoothly but slowly after 500 steps. Name two changes that might help, and the risk of the two. (This chapter, 0.6)

Raise the learning rate (risk: overshoot and divergence, since there is a limit of \(2/\lambda_{\max}\)). Standardise the inputs (risk: none for the answer, but new data must be scaled with the same mean and spread). Both aim at the same problem, a long flat valley.

What is the lazy model for a number, and what is its MSE? What is the \(R^2\) of a model that does no better than the lazy one? (A1)

The lazy model says the average of the labels. Its MSE is the variance of the labels (0.0199 kg2 for the spadenose weights). A model that matches it has \(R^2 = 0\).

Shark B is 1.5 times as long as shark A. By the fitted power law, how many times as heavy is B? Can you make the same statement for the straight line? (0.3)

\(1.5^{2.915} \approx 3.26\) times as heavy, whatever the lengths. A straight line has no such ratio: the ratio depends on the lengths, and the line can even predict a negative weight for a short shark.
