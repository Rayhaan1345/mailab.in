Ensembles and boosting

Many small trees that vote, many small trees that correct one another, and how to ask a boosted model what it relies on.

One tree is quick to read and jumpy: a different training sample grows a different tree. This chapter combines many trees in two ways. A random forest lets trees grown on different samples vote. Boosting adds small trees one after another, and the new tree fits what the earlier trees got wrong.

XGBoost is a fast library for boosting, with a penalty that stops a leaf from trusting a few sharks. At the end you ask a boosted SharkCam model which inputs it relies on, and you meet four answers that disagree, and SHAP values that share out one shark's score.

You will need: trees, Gini impurity and the depth limit from A5, and held-out sets from A4. You also need the learning rate and residuals from A2, the sigmoid from A3, counting orders and the bootstrap.

The data. This chapter uses the made-up SharkCam catch table: 600 records generated from published length-weight relationships, not field data. As in A5, sharks whose tag number ends in 0, 1 or 2 (164 sharks) are held out, and the other 436 are the training sharks. The question is "spadenose or not". Sections 1, 3 and 4 use length and depth. Section 2 predicts the weight of the 196 training spadenose sharks from length. Sections 5 and 6 use six measurements. In this table the girth was computed from the weight, so girth repeats part of what weight says.

Random forests in brief

Guess first. Grow one tree on the 436 training sharks. Grow another on a different draw of 436 sharks from the same table. Will the two trees ask the same questions? If you let 100 such trees vote, will the vote be better than the average tree?

ensemble
many models whose answers are combined
bootstrap sample
a sample of the same size drawn with repeats from the training sharks (0.7)
random forest
trees grown on different bootstrap samples, with a randomly chosen measurement tried first at a split, whose answers are combined by a vote
vote share
the share of the trees that say "spadenose"

A forest lets many different trees vote, and the vote is steadier than a single tree.

A single tree grown this way gets 79% of the held-out sharks right on average. The vote of 100 trees gets 85%, with a boundary that is smoother than any one tree's. Slide the number of trees, then click the map to see how the trees vote at one point.

To grow one tree of the forest, draw 436 sharks from the training sharks with repeats. A shark is left out of a draw with chance \((1 - 1/436)^{436}\), about 0.37. A tree therefore sees about 63% of the different sharks, and tree 1 here saw 267 of the 436. At a split the tree tries one randomly chosen measurement first. With only two measurements, that makes the six small trees at the right differ a lot. The trees are weak one by one, but their mistakes point in different directions, and a vote averages them out.

Click the map to move the marked point. The row of squares shows how all 100 trees vote there, and the equation box counts them. A point deep inside a region gets almost all the votes. A point near a boundary gets a split vote, and the vote share gives a rough sense of how sure the forest is. A vote share has not been tested as a probability (A4).

v =63trees voting spadenose÷100trees=0.63vote share⇒spadenosemore than half

\[ v(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbf{1}\big[\text{tree } b \text{ says class 1}\big], \qquad \text{call class 1 if } v(\mathbf{x}) > \tfrac{1}{2} \]

  • \(B\) is the number of trees, and \(b\) counts them from 1 to \(B\).
  • \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is 1 when the statement inside is true and 0 otherwise, so the sum counts the votes for class 1.
  • \(v(\mathbf{x})\) is the vote share for the shark with measurements \(\mathbf{x}\). The number 63 above is a made-up example, and the figure shows true counts.

Why about 63%? One draw misses a given shark with chance \(1 - 1/n\), and \(n\) draws all miss it with chance \((1 - 1/n)^n\). For \(n = 5\) that is 0.328, for \(n = 50\) it is 0.364 and for \(n = 436\) it is 0.367. As \(n\) grows the value settles at \(1/e = 0.368\). The code below draws 1,000 samples of 436 and finds 276 different sharks on average, in a range of 252 to 298. So the 267 of tree 1 is an ordinary draw.

Now why a vote of weak trees can win. Take three trees that are right for 79% of the sharks, about the average single tree here, and suppose their mistakes are independent. The vote is right when all three are right, or when two are right and one is wrong:

0.493all three right: 0.79³+0.393two right, one wrong: 3 × 0.79² × 0.21=0.886the vote is right

With 11 independent trees the vote is right 98.5% of the time, and with 101 trees 100%. The forest of 100 trees reaches 85%, far below that, because its trees were grown on overlapping sharks and tend to make the same mistakes. If the three trees always agreed, the vote would be right 79% of the time, like one tree. A vote gains as much as its trees differ. The bootstrap samples and the random first question are there to make them differ.

In practice, a forest of 100 to 500 trees is a common start, and more trees seldom hurt. The 37% of sharks that a tree did not see form a free test for it. Scoring a tree on its own left-out sharks, and pooling the results, gives the out-of-bag score, which scikit-learn reports with oob_score=True. Forests need no feature scaling. Like single trees, they cannot answer outside the range of the training data.

Common belief. More trees means more overfitting, so a forest of 1,000 trees is riskier than a forest of 10.

Why it fails. The chart in the figure flattens after a few dozen trees and does not turn down. A new tree is one more vote, from a tree grown on its own sample, so it does not chase the mistakes of the others.

Better account. More trees in a forest cost time and memory and give a steadier vote. The single trees can still overfit, so the depth limit still matters. Boosting, in the next section, behaves differently.

Common belief. A forest always beats the tree it is made of.

Why it fails. The depth-2 tree in A5 got 86.0% of these 164 held-out sharks right, and the forest of 100 trees gets 84.8%. The gap of 1.2 points is smaller than the noise of a 164-shark test (about 2.7 points). Here the individual trees are also weaker than that tree, because they try a random measurement first.

Better account. A forest pays off when single trees are jumpy and the boundary is complicated. Compare it with the simple model on held-out data before choosing it (A1).

Debug tip. Check that the trees differ. If they are grown on the same sharks with the same choices, they are copies, and a vote of copies is a single tree.

import numpy as np
from math import comb

# 1. how many different sharks does one bootstrap sample contain?
rng = np.random.RandomState(4127)
n = 436
seen = [len(np.unique(rng.randint(0, n, n))) for _ in range(1000)]
print("left out, from the formula:", round((1 - 1 / n) ** n, 3))
print("different sharks per tree: mean", round(np.mean(seen), 1),
      "range", min(seen), "to", max(seen))

# 2. a vote of k trees, right with chance 0.79 apiece, mistakes independent
def vote_accuracy(k, p):
    return sum(comb(k, i) * p ** i * (1 - p) ** (k - i)
               for i in range(k // 2 + 1, k + 1))

for k in (1, 3, 11, 101):
    print(f"{k:3d} in the vote: accuracy {vote_accuracy(k, 0.79):.3f}")

Quick quiz

Why does drawing with repeats leave a tree with only about 63% of the different training sharks?

A given shark is missed by one draw with chance \(1 - 1/n\), and by all \(n\) draws with chance \((1 - 1/n)^n\), close to 0.37. So about 37% of the sharks are left out and about 63% appear at least once.

Why can a vote of weak trees be better than the average tree?

The trees make different mistakes, because they saw different sharks and tried different questions. Where the mistakes differ, the majority is right more often than any single tree.

Three trees are right for 80% of the sharks. What is the accuracy of their vote if they always make the same mistakes, and if their mistakes are independent?

If they always agree, the vote is one tree: 80%. If the mistakes are independent, the vote is right with chance \(0.8^3 + 3 \times 0.8^2 \times 0.2 = 0.512 + 0.384 = 0.896\), about 90%.

Boosting: a new tree fits what is left over

Guess first. A model that always answers with the average weight of a spadenose shark is wrong by about 0.13 kg. A second model learns only the leftover errors, and the two answers are added. Will the sum be better than the first model? What if a third model learns what is left after that?

boosting
adding small trees one after another, with a new tree fitted to what the trees so far got wrong
residual
\(r = y - F(\mathbf{x})\) as in A2: the actual value minus the model's current answer; the "left over" in the figure
stump
a tree with one question and two leaves
learning rate
\(\eta\), as in A2: here the share of a new tree's answer that is added to the model

Boosting builds a good model by adding small trees one at a time, with the new tree fitted to the residuals of the sum so far.

With 100 stumps and a learning rate of 0.3, the staircase follows the curve. The training error falls from 0.134 kg to 0.030 kg, and the held-out error from 0.158 kg to 0.041 kg. Press Step a few times, then Play. Then change the learning rate and the tree depth and play again.

The upper plot shows 196 training sharks, with weight against length, and the teal staircase is the model after \(k\) trees. At \(k = 0\) the model is the average weight. The lower plot shows what the model gets wrong, \(r = y - F\), for all sharks. Press Step. A stump is fitted to the leftovers. It picks the cut that separates the sharks with positive leftovers from those with negative ones, and it answers with the average leftover on both sides. That is the coral step in the lower plot. Its answer, multiplied by \(\eta\), is added to the model. The faint dots in the lower plot are the old leftovers, the navy dots are the new ones, and the thin lines show how far the dots moved.

0.219model before (the average)+0.3learning rate η×0.144tree's answer h1=0.262model after one tree⇒0.168left over now (was 0.211)

\[ F_0 = \bar{y}, \qquad r_i = y_i - F_{k-1}(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad F_k(\mathbf{x}) = F_{k-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta\, h_k(\mathbf{x}) \]

  • \(F_k\) is the model after \(k\) trees, and \(F_0 = \bar{y}\) is the average of the training values.
  • \(r_i\) is the residual of training shark \(i\): its value minus the model's answer before tree \(k\).
  • \(h_k\) is tree \(k\), fitted to the residuals \(r_i\). On a leaf it answers with the average residual of the sharks in that leaf.
  • \(\eta\) is the learning rate, between 0 and 1. A small \(\eta\) takes small steps and needs more trees.

Try three experiments. Set \(\eta\) to 1: the first trees remove most of the error, and with stumps the held-out error bottoms out near 0.042 kg after about 60 trees. Set \(\eta\) to 0.05: after 100 trees the model has only just caught up, with a held-out error of 0.041 kg, and it is still improving. Set the depth to 3 with \(\eta\) at 0.3. The training error falls to 0.017 kg, but the held-out error is best at 10 trees (0.040 kg) and then rises to 0.045 kg. The numbers differ by a few thousandths of a kilogram, so look at the two curves in the chart rather than at single values.

import numpy as np

def best_stump(x, r):
    """The cut that lowers the squared error of the left-over values r the most."""
    order = np.argsort(x)
    x, r = x[order], r[order]
    n = len(r)
    left_sum = np.cumsum(r)[:-1]
    n_left = np.arange(1, n)
    right_sum = r.sum() - left_sum
    n_right = n - n_left
    gain = left_sum ** 2 / n_left + right_sum ** 2 / n_right   # larger is better
    gain[x[1:] == x[:-1]] = -np.inf                             # no cut between equal values
    i = gain.argmax()
    return (x[i] + x[i + 1]) / 2, left_sum[i] / n_left[i], right_sum[i] / n_right[i]

x = np.array([30., 34., 38., 42., 46., 50., 54., 58.])          # length, cm (made up)
y = np.array([.07, .11, .17, .24, .31, .42, .52, .66])          # weight, kg (made up)
eta = 0.3
F = np.full(len(y), y.mean())                                   # F_0: the average
for k in range(3):
    r = y - F                                                   # what is left over
    cut, left, right = best_stump(x, r)
    F = F + eta * np.where(x < cut, left, right)                # F_k = F_(k-1) + eta * h_k
    print(k + 1, cut, round(float(np.sqrt(np.mean((y - F) ** 2))), 4))

The toy block by hand. The eight weights add up to 2.50 kg, so \(F_0 = 0.3125\), and the leftovers are −0.2425, −0.2025, −0.1425, −0.0725, −0.0025, +0.1075, +0.2075 and +0.3475. The cut at 48.0 cm puts the first five sharks on the left, with an average leftover of −0.1325, and the last three on the right, with +0.2208. Multiply by \(\eta = 0.3\) and the answers are −0.040 and +0.066. So \(F_1\) is 0.273 on the left and 0.379 on the right, and the RMSE falls from 0.194 to 0.151, the first line of the output.

Why does the code pick the cut by left_sum ** 2 / n_left + right_sum ** 2 / n_right? If a side answers with its average leftover, the squared error left on that side is \(\sum r^2 - (\sum r)^2/n\). Add the two sides. The total is \(\sum r^2\) over all sharks, which is the same for any cut, minus the two terms of the code. So the best cut is the one that makes those two terms largest.

Back to Tag 3646. After stump 1 its model value is 0.2622 and its leftover 0.1678. Stump 2 cuts at 46.15 cm. The 40 sharks on the long side still have leftovers that average 0.1614. The model value moves by \(0.3 \times 0.1614 = 0.0484\), to 0.3106, and the leftover falls to 0.1194. Stump 3 cuts at 35.7 cm and answers +0.0501 for the long side. The model value becomes 0.3256 and the leftover 0.1044. Over three stumps the leftover of this shark falls from 0.211 to 0.168, 0.119 and 0.104 kg.

Why residuals? For squared error the loss of one shark is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(y - F)^2\), and its slope with respect to \(F\) is \(-(y - F) = -r\). The residual is the downhill direction. Adding \(\eta\,h_k\) to \(F\) is a step of gradient descent (A2) on the model's answers for the training sharks. A tree cannot move one shark alone, so it moves a whole leaf by its average leftover. For log loss the slope is \(p - y\), and the leftover \(y - p\) of the next section is its negative.

In practice, \(\eta\) and the number of trees go together: a smaller \(\eta\) needs more trees and usually gives a slightly better model. Set \(\eta\) first, for instance 0.05 to 0.1, and let early stopping (the next section) choose the number of trees. Shallow trees, from stumps to depth 5, are the usual weak learners.

Common belief. Boosting is a forest with a different name: many trees whose answers are averaged.

Why it fails. In a forest the trees are grown separately and get equal votes. In boosting tree \(k\) is fitted to the leftovers of trees 1 to \(k-1\), so it cannot be grown before them. The first trees make large moves and the later ones make small corrections.

Better account. A forest averages independent trees to steady a jumpy answer. Boosting adds dependent trees to remove the error that is left, which lowers the bias, and it can overfit if it runs too long.

Analogy. Boosting resembles a golfer. The first shot is long and gets near the hole, and the later shots aim at the distance that is left.

Where it breaks. A golfer sees the hole. A tree sees only the leftovers of the training sharks, and these contain noise, so after many shots the trees start aiming at noise.

Debug tip. Look at the leftovers in the lower plot. A pattern, such as a curve or a wider spread for long sharks, means the model is still missing structure. A cloud with no pattern means more trees will only fit noise. Stop adding trees when the held-out error stops falling (early stopping).

Quick quiz

In \(F_k = F_{k-1} + \eta\, h_k\), what does a small \(\eta\) do?

It scales down a tree's answer, so a step is small and more trees are needed to reach the same error. The same idea as a small learning rate in gradient descent.

What is a stump fitted to, and what does it answer on either side?

It is fitted to the residuals of the model so far. On either side of its cut it answers with the average residual of the sharks on that side.

A shark has a leftover of 0.20 kg, and a stump answers 0.20 for it. With \(\eta = 0.1\), what are the model's change and the new leftover?

The model value rises by \(0.1 \times 0.20 = 0.02\) kg, so the leftover falls from 0.20 to 0.18 kg. A small \(\eta\) removes only a tenth of what the stump sees.

Many small trees draw a boundary

Guess first. Boosting also answers yes/no questions. After 100 trees on length and depth, will its boundary be one clean line, or a patchwork? Will it find more of the held-out sharks than the single tree of A5, which got 86.0% right?

A new tree nudges the scores of the sharks in its boxes, so the boundary sharpens, and past a point it starts to follow noise.

The held-out loss is lowest near 45 trees, at 0.324, and rises to 0.390 by 300 trees while the training loss keeps falling. Press Play, then try a learning rate of 1 and of 0.01, and switch the depth between 1 and 4.

The model starts with a score that gives all sharks the same chance, the share of spadenose among the training sharks (45%). A new tree adds a score to the sharks in its boxes, so the colour moves toward coral or navy there. The first trees draw the big box that A5's tree drew. Later trees add small patches. Watch the black line, where the chance is 0.5, grow ragged around the training sharks as the trees multiply.

The leftover for a yes/no question is the gap between the label and the model's current chance: \(y - p\). A leaf answers with the sum of these gaps divided by a count of how uncertain its sharks are, plus a penalty (next section). The score then goes through the sigmoid, \(p = \sigma(F_k)\), as in A3.

\[ p_i = \sigma\bigl(F_k(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr) \qquad r_i = y_i - p_i \qquad F_k(\mathbf{x}) = F_{k-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta\, h_k(\mathbf{x}) \]

  • \(F_k(\mathbf{x})\) is the score after \(k\) trees. The sigmoid \(\sigma\) turns the score into the chance \(p_i\) that shark \(i\) is a spadenose.
  • \(y_i\) is 1 for a spadenose shark and 0 for any other shark, so the leftover \(r_i\) is how far the chance is from the right answer. \(h_k\) is tree \(k\), fitted to these leftovers, and \(\eta\) is the learning rate.

The learning rate and the number of trees trade against one another. At \(\eta = 1\), 100 trees reach 99.8% on the training sharks and 82.3% on the held-out sharks. The held-out loss is 0.519, far above the 0.324 that a gentler model reaches. At \(\eta = 0.01\), 100 trees have hardly moved the score from the average. Training accuracy is 91.3% and held-out accuracy 86.0%, but the held-out loss is 0.406 and keeps falling to 0.327 at 300 trees. A small \(\eta\) with many trees is slower but gentler.

The boosted model does not beat the single tree here. At its best it gets about 85% of the held-out sharks right, against 86.0% for the depth-2 tree, and the difference is within noise. With two inputs the true boundary is close to a box, which one small tree already draws, and the extra flexibility is a risk. A boosted model pays off when the boundary is intricate. The confusion matrices in 0.8 show a species task with six measurements. There a depth-3 tree and a boosted model have almost the same accuracy but differ on the rare Ganges shark.

Some numbers for the 2D model. Before any tree, all training sharks get the chance 196/436 = 0.4495 of being a spadenose. XGBoost estimates this start from the labels. It is the score \(F_0 = \ln(0.4495/0.5505) = -0.203\). The log loss of this start is 0.688, only a little below the 0.693 of always saying 0.5. One tree of depth 3 with \(\eta = 0.1\) lowers the training loss to 0.620. The code below prints the losses after more trees.

The held-out loss falls to 0.324 at 45 trees. Read it as a chance: the model gives the true class a typical chance of \(e^{-0.324} = 0.72\). Then the held-out loss rises to 0.344 at 100 trees and 0.390 at 300, while the training loss keeps falling to 0.085. The model has begun to memorise the training sharks.

In practice, let the library stop for you. Carve a validation set out of the training sharks, pass it to fit as the eval_set, and set early_stopping_rounds=20. It stops 20 trees after the validation loss last improved, and it records the best round. A small \(\eta\) makes the minimum wide and flat, so the stopping point matters less. The held-out sharks stay untouched for the final score.

Common belief. Boosting beats a single tree.

Why it fails. On this task it ties at best. At \(\eta = 1\) it is worse, because it memorises the training sharks: 99.8% right on them and 82.3% on the held-out sharks.

Better account. A boosted model has dials (trees, learning rate, depth, penalty) that must be set by held-out loss. Compare the result with the simpler model, and do not credit boosting for what the data allow anyway.

Debug tip. When the held-out loss rises while the training loss falls, there are too many trees for this learning rate. Choose the number of trees on a validation set cut from the training sharks, and keep the held-out sharks for one final check (A4). Choosing it on the held-out sharks makes the final score too good.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, xgboost as xgb

df = pd.read_csv("catch.csv")                  # made-up records
df["spadenose"] = (df.species == "spadenose").astype(int)
held = df.record_id % 10 < 3

tr, he = df[~held], df[held]

                          reg_lambda=1, tree_method="exact", random_state=0,
                          eval_metric="logloss")
model.fit(tr[X], tr.spadenose, verbose=False,

Quick quiz

The training loss falls from tree 1 to tree 300, and the held-out loss falls and then rises. Where would you stop, and why?

Near the lowest held-out loss, about 45 trees here. After it the trees fit noise in the training sharks, which does not help new sharks.

Why does a small learning rate need more trees?

A tree moves the scores by only a small share of its answer. It takes many such steps to reach a score as far from the average as a large \(\eta\) reaches in a few.

A model gives the true class a chance of 0.8 for all records. What is its log loss?

The loss of one record is \(-\ln 0.8 = 0.223\), and the average of identical values is the same, 0.223. A model that gives 0.5 to all records scores \(\ln 2 = 0.693\).

What XGBoost adds: a penalty on small leaves

Guess first. One leaf has 2 sharks whose leftovers add up to 0.10 kg. Another leaf has 120 sharks whose leftovers add up to 6.0 kg. Both average 0.05 kg. Should the model trust the two leaves equally?

XGBoost
a library that fits boosted trees quickly, with the penalties below (Chen and Guestrin)
regularisation
a penalty that discourages the model from fitting noise
\(\lambda\)
the leaf penalty: added to the count of sharks in a leaf's denominator
\(\gamma\)
the least improvement a split must give, or the tree does not make it

XGBoost shrinks the answer of a leaf with few sharks more than the answer of a leaf with many, so a few unusual sharks cannot move the model far.

With \(\lambda = 3\), a leaf of 2 sharks answers with 40% of its plain average, a leaf of 12 with 80% and a leaf of 120 with 98%. Slide \(\lambda\) up to 20 and watch the small leaf fade first.

A leaf's plain answer is the average of its leftovers, the sum divided by the count. XGBoost divides by the count plus \(\lambda\). With \(\lambda = 0\) the answer is the plain average. With \(\lambda = 3\), the sum 0.10 over \(2 + 3\) gives 0.020, where the plain average is 0.050, and the sum 6.0 over \(120 + 3\) gives 0.049. A two-shark leaf is probably noise, so its answer is pulled toward zero, and a leaf with 120 sharks keeps almost all of its answer. The 1D and 2D figures have a \(\lambda\) slider too. In the 2D figure, set \(\lambda\) to 20 and play to 300 trees: the held-out loss at 300 trees falls from 0.390 to 0.330.

\[ h_{\text{leaf}} = \frac{\sum_{i\,\in\,\text{leaf}} r_i}{n_{\text{leaf}} + \lambda}, \qquad \text{gain} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\tfrac{G_L^{2}}{H_L+\lambda} + \tfrac{G_R^{2}}{H_R+\lambda} - \tfrac{(G_L+G_R)^{2}}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\Big] - \gamma \]

  • \(r_i\) is the residual of shark \(i\), and \(n_{\text{leaf}}\) the number of sharks in the leaf. The first formula is the simplified form for squared error.
  • \(G\) and \(H\) are the sums over a side of the first and second slopes of the loss (0.6). For squared error \(G = -\sum r_i\) and \(H = n\), which gives the first formula. For yes/no labels, \(H\) is the sum of \(p(1-p)\) over the sharks.
  • \(L\) and \(R\) mark the two sides of a split. The gain compares the two leaves with the leaf they replace, and the split is made only if the gain is above zero after subtracting \(\gamma\).

These formulas come from the XGBoost documentation. A tree is allowed to grow only while splits have a positive gain. So the dials are the number of trees, the learning rate \(\eta\), the depth, \(\lambda\) and \(\gamma\). This is how a few lines produce the six-measurement model of the next two sections (the code keeps the table's made-up records):

import pandas as pd, xgboost as xgb

Now the leaf formula for a yes/no question, with numbers. In the 2D model all sharks start at the chance \(p = 196/436 = 0.4495\). Take the 161 training sharks longer than 59.85 cm. All are other sharks, so \(y = 0\), and the leftover of a shark is \(y - p = -0.4495\). The leftovers add up to −72.4. A shark has \(p(1-p) = 0.2475\), and these add up to 39.8. With \(\lambda = 1\) the leaf answers:

−72.4sum of leftovers: 161 × (−0.4495)÷40.8sum of p(1 − p), 39.8, plus λ = 1×0.1learning rate η=−0.177added to the score of all 161 sharks

The score of these sharks moves from −0.203 to −0.380, a chance of 0.406. The first tree of the library's model for the 2D setting has this leaf, with the value −0.1772. The penalty touches a big leaf lightly: \(\lambda = 1\) keeps 97.6% of the plain value here (39.8 / 40.8). For a leaf of 3 sharks, \(\sum p(1-p)\) is 0.74, and \(\lambda = 1\) keeps only 43% of the plain value (0.74 / 1.74).

The gain formula needs numbers too. Use the first split of the weight example in A5, with squared error. Of the 196 training sharks, 126 are shorter than 42.15 cm and 70 are longer. The leftovers around the average add up to −10.07 on the left and +10.07 on the right. So \(G_L = +10.07\), \(G_R = -10.07\), \(H_L = 126\) and \(H_R = 70\), and the parent term is zero because \(G_L + G_R = 0\). The gain is half the sum of the two fractions, less \(\gamma\):

With \(\lambda = 0\) the gain is half the drop in squared error found in A5 (2.252 / 2 = 1.126). A larger \(\lambda\) lowers the gain of all splits, and lowers it more when the sides are small. A split is made only if the gain beats \(\gamma\). With \(\gamma = 1\) this split is made at \(\lambda = 1\), since 1.113 − 1 is positive, and refused at \(\lambda = 20\), since 0.910 − 1 is negative.

In practice, XGBoost's defaults are \(\eta = 0.3\), depth 6, \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\gamma = 0\). It also asks for a sum of \(H\) of at least 1 in a leaf (min_child_weight). For yes/no labels \(H\) is at most 0.25 per shark, so that minimum forbids leaves of fewer than 4 sharks. The scikit-learn wrapper builds 100 trees unless you say otherwise. For a table of a few hundred rows, depth 3 to 5 and \(\eta\) from 0.01 to 0.1 with early stopping are a more usual start. Set \(\eta\) and the depth first, then tune \(\lambda\) and \(\gamma\) one at a time on validation data.

Common belief. A penalty only makes a model worse, because it pulls the answers away from the data.

Why it fails. The answers it pulls hardest are those of leaves with few sharks, which are mostly noise. In the 2D figure the held-out loss at 300 trees is 0.390 with \(\lambda = 1\) and 0.330 with \(\lambda = 20\).

Better account. A penalty trades a little fit to the training sharks for steadiness on new ones. Treat \(\lambda\) and \(\gamma\) as dials and set them by held-out loss.

Debug tip. If training accuracy is near 100% and held-out accuracy is far lower, raise \(\lambda\) or \(\gamma\), lower the depth, lower \(\eta\) and stop earlier. Change one dial at a time and keep a table of the held-out loss.

Quick quiz

Leaf A has 4 sharks with leftovers adding up to 0.2 kg, and \(\lambda = 4\). What does the leaf answer, and what is the plain average?

The answer is \(0.2/(4+4) = 0.025\) kg. The plain average is \(0.2/4 = 0.05\) kg, so the penalty halves it.

What does \(\gamma\) do?

It subtracts a fixed cost from the gain of any split, so a split that improves the score by less than \(\gamma\) is not made.

A split has \(G_L = 4\), \(H_L = 10\), \(G_R = -4\), \(H_R = 6\), with \(\lambda = 2\) and \(\gamma = 0.5\). Find its gain. Is the split made?

The parent term is \((4 - 4)^2/(16 + 2) = 0\). The gain is \(\tfrac{1}{2}[16/12 + 16/8 - 0] - 0.5 = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.333 + 2) - 0.5 = 1.17\). It is positive, so the split is made.

Which inputs matter? Four answers

Guess first. In the six-measurement model, the trees ask about length 84 times and about weight 82 times. Which of the two matters more to the model's calls? How would you check?

feature importance
any number that says how much a model relies on an input
split count
how many times the trees ask about an input
gain
the average improvement in the training score per question about an input
shuffle test
shuffle one input among the held-out sharks and see how much accuracy drops (also called permutation importance)

The four common importance measures can disagree, because they answer different questions.

Length is asked about 84 times, more often than weight (82), yet shuffling length costs 0.1 points of held-out accuracy and shuffling weight costs 8.1. Click a row, then run 50 shuffles for an input and watch the dots.

Weight is the clear leader in gain (17.95) and the shuffle test (8.1 points) and in SHAP, which comes in the next section. The split count disagrees: depth is asked about 122 times and girth 98, more than weight. The count measures how often the trees ask, not how much any answer changes. Length is a good example. The made-up weights follow a law of length, and the girths are built from the weights, so weight and girth contain nearly all the information that length has. Once the trees have asked about weight, a question about length only adjusts a score slightly, so it is asked often and moves little.

The shuffle test is direct. It keeps the values of a column but breaks their link to the shark. If the held-out accuracy falls, the model was using the input. Water temperature gets a shuffle cost of −3.0 points. Shuffling it makes the model more accurate, because the model had learned a pattern from temperature in the training sharks that does not apply to new ones. Month costs no accuracy.

I =8.15points lost, weight=85.37%held-out accuracy as it is−77.22%with weight shuffled (mean of 50)

\[ I_j = \text{accuracy}(\text{held-out sharks}) - \text{accuracy}(\text{held-out sharks with column } j \text{ shuffled}) \]

  • \(I_j\) is the shuffle importance of input \(j\), in points of accuracy. A repeat of the shuffle gives a slightly different value, so the figure averages 50 shuffles.
  • A negative \(I_j\) means that shuffling the input helped, which suggests the model fitted noise in it.

Does a shuffle cost of 0.1 mean length is useless? No. The check is to drop it and retrain. The model without length gets 85.4% of the held-out sharks right, the same score as the model with all six, so for this model length is redundant. A model that sees only length and depth gets 84.8%, and one that sees weight alone gets 82.9%. Length is unimportant to this model because two other inputs repeat it. A shuffle test can underrate an input whose information appears elsewhere, since the model leans on the copy.

The gain column explains part of the disagreement. The figure lists the average gain per question: weight 17.95, girth 6.34, depth 3.52 and length 1.32. Multiply by the count to get the total gain of an input. Weight gives 82 × 17.95 ≈ 1,472, girth 98 × 6.34 ≈ 621, depth 122 × 3.52 ≈ 430 and length 84 × 1.32 ≈ 111. Depth is asked about most often, but its questions gain little, so its total is under a third of weight's. Gain is still measured on the training sharks, so it can overrate an input just as a count does.

Now the shuffle arithmetic. The model gets 140 of the 164 held-out sharks right. With weight shuffled it gets 126.6 right on average, so it loses 13.4 sharks, about 8.1 points. A single shuffle is noisy. The cost of one shuffle of weight swings by about 2.5 points (one SD) around its average, so the figure averages 50 of them. The error of that average is about \(2.5/\sqrt{50} = 0.35\) points.

Shuffling has a limit too. It can build sharks that cannot exist, such as a 40 cm shark with the weight of a 90 cm one, and the model has seen none like that. Part of the cost then measures how the model behaves in odd places. In practice, run the shuffle test on held-out or validation sharks, repeat it, and plant a random column as a control. Scikit-learn's permutation_importance repeats the shuffles for you with n_repeats.

Common belief. The input a model asks about most often is the one that matters most.

Why it fails. A count includes the many small adjustments of questions that change almost no calls. A column of random numbers can be asked about often too. Adding one to the model, a column of normal random numbers, gets it asked about 73 times, more than length (47) or temperature (42) in that run. Shuffling it costs about 1 point, which is within the noise of the test.

Better account. Measure the effect directly, with a shuffle test or SHAP values, and say which question a number answers. Where two measures disagree, find out why before reporting either.

Quick quiz

What does a split count measure that a shuffle test does not?

It measures how often the trees ask about an input. It does not show how much those questions change the calls, which is what the shuffle test checks.

Shuffling water temperature raises held-out accuracy by 3 points. What does that suggest?

The model fitted a pattern in temperature that applies to the training sharks only. Shuffling removes the pattern, so the calls improve. The input is worse than useless for this model.

Input A is asked about 100 times with an average gain of 0.5. Input B is asked about 20 times with an average gain of 5. Which has the larger total gain?

A has \(100 \times 0.5 = 50\) and B has \(20 \times 5 = 100\). B has twice the total gain, although it is asked about a fifth as often.

Sharing out a score: SHAP values

Guess first. The model gives Tag 3305, the Sassoon spadenose, a score of 4.00, which is a 98% chance of spadenose. The model's average score is −0.24. The 4.24 points between them are shared out among six measurements. Which measurement gets the biggest share, and which the smallest?

average score
the model's score for a typical training shark, the starting point of the waterfall
SHAP value
\(\phi_j\): an input's share of the gap between a shark's score and the average score
Shapley value
the game idea behind it: an input's added effect, averaged over all orders in which the inputs could be revealed
waterfall
a chart that adds the shares one by one from the average score to the shark's score

A shark's score is the average score plus one share per input. A share is the input's average added effect over all 720 orders in which the six measurements could be revealed.

For Tag 3305 the shares add up to 4.24: weight +2.05, girth +1.14, depth +0.52, month +0.32, length +0.19 and temperature +0.02. Switch to "One order" to see one way of revealing the measurements, press "Another order", and click a row to see its step in all 720 orders.

Imagine the model first knowing none of the measurements, so it answers with the average score. Now reveal the measurements one at a time. After a reveal the expected score moves. A measurement's step depends on the order: weight adds more when it comes first than when girth, which says nearly the same, has come before. So take all orders and average a measurement's steps. There are \(6! = 720\) orders (0.4). In the "One order" view, the diamonds mark the average step, and the coral or navy bars are the steps of that one order. The strip on the right shows the 720 steps of one input, and their average is its SHAP value.

How can a tree ensemble score a shark when some measurements are unknown? For a known measurement the trees follow the shark's value. For an unknown one the trees split their answer between both branches, in proportion to the share of training sharks that went either way. The figure does this for all \(2^6 = 64\) sets of known measurements and combines them. For 30 inputs that would be a billion sets, so TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al.) shares the work along the paths of the trees, and XGBoost's pred_contribs uses that method.

\[ F(\mathbf{x}) = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{d} \phi_j, \qquad \phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq \{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(d-|S|-1)!}{d!}\,\big[v(S\cup\{j\}) - v(S)\big] \]

  • \(F(\mathbf{x})\) is the shark's score, \(\phi_0\) the average score, and \(\phi_j\) the SHAP value of input \(j\). The first formula is the sum in the equation boxes of the figure.
  • \(S\) is a set of inputs already revealed, \(d\) is the number of inputs (6), and \(v(S)\) is the model's expected score when only the inputs in \(S\) are known.
  • The fraction is the share of the \(d!\) orders in which only the inputs of \(S\) come before \(j\), so the sum is an average over orders.

The same numbers from the library, for Tag 3305. The last value is the average score, and the shares plus the average add up to the model's own score:

tag = df[df.record_id == 3305][F6]                           # the Sassoon spadenose
shares = booster.predict(xgb.DMatrix(tag), pred_contribs=True)[0]
print([round(float(v), 2) for v in shares])                  # six shares, then the average score
print(round(float(shares.sum()), 2), round(float(booster.predict(xgb.DMatrix(tag), output_margin=True)[0]), 2))

Now a check on the fraction in the formula, for \(d = 6\). The weight of a set \(S\) is the share of the 720 orders in which the inputs of \(S\), and no others, come before \(j\). The inputs of \(S\) can be ordered in \(|S|!\) ways, then \(j\), then the other \(d - |S| - 1\) inputs in \((d - |S| - 1)!\) ways. With \(|S| = 0\) that is \(0! \times 5! = 120\) orders, a weight of 120/720 = 1/6. With \(|S| = 2\) it is \(2! \times 3! = 12\) orders, a weight of 1/60. The table gives all sizes.

The sets of one size share a sixth of the weight. So \(\phi_j\) is the plain average of six numbers, where the number for size \(s\) is the average step of \(j\) over the sets of size \(s\).

Why this rule for sharing? Lundberg and Lee (2017) show that one rule, and only one, meets three demands. The shares add up to the score. An input the model never uses gets zero. An input's share never falls when the model comes to rely on it more. The SHAP value is that rule.

Inputs that repeat one another split the credit. Tag 3305's weight and girth get +2.05 and +1.14 although they contain almost the same information. A small share does not prove that an input is unneeded, which is the limit the shuffle test had for length.

In practice, the shares are in score units (log-odds), so add them to the average score and apply the sigmoid to get a chance. The mean of the absolute shares over a set of sharks gives a ranking of the inputs. The code below does this for the 164 held-out sharks. The order is weight, girth, depth, length, month and temperature, which differs from the split counts, where depth came first.

Quick quiz

Why is the SHAP value an average over orders instead of one step?

A measurement's step depends on which measurements were revealed before it, especially when inputs repeat one another. Averaging over all orders gives an input a fair share that does not depend on one chosen order.

The shares for one shark are +1.0, +0.5 and −0.2, and the average score is −0.3. What is the shark's score and its chance?

The score is \(-0.3 + 1.0 + 0.5 - 0.2 = 1.0\). The chance is \(\sigma(1.0) = 1/(1+e^{-1}) \approx 0.73\).

Tag 4127 has a depth share of −3.51 and a weight share of +0.63. What does the +0.63 say?

On its own, the shark's weight pushed the score toward spadenose. The depth share of −3.51 outweighed it, so the final score is −3.74. A share describes the model's reading of one input, and the model's reading can differ from what the input means for the animal.

A forest of 100 trees gets 85% right and its average single tree 79%. Using the bootstrap idea from 0.7, why do different trees disagree, and why does a vote help?

A tree is grown on a resample, which leaves out about 37% of the sharks and repeats others, so the trees ask different questions and make different mistakes. A majority is right more often than a typical tree when the mistakes differ.

The learning rate appears in gradient descent (A2) and in boosting. What do the two have in common, and what differs?

Both scale a step: a small value takes small steps, needs more of them, and is gentler. In gradient descent the step moves weights along a slope. In boosting the step adds a share of a new tree's answer to the model.

The six-measurement model has 720 orders and 64 sets of known inputs. How many orders and sets would 30 inputs have, and why does that matter for SHAP? (0.4)

\(30!\) is about \(2.7 \times 10^{32}\) orders and \(2^{30}\) is about \(10^9\) sets. Listing either is too slow, so TreeSHAP uses the structure of the trees to get the same averages without listing them.
