One neuron

A weighted sum, then a bend. Three models you already know are one neuron with different bends.

A neural network is built from one small part, used many times: the neuron. A neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias, and passes the total through a function called the activation. This chapter takes one neuron apart with sliders. With no activation it is the line of A2. With a sigmoid it is the logistic regression of A3. With a step it is the perceptron, the oldest neural network. Then you tune one neuron to tell two kinds of sharks apart, and put three neurons side by side to make a layer.

The sharks are made up: records generated from published length-weight relationships, not field data.

You will need: the dot product and matrix as a layer, the sigmoid curve, the slopes of the activations, and the model chapters A2 and A3.

A neuron, taken apart

Guess first. A neuron adds up two inputs with weights and a bias, then applies an activation. Suppose you change the activation from "none" to "ReLU", which keeps positive values and turns negative ones into 0. On the map of outputs, which part changes: the part where the weighted sum is positive, or the part where it is negative?

neuron
a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, passed through an activation
weight
how strongly one input pushes the sum, like the slope in A2
bias \(b\)
a constant added to the sum; it moves the place where the sum is zero
weighted sum \(z\)
\(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b\), the score of A3
activation \(g\)
a function applied to \(z\); it decides how the output depends on the sum

A neuron computes the weighted sum \(z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b\) and outputs \(a = g(z)\). The weights set the direction in which the output changes, the bias sets where \(z\) is zero, and the activation sets how the output rises across that line.

No activationthe output is \(z\) itself: linear regression (A2)
Sigmoidthe output is a probability: logistic regression (A3)
Stepthe output is 0 or 1: the perceptron (section 3)
tanh, ReLU, GELUthe choices inside deeper networks (B2)

At the starting dials, \(z = 1.5 \times 0.8 + (-1.0) \times 0.4 + 0.3 = 1.10\) and the sigmoid gives 0.750. For all six activations, the output is constant along straight lines parallel to the dashed line \(z = 0\).

The star on the map is the input \((x_1, x_2)\). Two sliders move it, and so does a click or a drag on the map. The three dials \(w_1\), \(w_2\) and \(b\) set the weighted sum. The dashed line is where \(z = 0\). The coral arrow \(\mathbf{w}\) points in the direction in which \(z\) rises fastest, and it always meets the dashed line at a right angle. The curve on the left is the activation, with a teal dot at the current \(z\). The boxes above the curve show the arithmetic with the numbers you chose.

Try this. Set \(w_2\) to 0, then choose ReLU, then double all three dials with the sigmoid. Predict what you will see before you move anything.

Check your predictions

With \(w_2 = 0\), \(z = w_1 x_1 + b\) has no \(x_2\) in it, so the stripes become vertical and the output ignores \(x_2\). ReLU changes only the half of the map where \(z\) is negative, which turns flat at 0. The half where \(z\) is positive looks the same as with no activation. Doubling the three dials leaves the dashed line where it was, because \(z = 0\) at the same points. The stripes move closer together, so the output switches from near 0 to near 1 over a shorter distance.

The straight stripes have a reason. The output depends on the input only through \(z\), and \(z\) is constant along any line \(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 = \text{const}\). So a single level of the output, such as \(a = 0.5\), is always a straight line. That is the decision boundary of A3, and it is why one neuron can only cut the plane with a line.

\[ z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b \qquad\qquad a = g(z) \]

  • \(x_1, x_2\) are the inputs, collected in the vector \(\mathbf{x}\). \(w_1, w_2\) are the weights, collected in \(\mathbf{w}\). The product \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}\) is the dot product of 0.5.
  • \(b\) is the bias. With \(b = 0\) the line \(z = 0\) must pass through the point where both inputs are 0, which is a strong restriction.
  • \(g\) is the activation and \(a\) is the output. With \(d\) inputs the sum has \(d\) weights and one bias, so \(d + 1\) numbers in all, and the boundary is a flat plane with \(d - 1\) dimensions.

Common belief. A curved activation such as the sigmoid or tanh lets a neuron draw a curved boundary.

Why it fails. The map above shows straight contour lines for all six activations. The output depends on the input only through \(z\), and all levels of \(z\) are straight lines in the plane.

Better account. A single neuron cuts the plane with one straight line, whatever its activation. The activation changes how the output rises across the line. Curved boundaries need several neurons (B2).

Analogy. A neuron is like a nerve cell: it collects signals from many cells and passes on a signal that is stronger when the total is larger.

Where it breaks. The artificial neuron is a formula. It has no timing, no chemistry and no living cell. Most activations give a smooth number and do not fire or stay quiet. The name stays because the picture of inputs, weights and one output is useful.

Debug tip. If a sigmoid neuron outputs 0 or 1 for all inputs, print \(z\). A large \(|z|\) means the sigmoid is saturated, and the usual cause is inputs on a large scale, such as lengths in centimetres next to depths in metres. Scale the inputs first (A2).

import numpy as np

x = np.array([41.0, 11.5])                      # Tag 3305: length and girth, cm
mean, spread = np.array([39.138, 10.993]), np.array([8.614, 2.455])
w, b = np.array([4.732, -0.1215]), 2.4811       # the weights fitted in A3

xs = (x - mean) / spread                        # scale the inputs, as in A2
z = w @ xs + b                                  # 1. weighted sum
a = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))                        # 2. activation (sigmoid)
print(f"z = {z:.2f}, output a = {a:.3f}")       # 3. the neuron's output
z = 3.48, output a = 0.970

The weights were fitted to spadenose sharks, which mature at about 35 cm, while bamboo sharks mature at about 53 cm. A neuron describes the data it was fitted on, and gives a confident answer outside it (A3).

In practice: inputs are scaled to a mean of 0 and a spread of 1. Weights start small and random, with a typical size of 0.1 to 1. The bias starts at 0. A weight means something only next to the scale of its input: 0.5 per centimetre is a large weight, and 0.5 per spread is a small one. Compare the size of two weights only after scaling.

Used in: a network is many such neurons wired in rows (section 4) and in stacks (B2). My diabetes screening app runs a small neural network, and my project primer has a one-unit figure on glucose and BMI.

Quick quiz

A neuron has \(w_1 = 2\), \(w_2 = -1\), \(b = -1\) and no activation. What is its output for \(x = (1, 3)\)?

\(z = 2 \times 1 + (-1) \times 3 + (-1) = -2\). With no activation the output is \(a = z = -2\).

You add 5 to the bias. Does the line \(z = 0\) move, turn, or stay?

It moves, parallel to itself. The direction depends only on \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), so the line does not turn. The bias slides it along \(\mathbf{w}\).

Six activations

Guess first. A sigmoid neuron has a weighted sum of \(z = 8\), so its output is 0.9997. You raise \(z\) by 1. Does the output change a lot or a little? And what about a ReLU neuron at \(z = 8\)?

activation function
the function \(g\) applied to the weighted sum
slope \(g'(z)\)
how much the output changes for a small change in \(z\) (0.6)
saturated
on a flat tail of the curve, where the slope is close to 0
dead unit
a ReLU neuron whose \(z\) is negative for all inputs, so its slope is 0

The six activations differ in where they are flat and where they have slope. The slope matters because learning pushes the weights along it: where the slope is 0, the output cannot teach the weights anything.

The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25 and falls below 0.01 when \(|z|\) passes 4.6. At \(z = 8\) it is 0.0003, while ReLU's slope is still 1.

Drag \(z\) from the left end to the right end with the sigmoid chosen. The navy curve is the output, and the dashed sand curve is its slope. The slope peaks at \(z = 0\) and drops close to 0 in both tails. A neuron in a tail is saturated: a large change in the weights moves \(z\), but the output hardly responds, so learning is slow. At \(z = 8\) the sigmoid's slope is 0.0003, about 750 times smaller than the 0.25 it has at \(z = 0\). An update that would take one step at \(z = 0\) takes hundreds there. The tanh has the same problem in its tails, though its slope peaks at 1 instead of 0.25. In fact \(\tanh z = 2\sigma(2z) - 1\): it is the sigmoid stretched to run from \(-1\) to 1 and sharpened by a factor of 2 (0.3).

ReLU has a slope of 1 for all positive \(z\), so it never saturates on that side. Its price is the other side: for negative \(z\) the slope is 0, and a neuron that sees only negative sums gets no signal at all. GELU is the smooth cousin. It multiplies \(z\) by \(\Phi(z)\), the chance that a standard normal value falls below \(z\) (0.7). Large positive \(z\) passes almost unchanged. Large negative \(z\) is squashed to almost 0. The corner at 0 is rounded. Hendrycks and Gimpel proposed it in 2016, and the original GPT and BERT language models use it (D5). The step has slope 0 wherever it is defined, so gradient descent has no slope to follow, and the perceptron of the next section uses its own rule.

  • \(\Phi(z)\) is the cumulative normal curve: the share of a standard normal distribution below \(z\). \(\varphi(z)\) is the bell curve itself. Both come from 0.7.
  • The sigmoid slope \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\) is the rule from 0.6. At \(z = 0\) it is \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\).
  • At \(z = 0\) ReLU has a corner. Software picks a slope of 0 there, which almost never matters.
  • The last two columns are worked values. For GELU at \(z = 1\): \(\Phi(1) = 0.8413\) and \(\varphi(1) = 0.2420\), so the output is \(1 \times 0.8413 = 0.841\) and the slope is \(0.8413 + 1 \times 0.2420 = 1.083\). The slope is above 1 because the curve is still bending upwards there.
Show the steps behind the three slope rules

Sigmoid. Write \(\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}\). The chain rule (0.6) gives the slope of the outer power, then of the inner sum:

\[ \sigma'(z) = -(1 + e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z}) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^{2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr) \]

The last step uses \(1 - \sigma(z) = \dfrac{1 + e^{-z} - 1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \dfrac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}\).

tanh. With \(u = e^{z} - e^{-z}\) and \(v = e^{z} + e^{-z}\) the slopes are \(u' = v\) and \(v' = u\). The quotient rule gives:

\[ \frac{d}{dz}\frac{u}{v} = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^{2}} = \frac{v^{2} - u^{2}}{v^{2}} = 1 - \tanh^{2} z \]

GELU. It is a product \(z \cdot \Phi(z)\), so the product rule gives \(1\cdot\Phi(z) + z\,\Phi'(z)\), and the slope of the cumulative curve \(\Phi\) is the bell curve \(\varphi\).

In practice: ReLU is a common default for hidden layers, and GELU is used in several well-known transformer models. A sigmoid suits the output when the answer is a probability. No activation suits an output that can take any value, such as a temperature change. A frequent mistake is a ReLU at the output of a model that must predict values that can be negative: it can never output them.

Common belief. ReLU is two straight pieces, so it adds no value over using no activation.

Why it fails. A neuron on its own gains little from the bend: it still cuts the plane with a line. But a stack of neurons needs a bend. Without a bend, stacked layers collapse to one layer (B2).

Better account. The choice of activation matters less for one neuron than for a network. It sets which slopes stay large when many layers multiply them (B4, B6).

Debug tip. If a ReLU network does not learn at all, count how many neurons have a negative \(z\) for the whole batch. A neuron that is dead for all inputs has a slope of 0, so no update reaches it. A smaller learning rate and a sensible start often stop units dying (B8).

import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt

z = np.array([-2.0, 0.0, 2.0])

    "GELU":    np.array([v * 0.5 * (1 + erf(v / sqrt(2))) for v in z]),
}

Quick quiz

At \(z = 0\), which has the larger slope: the sigmoid or tanh?

The tanh: its slope is 1 at \(z = 0\), against 0.25 for the sigmoid.

A ReLU neuron has \(z = -3\) for the shark you are looking at. What happens to the weights if you train on that shark alone?

The weights do not change. The slope is 0, so the shark sends no signal back to them.

Fit a neuron to the sharks

Guess first. The made-up table has 200 milk sharks and 72 grey bamboo sharks. You know the length and the depth of all 272 of them. Always answering "milk shark" is right 73.5% of the time. Can one neuron with two inputs get to 90%?

perceptron
a neuron with a step activation, tuned by a simple rule: when a call is wrong, move the weights towards the right class
training
changing the dials to improve the score (A1)
learning rate \(\eta\)
the size of one step (A2)
score
here, the share of the 272 sharks called correctly

The same two-input neuron fits the sharks as a line, as a logistic regression or as a perceptron, depending on the activation and on the rule that tunes the dials.

With a sigmoid the neuron reaches 91.5% (249 of 272), against 73.5% for the lazy answer. With no activation it reaches 90.1%, and with tanh 91.2%. With a step it wobbles between about 80% and 91% over the first 40 passes, because the two kinds of shark overlap.

Navy circles are milk sharks and coral diamonds are bamboo sharks. A dark ring marks a shark the neuron calls wrong. The star is Tag 4127, the juvenile bamboo shark from the Juhu rock pool (44.0 cm, found at 0.4 m). First tune the dials by hand. Drag \(w_1\), \(w_2\) and \(b\) until the dashed line splits the two clouds and watch the score. Then press "Train" and see what the computer does with the sigmoid. Switch to "none", "tanh" and "step" and train again from "Reset the dials". The inputs are scaled as in A2, so the weights are comparable: a negative weight on length and depth says that shorter, shallower sharks are more likely bamboo.

Three activations, three old friends. With no activation the neuron computes \(\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b\). Training minimises squared error, so this is linear regression (A2), here fitted to the labels 0 and 1. With a sigmoid it computes a probability and training minimises log loss: this is logistic regression (A3), and the update is the same loop of A3. With a step it calls "bamboo" when \(z \ge 0\). It cannot use the slope, which is 0. Rosenblatt's perceptron rule moves the weights directly. For a shark with label \(y\) and call \(\hat{y}\), the update adds \(\eta\,(y - \hat{y})\) times the input.

−0.145new \(w_1\)=0old \(w_1\)+0.1learning rate \(\eta\)×1label 1 minus call 0×−1.45length of Tag 4127, scaled

The boxes show one update. Suppose a neuron with weights 0 and 0 and a bias of \(-0.1\) calls Tag 4127 "milk". Its label is 1 and its call is 0, so \(y - \hat{y} = 1\). The scaled length of Tag 4127 is \(-1.45\), so \(w_1\) moves by \(0.1 \times 1 \times (-1.45) = -0.145\). The weight on depth moves by \(0.1 \times (-1.87) = -0.187\) and the bias by \(+0.1\). The new weights make the score of Tag 4127 larger, which is the direction that can fix the call.

The update is not a guess. It adds \(\eta\,(y - \hat{y})\) times the shark's own input vector to the weights. So the score of that shark changes by \(\eta\,(y - \hat{y})\,(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + 1)\), where the last 1 comes from the bias. For Tag 4127 the scaled inputs are \(-1.45\) and \(-1.87\). Then \(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + 1 = 2.09 + 3.50 + 1 = 6.59\). The score rises by \(0.1 \times 1 \times 6.59 = 0.66\), from \(-0.10\) to \(+0.56\). The call flips to "bamboo". A small step can fix one shark and break another, which is why the rule needs several passes.

0.177new \(w_1\)=0.50old \(w_1\)−0.5learning rate \(\eta\)×−0.447\(p - y = 0.553 - 1\)×−1.45scaled length

The sigmoid neuron makes the same kind of move with a soft size. At the starting dials of the figure (\(\mathbf{w} = (0.5, -0.5)\), \(b = 0\)) the score of Tag 4127 is \(z = 0.21\) and the output is \(p = 0.553\). The gap to the label is \(p - y = -0.447\), the push of A3. For this shark alone, \(w_1\) moves from 0.50 to \(0.5 - 0.5 \times (-0.447) \times (-1.45) = 0.177\) and \(w_2\) from \(-0.50\) to \(-0.918\). The step shrinks as \(p\) gets closer to 1, while the perceptron always takes a full step. The figure averages these steps over all 272 sharks.

\[ \hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & z \ge 0\\ 0 & z < 0 \end{cases} \qquad w_j \leftarrow w_j + \eta\,(y - \hat{y})\,x_j \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\,(y - \hat{y}) \]

  • \(y\) is the true label (1 for bamboo, 0 for milk) and \(\hat{y}\) is the call. When they agree, \(y - \hat{y} = 0\) and the weights stay put.
  • \(x_j\) is input \(j\) after scaling. A wrong call on a shark adds or subtracts a multiple of that shark's input vector to the weights, so the dashed line turns towards the shark.
  • For the sigmoid the update of A3 has the same form, with the soft difference \(y - p\) in place of \(y - \hat{y}\).

Common belief. Train a perceptron for long enough and it settles on the best line.

Why it fails. The rule changes the weights whenever some shark is called wrong. When the classes overlap, as here, some shark is always wrong, so the weights never stop moving. The score keeps wobbling. When one line can separate the two classes with no mistakes, the rule is guaranteed to stop. When no such line exists, it cannot stop.

Better account. Use a smooth activation with a smooth loss. The sigmoid with log loss settles on one answer even when the classes overlap, which is why logistic regression replaced the perceptron rule in most work.

In practice: with inputs scaled to a spread of 1, learning rates between 0.01 and 1 are typical for a single neuron. If all the dials start at 0, the learning rate of the perceptron only rescales the weights and never changes a call. Almost nobody trains with the perceptron rule today. It is taught because it shows learning as small corrections.

Debug tip. Compare the score with the lazy answer before you celebrate: here 73.5%. If the score stays at that number and the dials do not move, look for a dead start. With ReLU, press "Random start" a few times: from some starts \(z\) is negative for all sharks, the slope is 0 and the dials stay put.

import numpy as np

# 8 made-up sharks: length (cm), depth (m), label 1 = bamboo, 0 = milk
X = np.array([[58, 14], [49, 20], [66, 30], [54, 6], [70, 24], [46, 12], [62, 18], [57, 3]], dtype=float)
y = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1])
X = (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0)        # scale both inputs

w, b, eta = np.zeros(2), 0.0, 0.1
for epoch in range(1, 21):
    mistakes = 0
    for x, label in zip(X, y):
        call = 1 if w @ x + b >= 0 else 0       # step activation
        if call != label:
            w += eta * (label - call) * x       # move towards the right class
            b += eta * (label - call)
            mistakes += 1
    print(f"epoch {epoch}: {mistakes} mistakes, w = {np.round(w, 3)}, b = {b:.2f}")
    if mistakes == 0:
        break
epoch 1: 3 mistakes, w = [-0.167 -0.046], b = 0.10
epoch 2: 1 mistakes, w = [-0.17  -0.024], b = 0.00
epoch 3: 0 mistakes, w = [-0.17  -0.024], b = 0.00

With eight sharks that one line can separate, the rule stops after three passes. With the 272 sharks of the figure it never stops, as the debug tip warns.

Used in: the perceptron rule is the oldest learning rule for a neuron (Rosenblatt, 1958). Its failure on XOR is the reason for hidden layers (B2). The log-loss update behind the sigmoid neuron is the one that B4 sends back through a whole network.

Quick quiz

A perceptron calls a juvenile shark "adult". Its label is 0 and its call is 1. In which direction does the update move the weights?

\(y - \hat{y} = -1\), so the weights move by \(-\eta\) times the shark's inputs. That lowers the shark's score \(z\), which pushes the call towards 0.

Why does the sigmoid neuron settle on one answer when the classes overlap, while the perceptron keeps moving?

Log loss has a lowest point, and the update shrinks to 0 there because the sigmoid's push \(p - y\) averages to 0. The perceptron update is a fixed step whenever any shark is called wrong, so it never shrinks to 0 while some sharks stay wrong.

A layer is many neurons

Guess first. Three neurons read the same two inputs. The neurons have separate weights and separate biases. How many numbers does the layer have in all? Can you write the three weighted sums as one matrix product?

layer
neurons that read the same inputs side by side (0.5)
weight matrix \(W\)
one row of weights per neuron
width
the number of neurons in a layer
output vector \(\mathbf{a}\)
the list of the neurons' outputs, one number per neuron

A layer of \(n\) neurons is one matrix product followed by the activation: \(\mathbf{a} = g(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})\), with one row of \(W\) and one bias per neuron.

The first row of the table is the neuron of section 1 (\(z = 1.10\), \(a = 0.750\)). Two more neurons read the same inputs, and the three rows together are the matrix product \(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\).

Type new numbers into the table, or move the two input sliders. The three small maps show the neurons one by one: the output over the plane of the inputs, with the teal dot at the current input. A map shows the stripes of a single neuron, and the stripes point in different directions because the weights differ. The bracketed boxes below the table show the same computation as a matrix product, the form that code and graphics cards use. Choose "ReLU" and look for a neuron whose output is 0: its map is flat on the side where \(z\) is negative.

Counting the numbers answers the guess. Three neurons, with two weights and a bias per neuron, have \(3 \times 3 = 9\) numbers. In general a layer with \(n\) neurons and \(d\) inputs has \(n \times d\) weights and \(n\) biases, so \(n\,(d + 1)\) numbers. The softmax classifier of A3 is such a layer with \(n = 5\), one neuron per species, followed by softmax: \(5 \times 3 = 15\) numbers.

The matrix product is three weighted sums done at once. For \(\mathbf{x} = (0.8, 0.4)\) the rows give:

1.10neuron 1=1.5 × 0.8 − 1.0 × 0.4 + 0.3
0.30neuron 2=0.5 × 0.8 + 1.0 × 0.4 − 0.5
−0.60neuron 3=−1.0 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.4 + 0

With the sigmoid the outputs are 0.750, 0.574 and 0.354. With ReLU they are 1.10, 0.30 and 0: neuron 3 is switched off for this input, but it may be on for another. A wider example: a layer of 8 neurons that reads 6 shark measurements has \(8 \times 6 + 8 = 56\) numbers.

In practice: layers are rarely as narrow as three. Widths from a few dozen to a few thousand are common, because a graphics card multiplies one large matrix faster than many small ones. Inputs go in as a batch of sharks, one row per shark, so the product becomes \(X W^\top + \mathbf{b}\) (0.5). A layer's outputs mean little one at a time. They are useful as a group, which the next chapter builds on.

\[ \mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \qquad \mathbf{a} = g(\mathbf{z}) \qquad \text{number of parameters} = n\,(d + 1) \]

  • \(W\) has \(n\) rows and \(d\) columns: one row per neuron and one column per input. \(\mathbf{x}\) has \(d\) entries, and \(\mathbf{b}\) and \(\mathbf{z}\) have \(n\) entries.
  • \(g\) is applied to the entries of \(\mathbf{z}\) separately, so the neurons in a layer do not talk to one another.
  • Layers are written with a number in square brackets, \(W^{[1]}\) for the first, when a network has several (B2).

Analogy. A layer is a panel of experts who look at the same photo, and report one number apiece.

Where it breaks. The experts in a layer never hear one another. Their only difference is their weights. Two neurons with the same weights and the same bias give the same output for any input. They receive the same updates, so they stay copies of one another. Training starts from different random weights for this reason (B6).

Debug tip. Print the dimensions of the arrays before you multiply, as in the first line of the code below. For \(n\) neurons and \(d\) inputs they should be \((n, d)\), \((d,)\) and \((n,)\). Swapping the rows and columns of \(W\) gives an error, or, worse, a plausible but wrong answer when \(n = d\).

import numpy as np

W = np.array([[1.5, -1.0], [0.5, 1.0], [-1.0, 0.5]])   # 3 neurons, 2 inputs: one row per neuron
b = np.array([0.3, -0.5, 0.0])
x = np.array([0.8, 0.4])
print(W.shape, x.shape, b.shape)                        # expect (3, 2) (2,) (3,)

z = W @ x + b                                           # three weighted sums at once
a = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))                                # sigmoid on all three
print("z =", np.round(z, 2))
print("a =", np.round(a, 3))
print("numbers in the layer:", W.size + b.size)
(3, 2) (2,) (3,)
z = [ 1.1  0.3 -0.6]
a = [0.75  0.574 0.354]
numbers in the layer: 9

Used in: a network is a stack of such layers, and B2 shows why a stack needs an activation between its layers. The five outputs of the species classifier in A3 are a layer of five neurons.

Quick quiz

A layer has 4 neurons and 6 inputs. What are the dimensions of \(W\), and how many numbers does the layer have?

\(W\) has 4 rows and 6 columns. The layer has \(4 \times 6 + 4 = 28\) numbers: 24 weights and 4 biases.

Two neurons in a layer have identical weights and biases. What does the layer gain from the second one?

It gains no new information. Both give the same output for all inputs, so the second adds a copy of the first.

Come back later

Try these after two days and again after a week, before you open the answers.

A neuron has no activation, one input and squared error. Which model from Part A is it, and what is its update rule for the slope?

It is the line of A2. With residuals \(r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\), the update is \(w \leftarrow w + \eta\,\tfrac{2}{m}\sum_i r_i x_i\).

A sigmoid neuron gives a juvenile shark (label 0) the probability 0.9 of being an adult. Compare the push on the score \(z\) from log loss with the push from squared error (A3).

Log loss pushes with \(p - y = 0.9\). Squared error pushes with \(2(p - y)\,p\,(1 - p) = 2 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.162\), about five times less, because the sigmoid is flat there.

One straight line cannot separate XOR (0.4). What does that say about a single neuron with any activation, and what would you add to fix it?

A single neuron's decision is always one straight line, so it cannot do XOR whatever its activation. More neurons, arranged in layers with a bend between them, can. That is B2.
