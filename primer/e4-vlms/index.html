Part E · Chapter 4

Vision-language models

A photo and a question go in and an answer comes out: how the parts are joined, what a photo costs in tokens, and where the answers go wrong

A vision-language model (VLM) answers questions about photos. It joins a vision encoder (E1) to a language model (D5). The join is either a projector that turns patch vectors into tokens placed in the prompt (LLaVA), or cross-attention layers inside the language model (E3, Flamingo). This chapter follows one photo and the question "Which shark is this, and is it protected?" through a small open model, and counts the tokens at the steps. It trains a model in two stages in the manner of LLaVA. It tests the model on counting, small text, names and murky water. Then it asks how such a model could adapt after deployment. That is the question of my project at MBZUAI.

All answers on this page are the output of Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct (Alibaba Cloud, Apache-2.0), run offline in 32-bit floating point with greedy decoding unless the text says otherwise. The answers were copied into data files. A 2 billion number model is small, and the tests use a handful of photos, so the results describe these runs only. They are no benchmark. Examples written for this page are labelled. Photos are credited under the figures.

You will need: patches and image tokens (E1), encoders (E2), cross-attention (E3), the generation loop and the KV cache (D5), prompts and templates (D7) and murky water (C3).

From photo to answer

Guess first. You send SharkCam a photo of a hammerhead, 640 by 362 pixels, with the question "Which shark is this, and is it protected?" The question has 10 tokens. How many tokens will the photo take inside the model: fewer than the question, about the same, or many more? And which half of the question can the photo answer?

vision-language model (VLM)
a model that reads photos and text and writes text
image token
one vector in the language model's input for a patch, or a block of patches, of the photo
projector
the layers that map image features to the width of the language model; also called the connector
prompt
all the tokens the model reads before it writes, with the special tokens of its template (D7)

A VLM is three parts in a row: a vision encoder, a projector and a language model. The photo reaches the language model as hundreds of image tokens, and that is many more than the question has.

About the model and the numbers

The model is Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct. Its vision tower has 24 blocks of width 1,024 and 406,957,056 numbers. Its language model has 28 layers of width 2,048 and 1,720,574,976 numbers, of which 311,164,928 are the table of word vectors. The numbers of tokens are counted from the prompt that the processor builds. The mean lengths of the vectors and the answer are measured on this photo. The answer is shown as the model wrote it, with its Markdown marks (the asterisks are bold type). The question is the same for all photos, so the answers differ only because of the photo and the model.

For the hammerhead photo the image takes 220 tokens and the question 10, so the photo costs 22 times as much as the question. The model then writes 196 tokens. The other photos take 260 to 300 tokens.

Press "Next step" six times, or "Play", and change the photo. The processor first resizes the photo so that both sides are multiples of 32, which makes 640 by 362 into 640 by 352. It cuts the photo into 16 by 16 patches, 22 rows by 40 columns, 880 patches (E1). A vision transformer with 24 blocks turns the 880 patches into 880 vectors of 1,024 numbers. The projector then joins 2 by 2 neighbouring patches into one vector of 4,096 numbers and maps it through two layers to 2,048 numbers, the width of the language model. That gives 880 / 4 = 220 image tokens. The prompt is 10 template tokens, the 220 image tokens and the 10 tokens of the question: 240 tokens in all. The model then writes the answer, one token at a time, using the key-value cache of D5.

A second photo, to see the formula work. The bamboo shark photo is 640 by 480, so both sides are already multiples of 32 and the grid is 30 by 40 patches. Joining 2 by 2 gives 15 by 20 = 300 image tokens, and the prompt has 320 tokens. The photo costs 30 times as much as the question. The cache takes 114,688 bytes per token (28 layers, 8 key and value heads of 128 numbers, keys and values, 16 bits). The prompt of 320 tokens alone keeps 320 × 114,688 = 36.7 MB. The prompt of the first photo keeps 240 × 114,688 = 27.5 MB.

\[ n = N_{\text{img}} + n_{\text{q}} + n_{\text{tpl}},\qquad \text{cache} = 2\,N_{\text{layer}}\,n_{\text{kv}}\,d_{\text{head}}\,b\;n \]

  • \(N_{\text{img}}\) is the number of image tokens, \(n_{\text{q}}\) the tokens of the question and \(n_{\text{tpl}}\) the special tokens of the template. Their sum \(n\) is the prompt length.
  • \(N_{\text{layer}}\) is the number of layers (28), \(n_{\text{kv}}\) the number of key and value heads (8) and \(d_{\text{head}}\) the numbers per head (128). \(b\) is the bytes per number (2 at 16 bits). The factor 2 counts keys and values. This is the cache formula of D5, with the sizes of this model.

Look at the answer, then press the check that appears with the last step. The question has two halves. "Which shark is this" asks the model to look. "Is it protected" asks it to recall, because the photo does not say what the law says. The model answered both from the same weights. For the hammerhead photo it said "hammerhead shark", which is right. Then it named the great hammerhead, but the photo shows a scalloped hammerhead. It called the shark Vulnerable, and the IUCN lists both species as Critically Endangered. It named two US laws. NOAA Fisheries lists four populations of the scalloped hammerhead under the Endangered Species Act, so that claim is partly right for the shark in the photo. No US law decides what is allowed in Indian waters. The Marine Mammal Protection Act covers marine mammals, and sharks are fish, so that claim is wrong. A mix of right and wrong claims in a confident voice is the hallucination of D7.

"Protected" has more than one meaning. A species can be listed as threatened by the IUCN, which is a statement about the risk of extinction. It can be on a schedule of a national law, which is a statement about what people may do. For India, two ICAR-CMFRI posters on the Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2022, give two cases. I checked both entries in the Gazette text of the Act (Act 18 of 2022). The Ganges shark is on Schedule I (poster 65 of 2024). The scalloped hammerhead is in Appendix II of Schedule IV, the list of species whose international trade is regulated (poster 69 of 2025). The great hammerhead that the model named is on Schedule II, so a wrong species name also gives a wrong schedule. Schedules change, so check the current schedule before you rely on this. The design that follows for SharkCam is simple. Let the VLM do the looking, and answer the second half from a table of species and statuses kept by people, called as a tool (D7).

Common belief. If the model names the shark correctly, its other claims about that shark can be trusted.

Why it fails. The answer for the hammerhead photo is right about the family. It is wrong about the species and the IUCN category, and mixed about the law: one US law lists some scalloped hammerheads and the other does not cover sharks. Naming a shark uses the photo. The claims about status use what the model remembers about the name, and the photo cannot check them.

Better account. Split a question into the part that the photo decides and the part that needs a source, and check the second part against a source.

Debug tip. When answers sound plausible and ignore the photo, count the image tokens in the prompt. The count should equal (H′ / 32) × (W′ / 32) for the resized photo. A prompt with no image tokens, or with a photo that was resized to a few tokens, gives fluent answers from the question alone. Also try a plain grey picture: asked "Which shark is this?" with a grey picture, this model answers "great white", a guess from the words alone.

Quick quiz

A photo of 1,024 by 768 pixels goes to this model. How many image tokens does it take?

The sides 1,024 and 768 are multiples of 32 (32 × 32 and 32 × 24), so there is no resize. The tokens are 32 × 24 = 768.

Why does the photo take about as many tokens as a short paragraph, when it has 640 × 362 numbers per colour?

A token covers a block of 32 by 32 pixels (2 by 2 patches of 16 by 16). A photo of 640 by 352 has 20 × 11 = 220 such blocks. The vector of a token has 2,048 numbers, so the photo is still 220 × 2,048 = 450,560 numbers.

Two ways to give a model eyes

Guess first. A language model reads tokens, and a photo is not made of tokens. Name two ways to let the model use a photo. One changes the photo into something the model already reads. The other changes the model. Which one would be cheaper to train, and which one costs more tokens when the model runs?

LLaVA-style
image tokens are placed in the prompt and the language model reads them like words
Flamingo-style
new cross-attention layers are inserted into a frozen language model, and the text never contains image tokens
resampler
a small module that turns many patch vectors into a fixed small number of tokens (64 in Flamingo)
frozen
not updated during a training stage

LLaVA turns the photo into tokens and puts them in the prompt. Flamingo leaves the prompt alone and adds layers that read the photo by cross-attention.

LLaVA-style: image tokens in the prompt

Flamingo-style: cross-attention layers

Both designs start with a vision encoder. They differ in the connection and in what the language model does with it: read the photo as a part of its prompt, or consult it in extra layers.

LLaVA (Liu and others, 2023) uses the vision encoder of CLIP (E2) and a language model. One trainable matrix connects them. It maps a patch vector to a vector of the language model's width. LLaVA-1.5 (Liu and others, 2024) replaced the matrix with a two-layer MLP and raised the photo to 336 pixels, which gives 24 × 24 = 576 image tokens. Qwen3-VL, the model of this page, belongs to the same family. Its projector joins 2 by 2 patches first and then applies two layers. Its configuration also lists three extra projectors, and the technical report (Bai and others, 2025) describes DeepStack as combining features from several levels of the vision transformer. I did not study that part.

Flamingo (Alayrac and others, 2022) takes the other route. A frozen vision encoder feeds a resampler that outputs 64 visual tokens for any photo or video. A frozen language model has new cross-attention blocks inserted between its original blocks, one block in four for the 9 billion parameter model. They are the cross-attention of E3, with queries from the text and keys and values from the 64 tokens. A new block is multiplied by tanh of a learned number that starts at 0, so that at the start of training the model is the original language model, unchanged. In the text of an interleaved prompt, a word attends to the photo that came just before it. It does not attend to earlier photos.

\[ H_{\text{img}} = Z\,W + \mathbf{b},\qquad H_{\text{img}} = \mathrm{GELU}\!\left(Z\,W_1 + \mathbf{b}_1\right)W_2 + \mathbf{b}_2 \]

  • \(Z\) is the table of patch vectors from the encoder, one row per patch (or per joined block). \(H_{\text{img}}\) is the table of image tokens, one row per token, with the width of the language model. This is the matrix product of 0.5.
  • The first form is LLaVA's single matrix \(W\). The second is the two-layer form of LLaVA-1.5 and of Qwen3-VL's projector (GELU is the activation of B1).
this model:220 × 4,0962 by 2 patches joined×4,096 × 4,096first layer→ GELU ×4,096 × 2,048second layer=220 × 2,048image tokens

The count of numbers in this projector is the worked example. A layer norm on the 1,024 numbers of a patch has 2 × 1,024 = 2,048 numbers. The first layer has 4,096 × 4,096 + 4,096 = 16,781,312. The second has 4,096 × 2,048 + 2,048 = 8,390,656. The total is 25,174,016, the number the model reports for its projector. That is 1.2% of the 2,127,532,032 numbers of the whole model. A second case is LLaVA-1.5. Its patch vector has 1,024 numbers (the width of CLIP ViT-L). The same two-layer form maps 576 patches to 576 tokens. There is no joining, because a patch is one token.

Common belief. The projector translates the photo into words, so that the language model reads a description.

Why it fails. The test here uses the hammerhead photo. The nearest word vector to an image token has a cosine of 0.094 (median over the 220 tokens). For random directions the same number is 0.095. Between two words, the nearest neighbour has a cosine of 0.59. An image token is not close to any word. Its mean length is 22.4, and a word vector has a mean length of 1.45.

Better account. The projector is a learned code. It maps patches to vectors that the language model has learned to use, as if the model had been given a new kind of token. The LLaVA paper describes the first stage of training as making a visual tokeniser for the frozen language model.

Debug tip. If the code fails with a message about image features and image tokens that do not match, count them. The number of image placeholders in the text must equal the number of rows that the projector returns. A processor that was changed (a different maximum size, for example) while the model was not will show this at once.

Quick quiz

Which design makes the prompt longer, and which one adds layers to the language model?

The LLaVA style makes the prompt longer by the number of image tokens and adds no layers. The Flamingo style keeps the prompt as it is and adds cross-attention layers.

Why does a Flamingo block start with a factor of tanh(0) = 0?

The new block then adds no output at the start of training, so the model begins as the original language model. The cross-attention can then grow from zero, and the language ability of the frozen model is not disturbed at the start.

Align first, then instruction-tune

Guess first. The projector starts as random numbers, so the image tokens are noise to the language model. Suppose you trained the whole model on questions about photos from the first step. What could go wrong with the language model? Which parts would you train first, and with what data?

alignment
the first stage: teach the projector to make image tokens that the language model can use, with the rest frozen
visual instruction tuning
the second stage: train on questions and answers about photos (D7)
loss mask
the rule that only some tokens, here the answer, count in the loss (D7)

LLaVA is trained in two stages. First only the projector learns, on captions. Then the projector and the language model learn together on questions and answers.

One training example of this stage, drawn with the token counts of the hammerhead photo (the caption of stage 1 is a made-up example of 12 tokens)

Data sizes: LLaVA (Liu and others, 2023) and LLaVA-1.5 (Liu and others, 2024). The sizes of the three parts are those of Qwen3-VL-2B, counted from the model, with the LLaVA recipe applied as an illustration. Qwen's own recipe is longer and is in its technical report. The connector here is the projector and the three extra projectors.

In stage 1 about 5% of the numbers of a model of this size learn and the loss counts the caption only. In stage 2 about 86% learn, and the loss counts the answer. The vision encoder stays frozen in both stages.

Choose a recipe, then a stage. In stage 1 the vision encoder and the language model are frozen, and only the projector changes. It is trained on image and caption pairs: 595,000 pairs in LLaVA, filtered from a public caption set, and 558,000 in LLaVA-1.5. A pair is turned into a request to describe the image briefly and the caption as the reply. In stage 2 the projector and the language model learn, on 158,000 conversations, descriptions and reasoning questions in LLaVA, and on 665,000 examples in LLaVA-1.5. The encoder stays frozen in both stages. The LLaVA paper describes stage 1 as training a visual tokeniser for the frozen language model. This is the reason for the order. Suppose the projector were random and the language model free to change. The loss would push the language model to explain noise, and it could lose what it knows (B7). The paper argues the order and shows its results. This page has not run the comparison.

Flamingo uses one stage on a large mix of web text, photos and video. The encoder and the language model are frozen, and only the resampler and the cross-attention blocks are trained. It tested the tanh factor and the spacing of the blocks in ablations: removing the tanh factor cost 4.2% of the overall score. One block in four, instead of a block in all layers, made training 66% faster for a loss of 1.9%.

\[ L = -\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{t\in A}\ln p_\theta\!\left(y_t \mid \text{image},\ \text{question},\ y_{\lt t}\right) \]

  • \(A\) is the set of positions of the answer tokens (the loss mask of D7), \(y_t\) the token at position \(t\) and \(y_{\lt t}\) the answer tokens before it. \(p_\theta\) is the model's probability of a token (D3).
  • The image tokens, the question and the template are read but have no target, so they add no term to the sum. With the hammerhead example, the loss counts 196 of 436 tokens (45%) in stage 2, and 12 of 252 (4.8%) for a caption of 12 tokens in stage 1.

A worked number. Suppose that the model gives the right answer token a probability of 0.5 on average. A counted token costs \(-\ln 0.5 = 0.69\) nats, and the loss is 0.69 whether the answer has 12 tokens or 196. The 220 image tokens do not change the loss directly. They change the probabilities that the answer tokens get. That is how the projector learns: the gradient reaches it through the attention of the answer tokens to the image tokens. Where it breaks: a model trained only on short answers learns to give short answers, which is one reason why LLaVA-1.5 added data with short-form replies.

Common belief. To teach a model to see, show it photos with questions from the first step.

Why it fails. At the start the projector's output has no meaning for the language model, so a full update has to fit the language model to noise. The order of LLaVA (align, then instruction-tune) avoids this by training the new part alone first.

Better account. Train the new connection first with the old parts frozen, and release the language model afterwards. This is a recipe and has no guarantee. Qwen3-VL's own training has more stages.

Debug tip. If a trained model ignores the photo, give it a plain grey picture and compare the answers. If they are the same as with the actual photo, the model answers from the words alone. The grey test is also a baseline for the questions on a test set.

Quick quiz

In stage 1 of LLaVA, which parts are frozen?

The vision encoder and the language model. Only the projector learns.

The caption has 12 tokens and the image 220. How many tokens does the loss count in stage 1?

The 12 tokens of the caption. The image tokens and the request have no target and are not counted.

How many tokens a photo costs

Guess first. A phone photo is 4000 by 3000 pixels and a frame of the SharkCam dive is 640 by 362. Which design would you expect to give the larger count of image tokens: one that always resizes to a fixed size, or one that keeps the size of the photo? What is the price of a fixed size for a photo of a tag with small text?

dynamic resolution
a processor that keeps the photo's size and aspect ratio, so that a larger photo gives more tokens
fixed resolution
a processor that resizes any photo to one size, so that the count never changes
minimum and maximum pixels
the limits that keep a tiny photo from giving too few tokens and a huge photo too many

The number of image tokens is the photo's area divided by the area of a token. A model with fixed resolution always pays the same, and a model with dynamic resolution pays for the size.

Measured with the released processors of Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct (Apache-2.0), llava-hf/llava-1.5-7b-hf (licence listed as llama2), llava-hf/llava-v1.6-mistral-7b-hf (LLaVA-NeXT, Apache-2.0) and SmolVLM-256M-Instruct (Apache-2.0). The pictures were plain grey, since the count depends on the size only. The 4000 by 3000 point comes from the formula, and the Qwen3-VL processor gives the same 11,750 tokens.

For Qwen3-VL a 640 by 362 frame costs 220 tokens, a 1920 by 1080 frame 2,040 and a 3840 by 2160 frame 8,160. LLaVA-1.5 costs 576 for any photo. The formula of this page agrees with the processor at all 11 sizes that were measured.

Qwen3-VL cuts the photo into patches of 16 by 16 pixels and joins 2 by 2 patches into a token, so a token covers 32 by 32 pixels. The processor first rounds both sides to a multiple of 32, and halves go to the even multiple, as in Python's round. It then keeps the area between 65,536 pixels (256 by 256) and 16,777,216. A 112 by 112 thumbnail is enlarged to 256 by 256 and costs 64 tokens. A 3840 by 2160 frame is rounded to 3840 by 2176: 120 × 68 = 8,160 tokens. The LLaVA-1.5 processor scales the short side of any photo to 336 pixels and crops the middle 336 by 336. It cuts that into patches of 14 pixels: 24 × 24 = 576, which is fixed. LLaVA-NeXT uses a variable number of tiles, and its processor returned 1,176 to 2,928 tokens in these measurements. The SmolVLM-256M processor returned 832 to 1,088. The cost of a fixed size is detail: a photo of a tag gets the same number of tokens as a photo of a landscape. The next section measures what a small token budget does to small text.

\[ H' = 32\,\mathrm{round}\!\left(\tfrac{H}{32}\right),\quad W' = 32\,\mathrm{round}\!\left(\tfrac{W}{32}\right),\qquad N_{\text{img}} = \frac{H'}{32}\cdot\frac{W'}{32} \]

  • \(H, W\) are the height and width of the photo in pixels, \(H', W'\) the sizes after the resize (before the limits on the area), and \(N_{\text{img}}\) the number of image tokens. The 32 is the 16 pixels of a patch times the 2 patches joined on a side.
  • For a patch of side \(P\) and no joining this is the \(N = (S/P)^2\) of E1.

A worked line. For 640 by 362: 640 / 32 = 20, and 362 / 32 = 11.3, which rounds to 11, so \(H' = 352\). Then \(N_{\text{img}} = 11 \times 20 = 220\). For the dive: 2,400 frames at 220 tokens are 528,000 image tokens. That is more than 2 windows of 262,144 tokens, and at 114,688 bytes of cache per token it is 60.6 GB at 16 bits. The practical levers are the maximum number of pixels in the processor, the number of frames (take one in ten), and a crop around the animal. All three trade detail for tokens. Compare this with the 470,400 patch tokens of D6, where a frame of 224 pixels gave 196 patches.

import math
def smart_resize(h, w, factor=32, min_pixels=65536, max_pixels=16777216):
    r = lambda x: max(factor, round(x / factor) * factor)      # Python's round: halves go to the even number
    hh, ww = r(h), r(w)
    if hh * ww > max_pixels:
        beta = math.sqrt(h * w / max_pixels)
        hh, ww = math.floor(h / beta / factor) * factor, math.floor(w / beta / factor) * factor
    elif hh * ww < min_pixels:
        beta = math.sqrt(min_pixels / (h * w))
        hh, ww = math.ceil(h * beta / factor) * factor, math.ceil(w * beta / factor) * factor
    return hh, ww
def image_tokens(w, h):
    hh, ww = smart_resize(h, w)
    return (hh // 32) * (ww // 32)

            (800, 600): 475, (1024, 768): 768, (1280, 720): 880, (1920, 1080): 2040, (3840, 2160): 8160}
for (w, h), n in measured.items():
    print(w, h, image_tokens(w, h), n, image_tokens(w, h) == n)

The second column is what the function computes and the third what the processor returned. The 336 pixel case shows why the halves go to the even number. The value 336 / 32 = 10.5 rounds to 10 in Python and to 11 in a plain rounding. The processor gives 100 tokens, which is 10 × 10.

Common belief. More tokens always mean a better answer.

Why it fails. More tokens give the model more detail to read, and they also cost time and cache, and they dilute the question (the photo is already 22 times the question). The next section shows a case in which tokens matter a lot (small text) and a case in which they do not (the name of a shark).

Better account. Spend tokens where the detail is. Use a crop of the part of the photo that contains the information, and skip the larger photo.

Debug tip. If a model runs out of memory on a high-resolution photo, print the number of image tokens first. Halving the sides of the photo divides the tokens by 4. A maximum number of pixels in the processor fixes the problem without touching the model.

Quick quiz

How many image tokens does a 1280 by 720 frame give in Qwen3-VL?

The side 1280 gives 1280 / 32 = 40 and 720 gives 720 / 32 = 22.5, which rounds to 22 (the even number), so \(H' = 704\). The tokens are 22 × 40 = 880, as the processor returns.

Why is a fixed 576 tokens a problem for a photo of a tag with small text?

The processor brings the photo to 336 by 336 pixels whatever its size, and it crops the sides of a wide photo. A tag that took 20 pixels in the original may take 8, and the details of the letters are lost before the model reads anything.

Where it goes wrong

Guess first. Four things are known to trouble VLMs. They are counting objects, reading small text, saying things that are not in the photo, and photos that differ from the training photos, such as murky water. For a 2 billion number model on the shark photos, rank them from the one you expect to fail first. Then test your ranking in the figure.

probe
a small test built to find one kind of failure
greedy decoding
always writing the most probable token (D3); the answer is then the same at all runs
sampling
choosing the next token at random from the probabilities, with a temperature; answers differ from run to run
hallucination
a fluent claim that the photo or the model's training does not support (D7)

Small VLMs pass easy versions of the usual tests and fail when the test gets harder. The probes below show where the edge is for this model.

What the model got right in the easy versions of the tests

Counting was right for 1 to 8 copies in all 18 composite pictures. At 12 copies the model said 14, 12 and 12, at 16 it said 15 three times, and at 24 it said 16, 24 and 30. On 11 natural photos with 0 to 3 sharks it was right in all 11 photos.

Counting. On the natural photos (1 to 3 sharks, or none) the model gave the right number for all 11. To find the edge, the composites paste 1 to 24 copies of one shark on a white board. Up to 8 copies the count is right in 18 of 18 trials. From 12 copies it drifts: 14, 12, 12, then 15, 15, 15, then 16, 24, 30. This agrees with Rahmanzadehgervi and others (2024). They found that VLMs miss simple visual facts that people get right, and they report an average of 58% for four models on seven simple tasks. The edge depends on the picture. Small copies, overlap and clutter will pull it down.

Small text. The tag says "TAG 4127" and the photo is 640 pixels wide. At its own size, the answers for letter sizes em 3 to 7 (capital letters 2 to 5 pixels tall) are "10", "1014127", "4127", "4327" and "4127". That is two clear misses, one wrong digit and two right. From em 8 up (capital letters 6 pixels tall), all seven sizes were read right. At twice the size (920 tokens instead of 220) the only miss is the smallest tag, which read "4327". On the longer tag with several numbers the question asked for the weight. The weight "0.281" was read right at four of five sizes at 1× and came back as "283" at the smallest size. At 2× it was right at all five sizes. A token covers 32 by 32 pixels, and a letter 3 pixels tall is a small part of it, so the model sees a smudge and writes a likely number. Spending more tokens helped here, which is the trade of the section on cost.

Names and facts. Asked for the common name of the shark 8 times with sampling, the model said "hammerhead" 8 times for the hammerhead photo. For the grey bamboo shark it said "banded shark" 6 times, "dusky shark" once and "dogfish" once. For the spadenose shark it gave 7 different names in 8 tries, and for the milk shark 6. None of the 24 answers for these three photos names the species of the photo. Look at the pattern: a consistent answer ("banded shark" six times) can be wrong, and scattered answers show a guess. Asking several times and comparing is a cheap check. It finds guessing and cannot find a consistent mistake.

In two-sentence descriptions of all 14 photos, the model got the objects right: clownfish in an anemone, an eagle ray, a dolphin, two grey sharks on rock. It got the species names wrong. It called the spadenose shark "Squalus acanthias (commonly known as the blacktip shark)". The photo shows a spadenose shark, so both names are wrong. It called the Ganges shark drawing "Carcharhinus (Pseudodon) gansettius", a name I could not match to a species. The olive ridley turtle became a "green sea turtle". A yes or no test had 70 questions: is there a diver, a dolphin, a turtle, a ruler or a shark in the photo? Of these, 69 were right. The one miss was a shark that the model called absent. Three questions had a false premise. Asked "What colour is the diver's wetsuit?" on the hammerhead photo, it refused. Asked "What name is written on the tag?" on the bamboo shark photo, it said that there was no tag. Asked how many people are next to the turtle, it said one person was in the background, which matches a small figure far away in the photo. So the hallucination that appeared is in the knowledge about names and laws, as in the first section. Li and others (2023) found object hallucination to be common in large vision-language models. It was worse for objects that are frequent in the instruction data or that often appear together with the objects in the photo. A photo with a reef and a diver in the water would be a harder test than mine.

Murky water. At 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 m of the simple depth model of C3, the answer for the hammerhead photo stays "hammerhead". For the bamboo shark photo "banded shark" becomes "great white" from 15 m. For the spadenose photo it goes blacktip, banded, banded, barracuda, barracuda, great white. All 18 shark photos got "yes" to "Is there a shark in this photo?". All 16 photos without a shark got "no". Those are the turtle, dolphin, clownfish and ray at 0, 10, 20 and 30 m. So this model had no false alarms on 16 photos. The ResNet-18 of C3 and the CLIP model of E2 gave growing shark scores on the same kind of photos. The questions differ (a yes or no answer here, a score there), and 16 photos is a small test. Its species calls move with the water. Depth only changes colour and contrast in that model, and an actual change of water would also change the animals and the angle.

Common belief. A model that answers well on easy tests will answer well on the field photos.

Why it fails. The probes above have an edge: a count of 12, letters 4 pixels tall or less, a name that is not in the photo, a depth of 15 m. The easy versions of the tests lie before the edge, and a field photo can lie beyond it.

Better account. Find the edge for your photos with a probe like these, using sizes, counts and depths close to what SharkCam sees. Keep a check that does not need the model: a table, a second view or a person.

Debug tip. Keep a fixed set of probe photos and run them after any change of model, processor or prompt. Record the answers as well as a pass or fail. A change from "hammerhead" to "great white" at a depth of 15 m is visible in the answers and may be invisible in a score.

Quick quiz

Eight sampled answers to "Which shark is this?" are all "banded shark". Does this show the answer is right?

No. It shows that the model is consistent, and a consistent answer can still be wrong. For the bamboo shark photo it was consistent six times of eight and wrong.

Why did the 2× photo read a tag that the 1× photo missed?

At 2× the photo has 920 tokens, against 220 at 1×, so a letter covers more of a token's 32 by 32 pixel area in the model's input. More detail of the letters reaches the language model.

Adapting at test time

Guess first. The murky-water probe showed that the model's calls move with the water. The model has 2.1 billion numbers and no one labels the frames of a dive. Which few numbers would you let change while the model reads the dive, and what target would you give them?

test-time adaptation
changing a model a little while it is used, without labels (C3)
fast weights
weights that are updated while the model reads and start again from the trained values for the next input (D6)
chunk
the block of tokens that is read before one update (D6)
self-made target
a target that is built from the input itself, so that no label is needed

A VLM can adapt after deployment if a small set of its numbers is allowed to change while it reads, with a target built from the input. Which numbers, and how often, are design choices.

Frames of a dive, murkier along the way (the hammerhead photo through the depth model). Coral marks: the end of a chunk, where an update would happen.

Sizes are counted from Qwen3-VL-2B. The choice of five blocks (0, 6, 12, 18 and 24) applies the stride of the text-model study of D6 to a 28-block model as an illustration. No project made this choice. One frame is 220 tokens, as for a photo of 640 by 362 pixels.

Letting the down projections of five blocks change means 62,914,560 numbers, 3.0% of the model. A chunk of 4,096 tokens contains 18.6 frames, so a dive of 2,400 frames has 128 updates. These counts come from the design. The figure shows no results.

C3 re-estimated the statistics of batch normalisation on new frames, one way to adapt. A transformer has none to re-estimate: it uses layer norm or RMS norm, which compute their statistics per token (B6). The options in the figure are what remains. The scale numbers of the norm layers are 248,832 numbers in all. The projector has 100,714,496 with its three extra ones. The fast weights of D6 are the down projections of some MLP blocks (D5). This model has 12,582,912 numbers in a block, and five blocks if the stride is 6. The whole language model has 1,720,574,976. The slider changes the chunk and the dive. The strip shows where the updates fall, and the frames get murkier from left to right, as in C3's stream.

In the text-model version of the method, an update happens after a chunk is read. It adds to the fast weight an outer product of a target made from the chunk's own tokens and the layer's hidden patterns, so no label is needed. The fast weights go back to the trained values for the next input (D6). In a VLM the chunk contains image tokens as well as words. A chunk of 4,096 tokens is about 18 frames at 220 tokens, so a dive of 2,400 frames gives 128 updates. A question that decides the design is whether the target built from murky frames points in a useful direction. This page has no data on that question.

Bridge to my project. My project at MBZUAI, in Prof. Salman Khan's group, ports In-Place Test-Time Training from text models to a Qwen vision-language model, so that it can adapt to an input after deployment. In-Place TTT (Feng and others, from ByteDance Seed) treats the down projection of selected MLP blocks as fast weights. It updates them chunk by chunk as the model reads, with a target built from the input. Generation does not pause. My motivation is the failure of this section: recognising a shark when the water, light and animals change between dives. See my project page and my step-by-step primer. This page reports no result of the project. The figure above shows sizes and counts only.

Common belief. Test-time adaptation means retraining the model on new data after it is deployed.

Why it fails. There are no labels, and only a small set of numbers changes (3% in the figure). The change is a small step per chunk, and it is discarded when the input ends.

Better account. It is a small online update with a target made from the input. It has a risk: the update can move the model the wrong way, as C3 measured when re-estimating statistics on 14 photos made the shark confidence worse.

Debug tip. Run the no-shift check of C3 first. On clear-water frames, the answers with the update on and off should be the same. If they differ, the update itself is doing damage, and the murky-water result cannot be trusted.

Quick quiz

Why can C3's batch-norm trick not be copied directly to a VLM?

A transformer uses layer norm or RMS norm, which take their statistics from one token, so there are no stored averages over a batch to replace by new ones.

With chunks of 4,096 tokens and frames of 220 tokens, how many updates does a dive of 1,000 frames give?

The dive has 1,000 × 220 = 220,000 tokens, and 220,000 / 4,096 is 53.7, so 53 complete chunks and 53 updates.

Come back later

Answer after two days and again after a week, without looking back.

Name the two designs for joining a vision encoder to a language model, and say what they do with the photo's patch vectors.

LLaVA style: a projector maps the vectors to the width of the language model, and they enter the prompt as image tokens. Flamingo style: a resampler makes 64 tokens, and cross-attention layers inserted in the frozen language model read them (designs).

E3 showed that BLIP's cross-attention puts about three quarters of its weight on a few empty patches. What would you expect for the weights of the language model on image tokens in a LLaVA-style model, and how would you test it?

The same kind of parking is possible, since a softmax row must add up to 1 and the model has a first token to park on (D6). A test is to measure the weight of the answer tokens on the image tokens, and the value lengths, as in E3.

C3 and D6 both leave most of a model fixed during test-time adaptation. What does the figure of this chapter say about how much is allowed to change, and what must be checked first?

About 3.0% of the numbers, in the figure's main option. The no-shift check on clear frames must come first, to show the update does no harm when no shift has happened (C3, adapt).
