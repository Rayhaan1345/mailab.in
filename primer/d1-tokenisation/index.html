Part D · Chapter 1

Tokenisation

How text becomes the whole numbers a language model reads, and why some languages cost more

A language model never sees letters. It sees a list of whole numbers, one per token. This chapter builds a tokenizer from scratch with byte-pair encoding, one merge at a time. Then it looks at two tokenizers in current use. Under GPT-2's tokenizer, a short fisherman's report that takes 23 tokens in English takes 117 tokens in Hindi.

For SharkCam this matters because the messages arrive in four languages, and tokens set the cost in memory, time and money. All sixteen messages are invented. The Hindi, Marathi and Hinglish texts have not yet been checked by a native speaker.

You will need: dictionary lookup (0.2), dictionaries and tuples in Python (0.1) and a feel for Big-O (0.2).

Characters, words or pieces?

Guess first. A model must read the sentence “Two sharkcams filmed hammerheads at Sassoon Dock.” You can cut it into letters, into whole words, or into something in between. Which cut gives the shortest list, which needs the biggest dictionary, and which fails on a word it has never seen?

token
one unit of text that gets its own number
tokenizer
the program that cuts text into tokens and looks up their numbers
vocabulary
the list of all tokens a model knows
piece (subword)
a token that is a part of a word, or a whole common word
unknown token
a placeholder for text the vocabulary cannot write

Letters never fail but make long lists. Words make short lists but cannot read a new word. Pieces fall in between: short lists, no unknown words, and a vocabulary that has to be learned from text.

The first sentence is 49 character tokens, 8 word tokens with 3 of them unknown, and 12 GPT-2 pieces with none unknown. The word list here has only the 79 words and signs of the four English messages, so it is a small version of a full word list. The sentences are made up.

Switch between the three cuts for a sentence. Characters give the longest list, and a model pays for length. Attention, which D4 covers, compares all pairs of tokens, so a list five times longer costs about 25 times as much in that step (0.2). Words give the shortest list, but look at the second sentence. The number 4128 is not in the list, so a word-level model has no way to read it. Names, typos and new spellings arrive forever. Hinglish is written in Latin letters, and writers spell it in their own ways, so one word may appear as chhoti and choti.

Pieces solve both problems. GPT-2's pieces keep common words whole (“▁at”, “▁Dock”) and split rare ones into known parts (“▁shark” + “c” + “ams”). The mark ▁ means a space before the piece, because GPT-2 treats “▁shark” and “shark” as different tokens. The pieces are not chosen by hand. A short algorithm learns them from text, and the next section runs it.

Common belief. A tokenizer splits text into words.

Why it fails. Tokens are pieces of any size. The number 4128 became “▁4” and “128”. The word “sharkcamera” became “▁shark” and “camera”. A Devanagari letter can be cut into bytes.

Better account. A token is whatever piece the vocabulary contains. One word may be one token or several, and counting words predicts the token count badly.

Used in: the sequence length n that attention works on (D4), and the cost of long inputs (D6).

Quick quiz

A word-level model reads the report “Tag 4128”. What does it do with 4128?

If 4128 is not in its list, it replaces the number with the unknown token, so the model never learns which tag was meant. A piece-level tokenizer writes it as known pieces.

Why not simply use one token per character, always?

The vocabulary stays tiny, but the sequence gets long. Attention compares all pairs of tokens, so the cost grows with the square of the length.

Byte-pair encoding, one merge at a time

Guess first. Words start as rows of single letters. At a step you may glue one pair of neighbouring symbols into a new symbol, in all the places it occurs. The pair “ha” appears inside shark, sharks, sharkcam and hammerhead. Which pair would you glue first to make the corpus shorter, and when would you stop?

pair
two symbols next to one another inside a word
merge
replacing all occurrences of a pair by one new symbol, which joins the vocabulary
byte-pair encoding (BPE)
training a tokenizer by merging the most frequent pair, again and again
merge list
the merges in the order they were learned

Byte-pair encoding starts from single characters and merges the most frequent neighbouring pair, so common words become one token and rare words stay in pieces.

The corpus: made-up word counts, with the words cut into pieces

Pair counts: the coral bar is merged next

Five merges turn “shark” into one token. After 30 merges the corpus is 99 pieces long instead of 408, but “bamboo” is still in five pieces because it is rare. Press Step to do one merge, Play to watch them run, and Back to undo. The word counts are made up.

Read the figure in four steps. First, the words are cut into characters, with ▁ marking the space before it. Second, the plot counts all neighbouring pairs, weighted by how often its word occurs. Third, the most frequent pair is glued together in all words, and the new symbol joins the vocabulary. Fourth, the loop repeats. The dashed outlines in the corpus show where the next merge will happen. When two pairs tie, the one met first in the table wins. Training stops after a chosen number of merges. In this tiny corpus all words are single pieces after 44 merges, so the vocabulary would only be the word list. A production tokenizer stops much earlier, at a size chosen in advance.

The first merge is “h” + “a” with a count of 29, and the working is the equation box. Its terms are the words that contain “ha”: shark 10, sharks 7, sharkcam 5, hammerhead 4 and hammerheads 3. Take a second example by hand. The pair “e” + “r” is merged eighth with a count of 9. It occurs once in hammerhead (4), once in hammerheads (3) and once in diver (2), and \(4 + 3 + 2 = 9\). Pair counts change as merges happen, because a merge removes old pairs and creates new ones. That is why the plot is redrawn after a merge.

\[ \mathrm{count}(a,b) \;=\; \sum_{w} f(w)\; c_w(a,b) \qquad\qquad (a^{*}, b^{*}) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{(a,b)} \; \mathrm{count}(a,b) \]

  • \(w\) is a distinct word, \(f(w)\) is how often it occurs in the corpus, and \(c_w(a,b)\) is how many times the symbol \(a\) is directly followed by \(b\) inside \(w\).
  • \((a^{*}, b^{*})\) is the pair with the largest count. It becomes the new symbol \(a^{*}b^{*}\).
  • The vocabulary size is the alphabet size plus the number of merges. Here \(43 + 44 = 87\).

Why does counting words with their frequencies work? Scanning ten copies of “shark” finds the same pair ten times, so counting the word once and multiplying by 10 gives the same total, much faster. Practical implementations do this. The method is greedy: it never looks ahead. The pieces “▁sha” and “▁shar” were useful stepping stones, but after “▁shark” forms they no longer appear in any word. They stay in the vocabulary anyway. The method began as a way to compress data (Gage, 1994). Sennrich, Haddow and Birch (2016) brought it to machine translation to cope with rare words. The same algorithm trains GPT-2's tokenizer, with bytes as the alphabet (256 byte symbols, 50,000 merges and one special token make its 50,257 entries).

Analogy. Text-message shorthand. People shorten the phrases they type most, such as “lol”, so the common phrases take few symbols.

Where it breaks. People choose shorthand by meaning. BPE chooses by counts alone, so a piece such as “▁hammerh” can appear because it was frequent. Its meaning plays no part. Pieces often look like word parts, as with the plural “s”, only because word parts repeat.

Debug tip. If two runs on the same text give different vocabularies, check how ties are broken. Production trainers fix a rule (here: first seen). A tie broken at random would give a different tokenizer from one run to the next, and a model works only with the tokenizer it was trained with.

from collections import Counter

words = {"shark": 10, "sharks": 7, "hammerhead": 4, "dock": 5, "docks": 3}   # made-up counts
corpus = {w: ["▁"] + list(w) for w in words}   # start from characters; ▁ marks the space before a word

def count_pairs(corpus, words):
    pairs = Counter()
    for w, pieces in corpus.items():
        for a, b in zip(pieces, pieces[1:]):
            pairs[(a, b)] += words[w]            # a word counts as often as it occurs
    return pairs

def merge(pieces, pair):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(pieces):
        if i + 1 < len(pieces) and (pieces[i], pieces[i + 1]) == pair:
            out.append(pieces[i] + pieces[i + 1]); i += 2      # glue the pair into one piece
        else:
            out.append(pieces[i]); i += 1
    return out

for step in range(1, 4):
    pairs = count_pairs(corpus, words)
    best, n = pairs.most_common(1)[0]
    corpus = {w: merge(p, best) for w, p in corpus.items()}
    print(f"merge {step}: {best[0]} + {best[1]} -> {best[0] + best[1]}  ({n} times)")
print(corpus["sharks"], corpus["hammerhead"])

Used in: all modern language models. The next sections use the merges to cut new text (3) and give the pieces their numbers (4).

Quick quiz

After the merge “h” + “a”, is the pair “s” + “h” still in “shark”?

No. The row is now “▁ s ha r k”, so the pairs are “▁s”, “s ha”, “ha r” and “r k”. A merge removes old pairs and makes new ones.

Does training need the labels or meanings of words?

No. It only counts neighbouring pairs in raw text, so it needs no labels.

Using the merges on new text

Guess first. You trained 44 merges. Someone now types “hammerheads at the dock”. Does the tokenizer need the training corpus, or only the merge list? What will it do with “at”, which never appeared in training?

encode
turn text into tokens, using the merge list
pre-tokenise
split text into words and signs first, so merges never join two words
byte fallback
writing a character that is not in the alphabet as its UTF-8 bytes
<unk>
the unknown token, used when no fallback exists

To tokenise new text, split it into words, break the words into characters, then apply the merges in the order they were learned.

With no merges the text “hammerheads at the dock” takes 24 tokens. With all 44 merges it takes 8. The small number under a token is its ID, the subject of the next section. The merges come from the trainer above, so the slider and the trainer stay in step.

Slide the merges from 0 to 44 and watch the text shrink. Words from the training corpus collapse quickly. “Hammerheads” is one token once its merges are done. “At” and “the” never appeared in training, so they stay as letters. Only the merge “▁” + “t” helps, so “the” becomes “▁t”, “h” and “e”. A tokenizer trained on SharkCam notes would cut ordinary English into many pieces, in the same way GPT-2's tokenizer, trained on mostly English text, cuts Devanagari (section 7). Now try the Hindi preset. With byte fallback, a Devanagari letter becomes three byte tokens and no information is lost. With <unk>, all letters become the same placeholder, and the model cannot tell them apart.

The merges must be applied in the order they were learned. Take the two merges “▁” + “s” and then “▁s” + “ha”. The second needs the first. Applying them out of order would never produce “▁sha”. Most tokenizers in use also split text into words with a pattern before merging, as the figure does. A merge then never joins the end of one word to the start of the next.

Common belief. A tokenizer looks up words in a dictionary of words.

Why it fails. The dictionary contains pieces. A word it has never seen is built from pieces by the merge list, so no word is ever missing. Only the pieces are looked up.

Better account. The merge list is a recipe for cutting text. The vocabulary, covered next, gives the pieces their numbers.

Debug tip. A stray space changes the tokens. In GPT-2, “▁shark” is token 21027 but “shark” without a space is two tokens, 1477 and 668. When a prompt behaves oddly, print the tokens, not the text. A model trained with one tokenizer also cannot read the IDs of another, so always load the tokenizer that belongs to the model.

Used in: any call to a language model. A prompt is charged by its number of tokens (section 7).

Quick quiz

Why does “the” stay as three tokens in the toy tokenizer?

No merge ever joined those letters, because the word “the” is not in the training corpus. The merge list only knows the pairs it counted.

Does byte fallback lose information?

No. Any character can be written as bytes, so the text can be rebuilt. It only costs more tokens.

Vocabulary and IDs

Guess first. A model works on numbers. GPT-2's vocabulary has 50,257 tokens. If you stored it as a list and scanned it to find a token's number, about how many comparisons would one lookup need on average? What would a dictionary need?

ID
the whole number that names a token, its position in the vocabulary
vocabulary
a dictionary from token to ID, plus the list from ID back to token
decode
turn IDs back into text

A vocabulary is a dictionary from token to ID and a list from ID back to token, so a lookup takes one step.

The toy vocabulary, with the merges chosen above (click a row)

A list scan needs one comparison per position it passes, so a token with ID 307 costs 308 comparisons. A dictionary needs one step. The vocabulary changes with the merge slider above, so IDs of merged tokens depend on how many merges you did.

The layout follows common practice. IDs 0 to 3 are special tokens (section 5 explains them). Then come 256 byte tokens for the fallback, then the alphabet, then the merges in the order they were learned. To encode, the tokenizer looks up the tokens in the dictionary. To decode, it uses the IDs as positions in the list and joins the pieces. This is the dictionary lookup of 0.2. Python's dict finds a key in about one comparison however large it is, while a list needs about half its length.

(50,257 + 1) ÷ 2average list scan=25,129comparisons per token×1,000tokens in a text=25,129,000comparisonsvs1,000with a dictionary

\[ \text{list scan: } \frac{V+1}{2} \text{ comparisons on average} \qquad \text{dictionary: } \approx 1 \qquad \text{ID} \to \text{token: } 1 \]

  • \(V\) is the vocabulary size. For GPT-2, \(V = 50{,}257\).
  • A sorted list with binary search (0.2) would need at most \(\lfloor\log_2 V\rfloor + 1 = 16\) comparisons. A dictionary still wins, and needs no sorting.

Here are some GPT-2 IDs, found with the tokenizer: “▁shark” is 21027, “▁sharks” is 27476, “▁Dock” is 36444, “▁bamboo” is 37252 and “▁the” is 262. Common words got small IDs because their merges were learned early. The IDs have no meaning by themselves: 21027 is not close in meaning to 21028. The model turns an ID into a list of learned numbers, an embedding, which is the next chapter (D2).

# a toy vocabulary: token -> ID
vocab = {"<pad>": 0, "<unk>": 1, "<bos>": 2, "<eos>": 3, "▁shark": 4, "s": 5, "▁dock": 6, "▁at": 7}
inverse = {i: t for t, i in vocab.items()}          # ID -> token, to read the model's answer

tokens = ["<bos>", "▁shark", "s", "▁at", "▁dock", "<eos>"]
ids = [vocab.get(t, vocab["<unk>"]) for t in tokens]  # unknown pieces become <unk>
print(ids)
print("".join(inverse[i] for i in ids if i > 3).replace("▁", " "))
print(vocab.get("▁hammerhead", vocab["<unk>"]))

Common belief. Token IDs have meaning, so nearby IDs are similar words.

Why it fails. An ID is only a position in a list that came from training order. “▁shark” (21027) and “▁sharks” (27476) are far apart.

Better account. An ID is a name. Meaning is added later by the embedding table, which gives all IDs learned vectors.

Used in: the embedding table has one row per ID (D2), and the model's output has one score per ID (D3).

Quick quiz

Why is decoding (ID to token) even cheaper than encoding?

An ID is already a position in a list, so reading the token is one index step, with no hashing.

What is the unknown token ID for, if byte fallback exists?

Tokenizers without byte fallback need it for any character outside the alphabet. Byte-level tokenizers such as GPT-2's do not need it for text.

Special tokens

Guess first. Four reports are processed together, as one rectangle of numbers. They have 27, 179, 193 and 44 tokens. What must be added so that the rows have the same length, and how can the model tell the filler from the text?

special token
a vocabulary entry that marks structure instead of standing for text
padding (<pad>)
filler that makes short rows as long as the longest one
start and end (<bos>, <eos>)
tokens that mark where a text begins and ends
chat tokens
tokens that mark who is speaking in a conversation

Special tokens are vocabulary entries that mark structure: where text starts and ends, which slots are padding and who is speaking.

Padding the young-hammerhead reports to the longest one wastes 42% of the slots under GPT-2, because the English report is 27 tokens and the Marathi report is 193. The reports are the invented messages m03, m07, m11 and m15, counted with the two tokenizers.

Switch the tokenizer to Qwen2.5 and the padding share falls, because its tokens for Hindi and Marathi are fewer. A model reads a batch as a rectangle, so all rows must have the same length. The short rows get <pad> tokens. A mask tells attention to skip them (D4). Compute spent on padding is wasted, so training code often groups reports of similar length into the same batch.

Other special tokens give the text edges. GPT-2 has just one, <|endoftext|> with ID 50256. It marks both the start and the end of a document. Qwen2.5's tokenizer has this token (151643). It also has <|im_start|> (151644) and <|im_end|> (151645), which mark the beginning and end of a turn in a chat, together with a role such as “user”. A model learns to emit the end token when a reply is finished, and generation stops there (D3).

Common belief. Padding is harmless, because it is only filler.

Why it fails. A pad slot is an ordinary token with an ID. If attention is not told to skip it, the model reads it as part of the text, and short reports get different results when they are batched with long ones.

Better account. Padding is safe only together with a mask. It also costs compute, so batch similar lengths together.

Debug tip. If a model never stops talking, check that the end token is added to the training text and that generation stops on it. If results change when you batch, check the padding mask first.

Used in: attention masks (D4), the generation loop (D3) and chat assistants (D7).

Quick quiz

Four rows have 10, 20, 30 and 40 tokens. How many slots are padding if the batch is padded to 40?

Slots total 4 × 40 = 160. Message tokens total 100. Padding is 60 slots, or 37.5%.

Bytes, characters and scripts

Guess first. The letter “s” takes one byte in a computer file. How many bytes does the Devanagari letter “श” (sha) take?

Unicode
a table that gives all characters a number, its code point
UTF-8
the usual way to store code points as one to four bytes
byte
a number from 0 to 255
byte-level BPE
BPE whose alphabet is the 256 byte values, so any text can be tokenised

In UTF-8 an English letter takes one byte and a Devanagari letter takes three. A byte-level tokenizer therefore starts from a text two to three times longer for Hindi and Marathi.

Message m01 in English is 170 characters and 170 bytes. The Hindi message m05 is 77 characters but 193 bytes, and the Marathi message m09 is 74 characters and 194 bytes. Digits, spaces and the sign “।” also appear in those counts, so the average is 2.5 bytes per character, a little under 3.

Press the preset buttons to see the difference. The code point of “s” is U+0073, below 128, so one byte is enough. Devanagari lives at U+0900 to U+097F, in the range that UTF-8 stores with three bytes. A tokenizer built on bytes, as GPT-2's is, therefore starts from a long row of bytes and must learn merges to bring it back to a reasonable length. GPT-2 learned many merges for English and few for Devanagari, because its training text was mostly English. Radford and colleagues chose bytes so that the model can write any string, with no unknown token. That choice is why the Hindi preset in section 1 was cut into hex pieces such as E0 A4. These are the first two bytes of a letter, and they are not a letter.

Common belief. A character is one byte, so the length of a text in characters is also its length in bytes.

Why it fails. That is true for English letters. Message m05 has 77 characters and 193 bytes.

Better account. A character can take one to four bytes in UTF-8. A byte-level tokenizer starts from the bytes, so count them with len(text.encode("utf-8")).

for text in ["shark", "शार्क"]:
    b = text.encode("utf-8")
    print(f"{text}: {len(text)} characters, {len(b)} bytes")
    print("   bytes in hex:", b.hex(" "))

Quick quiz

The Marathi report m09 has 74 characters and 194 bytes. If a byte-level tokenizer had no merges for Devanagari, how many tokens would it need?

It would need 194, one per byte. GPT-2 used 118, so its merges joined some bytes, but only about 1.6 bytes per token.

Why some languages cost more

Guess first. A fisherman's report in English takes 23 tokens. The same report in Hindi, cut by GPT-2's tokenizer, takes about how many: 25, 50 or 100? And in Hinglish, which is Hindi written in Latin letters?

fertility
tokens per word, a measure of how finely a language is cut
cost ratio \(\rho\)
tokens of a text divided by tokens of its English meaning
context window
the most tokens a model can read at once (GPT-2: 1,024; D3)

Under GPT-2's tokenizer the Hindi and Marathi reports take about five times the tokens of their English meaning, and the Hinglish reports about one and a third times.

For the reports on Tag 4127, GPT-2 uses 40 tokens in English, 227 in Hindi, 236 in Marathi and 50 in Hinglish. Qwen2.5 cuts Hindi to 146 and Marathi to 154. Coral hatched pieces are parts of a letter, shown in hex. Counts were computed offline with the two tokenizers.

Check before reuse. The Hindi, Marathi and Hinglish texts were written for this primer and have not yet been checked by a native speaker. The English meaning of a message is given in the data file (tokens.json) and is the baseline for the cost ratio. The counts illustrate how two tokenizers cut these texts. They do not measure how fishers write.

Compare the four rows for one topic, then press the other tokenizer button. The two tokenizers are open ones: GPT-2 (OpenAI, MIT licence) and Qwen2.5 (Alibaba Cloud, Apache-2.0 licence for the 0.5B model used here). Both use byte-level BPE. Qwen2.5 has 151,643 ordinary tokens (Qwen Team, 2024). Four causes add up. First, the merges were learned from text that was mostly English, so English words became single tokens and Devanagari words did not. Second, a Devanagari letter is three bytes (section 6), so even a letter without merges starts with three tokens. Third, Hinglish is written in Latin letters, and writers spell it in their own ways, so words such as lagbhag and chhoti are cut into three pieces. Fourth, Marathi joins endings onto words. The report m11 writes वर्सोव्याजवळ (“near Versova”) as one word. The tokenizer has seen few long words like it, so it cuts it into many pieces.

The working below the rows follows your click on a row. For the Hindi report m06, the box shows 227 tokens against 45 for its English meaning, a cost ratio of \(\rho = 5.0\). Its fertility is 7.1 tokens per word. In a window of 1,024 tokens, the Hindi report fits 4 times and the English one 22 times. A second worked case is m09: 118 tokens against 27 gives \(\rho = 4.4\), and Qwen2.5 brings the same message to \(72 / 31 = 2.3\).

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{n_{\text{text}}}{n_{\text{English meaning}}} \qquad\qquad \text{fertility} \;=\; \frac{n_{\text{tokens}}}{n_{\text{words}}} \]

  • \(n_{\text{text}}\) is the number of tokens of the message in its own language and \(n_{\text{English meaning}}\) is the number for its English meaning.
  • \(\rho = 1\) means the language is as cheap as English. The values in the chart are for this tokenizer and these twelve short messages only.

Why does it matter? A model reads at most a fixed number of tokens at once, so a Hindi report uses up the window five times faster. Commercial services charge by the token. Ahia and colleagues (2023) tested 22 languages and showed that the same content costs very different amounts in OpenAI's API. Petrov and colleagues (2023) found gaps of up to about 15 times between languages, even with tokenizers meant for many languages. The fix is a tokenizer trained on balanced text, which Qwen2.5 partly shows: its Hindi ratio is about 3.1 against 5.5. Qwen2.5 also writes the digits one by one, so its English counts are a little higher than GPT-2's. A larger vocabulary costs more, because the embedding table gets a row per token (D2).

Common belief. A token is about four letters, or three quarters of a word.

Why it fails. That rule is a measured average for English. In the four English messages GPT-2 averages 4.1 characters per token. For the Hindi and Marathi messages it is between 0.6 and 0.7 characters per token, and for Hinglish 2.8.

Better account. The number of tokens depends on the language, the script and the tokenizer. Count with the tokenizer of the model you will call.

Debug tip. If a prompt in Hindi or Marathi is cut off or costs more than you planned, count its tokens with the model's own tokenizer before anything else. An estimate from words can be off by a factor of five.

Used in: the context window (D3) and the loss per token (D3), where the same Hindi and Marathi reports look surprisingly easy. This chapter explains why.

Quick quiz

The Hinglish report m14 takes 50 tokens, and its English meaning takes 45. What is \(\rho\), and what does it say?

\(\rho = 50/45 = 1.1\). Hinglish costs about 10% more than English for this report, far less than Hindi in its own script (5.0 for m06).

Give two reasons a Marathi report takes more tokens than the English one.

The tokenizer learned few Devanagari merges from English-heavy text, and a Devanagari letter starts as three bytes. Long words with attached endings are cut into many pieces.

Why is a tokenizer's vocabulary a dictionary and not a plain list? (Chapter 0.2)

A dictionary finds a key in about one step however large it is. A list scan needs about half its length, which is 25,000 comparisons for 50,257 tokens.

A Hindi report needs 5 times as many tokens as the English one. By how much does attention cost grow for that report? (Chapter 0.2)

Attention cost grows with the square of the length, so about 25 times.

A model with a 50,257-token vocabulary starts training by giving all tokens the same probability. What loss does it start with, and why? (Chapter 0.7)

The loss is \(-\ln(1/50{,}257) = \ln 50{,}257 \approx 10.82\). Equal probabilities give the true token a probability of \(1/V\).
