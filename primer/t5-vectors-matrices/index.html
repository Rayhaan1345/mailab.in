Vectors and matrices

Dot products, grids that bend, and directions that only stretch

A shark is a list of measurements, and a list of measurements is a vector. This chapter shows how to compare vectors with the dot product and cosine similarity. It shows how a matrix turns one vector into another, and how to read a matrix as a bending of the plane.

These ideas are the working parts of AI. A neuron is a dot product and a layer is a matrix. Attention is a table of dot products, and a weight update is an outer product. The curvature of a loss is described by eigenvectors.

You will need: the arrays and @ from chapter 0.1, and composition from chapter 0.3 for the matrix product.

Vectors as lists of measurements

Guess first. Tag 4127, the juvenile bamboo shark from a Juhu rock pool, measures 44.0 cm, weighs 0.281 kg and has a girth of 12.4 cm. Tag 5519, the young hammerhead, measures 82.0 cm, 2.610 kg and 27.6 cm. If you write a shark as a list of three numbers, what would the difference of the two lists mean, and what would "twice Tag 4127" mean?

vector
an ordered list of numbers, such as the measurements of one shark
component
one number in the list
scalar
a single number, used to scale a vector
length
\(\|\mathbf{x}\|\), the distance from the origin to the tip of the arrow

A vector is a list of measurements that you can add, scale and measure, and with two components you can draw it as an arrow.

Tag 4127 is the vector \(\mathbf{a} = (44.0,\ 0.281,\ 12.4)\), with components for length in cm, weight in kg and girth in cm. These are made-up catch records, not field data. Tag 5519 is \(\mathbf{b} = (82.0,\ 2.610,\ 27.6)\). Subtract component by component and you get \(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a} = (38.0,\ 2.329,\ 15.2)\): Tag 5519 is 38.0 cm longer, 2.329 kg heavier and 15.2 cm wider. Multiply all components by 2 and you get \(2\mathbf{a} = (88.0,\ 0.562,\ 24.8)\). This is only arithmetic. A shark twice as long weighs about 8 times as much (0.3), so the doubled list is not a likely shark.

Subtraction also gives a distance: the distance between two sharks is the length of their difference. Tag 3305 (41.0 cm, 0.228 kg, girth 11.5 cm) minus Tag 4127 is \((-3.0,\ -0.053,\ -0.9)\). The squares are 9, 0.0028 and 0.81, which add up to 9.8128, and the root is 3.13. For Tag 5519 the squares are 1,444, 5.42 and 231.04, the sum is 1,680.5 and the root is 40.99. So Tag 4127 lies far closer to the Sassoon spadenose than to the young hammerhead. The weight differences hardly count, because kilograms squared are tiny next to centimetres squared.

Two components are easy to draw, so the figure uses arrows in a plane. Drag the tips of the navy arrow \(\mathbf{u}\) and the coral arrow \(\mathbf{v}\). Pick what to see with the chips.

Adding vectors places one arrow's tail on the other's tip, scaling stretches an arrow along its own line, and length comes from Pythagoras.

The sum \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) is the arrow from the origin to the far corner of the parallelogram. The scaled vector \(c\,\mathbf{u}\) keeps the line of \(\mathbf{u}\) and flips direction if \(c\) is negative. The length of \((u_1,u_2)\) is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides \(u_1\) and \(u_2\). In three or more components the same rule applies: square, add, take the root. It works because Pythagoras applies twice. The first two parts give the diagonal of a floor, and that diagonal with the third part gives the diagonal of the box.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} &= (u_1+v_1,\ u_2+v_2) \\ c\,\mathbf{u} &= (c\,u_1,\ c\,u_2) \\ \|\mathbf{u}\| &= \sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\) are vectors with components \(u_1,u_2\) and \(v_1,v_2\). Bold letters mark vectors, as in A1.
  • \(c\) is a scalar, an ordinary number.
  • \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\) is the length of \(\mathbf{u}\).

Analogy. A vector is an arrow, and adding two vectors is walking along one arrow and then the other.

Where it breaks. A plane has the same units along both directions. A shark's list mixes centimetres and kilograms, so a step along one component is not comparable with a step along another. Adding and scaling lists is valid arithmetic, but the result need not be a possible shark, as \(2\mathbf{a}\) above shows.

Debug tip. Length treats all components alike, so a feature with big numbers dominates it. The length of the vector of Tag 4127 is 45.7, almost all from the 44.0 cm. If features use different units, scale them first (standardising, 0.7) before you compare vectors.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([44.0, 0.281, 12.4])    # Tag 4127: length cm, weight kg, girth cm
B = np.array([82.0, 2.610, 27.6])    # Tag 5519 (made-up catch records)
C = np.array([41.0, 0.228, 11.5])    # Tag 3305
print(B - A)
print(2 * A)
print(round(np.linalg.norm(A), 2))
print(round(np.linalg.norm(C - A), 2), round(np.linalg.norm(B - A), 2))

In practice. A row of data in AI is a vector with many more than three components. A GPT-2 small embedding has 768 (D2). The arithmetic is the same, and np.linalg.norm(a - b) gives a distance in one line.

Used in: a model input is a vector (A1); a word becomes a vector (D2); a gradient is a vector (0.6).

What is the length of the vector (3, 4)?

\(\sqrt{9+16} = 5\).

If \(\mathbf{u}=(2,1)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(1,3)\), what is \(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\) and what is \(3\mathbf{u}\)?

Why does the weight in kilograms hardly change the distance between two sharks?

Its differences are small numbers, such as 0.053 or 2.329, and their squares are tiny next to the squares of differences in centimetres. A feature with small numbers has little say until you rescale the features.

The dot product: a weighted sum

Guess first. A made-up score is \(0.05\times\text{length} + 0.1\times\text{weight} + 0.1\times\text{girth} - 6\). Tag 4127 (44.0 cm, 0.281 kg, girth 12.4 cm) and Tag 5519 (82.0 cm, 2.610 kg, girth 27.6 cm) differ in all three measurements. Which of the three terms adds the most to the difference between their scores?

dot product
multiply matching components and add the results: \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}\)
weights
the numbers \(w_j\) that say how much a feature counts
score
\(z = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b\), a weighted sum plus a bias \(b\)

The dot product multiplies matching components and adds them up, so it is a weighted sum in which a feature counts in proportion to its weight.

Made-up catch records, not field data. Tag 3305 is the Sassoon spadenose.

A feature's push on the score is its weight times its value, so a small weight on a big number can outweigh a big weight on a small one.

The answer to the guess: length adds \(0.05\times 38.0 = 1.9\), girth adds \(0.1\times15.2=1.52\), and weight adds \(0.1\times 2.329 = 0.23\). Length wins by a small margin because its numbers are large, although its weight of 0.05 is the smallest of the three. Choose a shark with the chips, then change the weights. The table on the right scores all three sharks at once. That is a matrix times a vector, which you meet two sections from now.

This formula is what a neuron computes before it applies a bend (B1), and what linear regression predicts with (A2). The same sum, with the query as \(\mathbf{w}\) and a key as \(\mathbf{x}\), scores how well two words match in attention (D4).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} &= w_1x_1+\dots+w_dx_d = \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_j x_j \\ z &= \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathbf{w}\) is the weight vector and \(\mathbf{x}\) the feature vector, both with \(d\) components.
  • \(b\) is the bias, a number added to the sum.
  • \(z\) is the score. It is the same \(z\) as in the sigmoid of chapter 0.3.

Here is the second shark. Tag 3305 is \((41.0,\ 0.228,\ 11.5)\), so the products are \(0.05\times41.0 = 2.05\), \(0.1\times0.228 = 0.0228\) and \(0.1\times11.5 = 1.15\). They add up to 3.2228, and the bias of \(-6\) brings the score to \(-2.777\). A gap between two scores needs no scoring of either shark. Take the difference vector \((38.0,\ 2.329,\ 15.2)\) and dot it with the weights: \(1.9 + 0.2329 + 1.52 = 3.6529\). This equals \(1.121-(-2.532)\), the gap between Tag 5519 and Tag 4127. The bias cancels in a gap.

This works because a weighted sum is linear: doubling an input doubles its push. It is also the limit, since the score can never respond to a feature in a curved way. That is why a neuron adds a bend after the sum (B1).

Common belief. The feature with the largest weight matters most to the score.

Why it fails. A feature pushes the score by its weight times its value. In the example, length has the smallest weight (0.05) and still adds the most, because its values are large.

Better account. Compare the products, or put the features on one scale first, as 0.7 shows, before you read a weight as importance.

import numpy as np

X = np.array([[44.0, 0.281, 12.4],     # Tag 4127
              [41.0, 0.228, 11.5],     # Tag 3305
              [82.0, 2.610, 27.6]])    # Tag 5519, one shark per row
w = np.array([0.05, 0.1, 0.1])
b = -6
print(round(X[0] @ w + b, 3))    # one shark
print(np.round(X @ w + b, 3))    # all three at once
print(round(w @ (X[2] - X[0]), 4))        # gap between Tag 5519 and Tag 4127
print(np.round(X @ w + (-3), 3))    # bias -3: all scores shift
-2.532
[-2.532 -2.777  1.121]
3.6529
[0.468 0.223 4.121]

In practice. Libraries score a whole table with one product, X @ w + b, where the rows of X are the examples. A common mistake is to leave out the bias, which forces a score of 0 when all features are 0.

Used in: a neuron (B1); a linear model (A2); attention scores (D4).

Quick quiz

Compute \((1,2,3)\cdot(4,0,-1)\).

\(1\cdot4 + 2\cdot0 + 3\cdot(-1) = 1\).

Why do two vectors need the same number of components for a dot product?

A component of one vector is multiplied by the matching component of the other. A spare component has no partner.

The bias changes from \(-6\) to \(-3\). What happens to the three scores?

All three rise by 3, to 0.468, 0.223 and 4.121. The gaps between the scores do not change.

Length, angle and cosine similarity

Guess first. Two arrows point the same way, but one is three times as long. What happens to the dot product, and what happens to the angle between them?

angle
\(\theta\), the angle between two arrows drawn from the same point
cosine similarity
\(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\), from 1 (same direction) to \(-1\) (opposite)
perpendicular
at a right angle; the dot product is 0
projection
the shadow of one arrow on the line of the other

The dot product grows with the lengths and with how well the arrows line up, and cosine similarity keeps only the lining up.

Dragging the tip of an arrow outward along its own line multiplies the dot product and leaves the cosine unchanged.

The thick sand segment along \(\mathbf{v}\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{u}\): the part of \(\mathbf{u}\) that points along \(\mathbf{v}\). The dot product equals the length of that shadow times the length of \(\mathbf{v}\). So the dot product is large when the arrows are long and close in direction, zero when they are perpendicular, and negative when they point apart.

Cosine similarity divides the lengths out. It is 1 for the same direction, 0 for perpendicular arrows and \(-1\) for opposite ones. Two sharks of different sizes but the same proportions have a cosine close to 1. Systems that compare meanings, such as word embeddings (D2) and image-text models (E2), use cosine similarity for this reason.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} &= \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta \\ \cos\theta &= \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), from 0 to 180 degrees.
  • \(\cos\theta\) is the cosine of that angle. It is the dot product after both lengths are divided out.

The formula comes from two ways to write the squared third side of a triangle. Draw both arrows from one point and join their tips. The law of cosines gives \(\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta\). Writing the squared length out by components gives \(\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\). The last terms must agree, so \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta\).

A second case with numbers. Take \(\mathbf{u}=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(4,3)\). The dot product is \(12+12=24\) and both lengths are 5, so the cosine is \(24/25 = 0.96\) and the angle is \(\arccos 0.96 = 16.3\) degrees. Double \(\mathbf{v}\) to \((8,6)\). The dot product becomes 48 and \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\) becomes 10, so the cosine is \(48/50 = 0.96\) again.

Common belief. A bigger dot product means the two vectors are more similar.

Why it fails. The dot product also grows with length. A long arrow in a so-so direction can beat a short arrow in the very same direction as the query.

Better account. Use the cosine to compare directions, and the dot product when the size should count too.

Debug tip. A vector of length 0 has no direction, so its cosine divides by zero and gives nan. Rounding can also push a cosine to 1.0000000002, which makes np.arccos fail. Use np.clip(c, -1, 1) before taking the angle. The cosine of raw shark measurements is close to 1 for almost any pair, because the large centimetre numbers dominate: standardise first.

import numpy as np

def cosine(u, v):
    return u @ v / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v))

u = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
v = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
print(u @ v, round(cosine(u, v), 4))
print((3 * u) @ v, round(cosine(3 * u, v), 4))
print(cosine(u, np.array([-1.0, 2.0])))
A = np.array([44.0, 0.281, 12.4]); B = np.array([82.0, 2.610, 27.6])
print(round(cosine(A, B), 4), round(cosine(A, 2 * A), 4))

# standardising removes the unit problem, but all three measures grow with size
mu = np.array([53.98, 1.185, 16.31])     # means of the 600 made-up records
sd = np.array([22.77, 4.20, 8.16])       # standard deviations
print(round(cosine((A - mu) / sd, (B - mu) / sd), 2))

In practice. Scale vectors to length 1 first, and the cosine is just the dot product, so one matrix product gives all pairs. Standardising removes the unit problem and leaves the size problem. Length, weight and girth grow together. The standardised vectors of Tag 4127 and Tag 5519 point nearly opposite ways (cosine \(-0.99\)), because one shark lies below the average and the other above.

Quick quiz

What is the cosine similarity of \((1,0)\) and \((0,5)\)?

The dot product is 0, so the cosine is 0. The arrows are perpendicular.

The dot product of \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) doubles when you double \(\mathbf{u}\). What happens to the cosine?

It stays the same. Doubling \(\mathbf{u}\) doubles both the dot product and \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\), and the doubling cancels.

The dot product of two arrows is 0. What is the angle between them, and must both arrows be short?

The angle is 90 degrees, unless one arrow has length 0. The arrows can have any lengths. A dot product of 0 says that no part of one arrow points along the other.

A matrix is a layer of neurons

Guess first. Three made-up catch records are listed in a table, one shark per row. One scoring recipe is a vector of 3 weights. Applying the recipe to the whole table gives how many numbers: 1, 3 or 9?

matrix
a rectangular table of numbers with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, an \(m\times n\) matrix
matrix-vector product
\(A\mathbf{x}\): the dot product of a row of \(A\) with \(\mathbf{x}\), for all rows
layer
a matrix whose rows are the weights of several neurons that read the same input
column
a vertical list in the matrix; \(A\mathbf{x}\) mixes the columns

A matrix times a vector gives one dot product per row, and the same answer is a weighted mix of the matrix's columns.

The answer to the guess is 3: one score per shark. A table with one shark per row is a matrix \(X\), here \(3\times3\). A recipe vector \(\mathbf{w}\) gives \(X\mathbf{w}\), three scores at once. Reading the other way, a matrix \(W\) whose rows are the weights of several neurons turns one input \(\mathbf{x}\) into one number per neuron. That is a layer: \(W\mathbf{x}\), with a bias \(\mathbf{b}\) added (B1, B2).

The figure uses a \(2\times2\) matrix so that you can see both views. On the left, the rows are two neurons, and both read the inputs \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). On the right, the columns are two arrows. The output \(A\mathbf{x}\) is \(x_1\) times the first column plus \(x_2\) times the second column.

The outputs of A x are dot products of the rows with x, and the whole output is x1 times column 1 plus x2 times column 2.

Set \(x_1\) to 0 and the output lies along column 2. Set both to 1 and the output is the sum of the columns. The row view is how a computer does the arithmetic. The column view is how you see where the matrix sends a point, and the next section builds on it.

\[ \begin{aligned} A\mathbf{x} &= \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a x_1 + b x_2 \\ c x_1 + d x_2 \end{pmatrix} \\ &= x_1\begin{pmatrix} a \\ c \end{pmatrix} + x_2\begin{pmatrix} b \\ d \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(A\) is a matrix with entries \(a,b,c,d\), and \(\mathbf{x}\) a vector with components \(x_1,x_2\).
  • The first answer line reads rows. The last reads columns. They are equal.

Now a layer for the sharks, with two neurons that read length, weight and girth. Neuron 1 is the recipe of the last section, with bias \(-6\). Neuron 2 reads length and weight only, with weights 0.02 and 0.5 and bias \(-1\). Together the rows form \(W\). For Tag 4127, neuron 2 gives \(0.02\times44.0 + 0.5\times0.281 - 1 = 0.0205\), so \(W\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} = (-2.532,\ 0.0205)\). The code sends all three sharks through at once, using the transpose W.T (see the section on products).

Analogy. A layer is a row of neurons, and the rows of the matrix are their recipes.

Where it breaks. A matrix alone is only the weighted sums. A neuron also adds a bias and then applies a bend such as a sigmoid (0.3). A matrix on its own can only stretch, turn and shear the plane, so it cannot make a curve.

Debug tip. A matrix with \(n\) columns needs an input with \(n\) components. NumPy raises a "matmul: Input operand ... mismatch" error otherwise. Print the sizes of both and check that the number of columns of A equals the number of components of x. Remember that * multiplies entry by entry and @ is the matrix product (0.1).

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 3.0]])
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
print(A @ x)                                  # rows: dot products
print(x[0] * A[:, 0] + x[1] * A[:, 1])        # columns: weighted mix
print(A.shape, x.shape)                       # sizes to check

A layer has 4 neurons and reads 3 inputs. What are the size of its weight matrix and the size of its output?

The matrix is \(4\times3\), one row per neuron. The output has 4 numbers.

A matrix bends the plane

Guess first. A matrix stretches the plane by 2 in the x direction and by 3 in the y direction. By what factor does the area of any region grow?

linear transformation
a map that keeps the origin fixed and keeps grid lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced
basis arrows
the arrows \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) along the grid; the columns of \(A\) are where they end up
determinant
\(\det A = ad - bc\), the factor by which areas scale, negative if the plane is flipped
identity matrix
\(I\), the matrix that leaves the plane as it is: 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere

The columns of a matrix show where the two grid arrows end up, and the determinant is the factor by which the unit square's area changes.

The determinant is the signed area of the transformed unit square: it is 0 when the grid collapses onto a line and negative when the plane is flipped.

The answer to the guess is \(2\times3=6\). The matrix with entries 2 and 3 on the diagonal sends the unit square to a rectangle of area 6, and \(\det = 2\cdot3 - 0\cdot0 = 6\).

The navy grid shows where the grid lines go. The coral arrow is where \((1,0)\) ends up, which is the first column. The teal arrow is where \((0,1)\) ends up, the second column. Here is the determinant at work on the matrix of the code below, \(M = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0.5&1.5\end{pmatrix}\). The corners of the unit square go to \((0,0)\), \((2,0.5)\), \((3,2)\) and \((1,1.5)\). Walk round them and add \(x_iy_{i+1} - x_{i+1}y_i\) for the step from one corner to the next: \(0\), \(2\cdot2 - 3\cdot0.5 = 2.5\), \(3\cdot1.5 - 1\cdot2 = 2.5\) and \(0\). The total is 5, and the area is half of it, 2.5. This equals \(\det M = 2\cdot1.5 - 1\cdot0.5\).

\[ \begin{aligned} A\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix}a\\c\end{pmatrix}, & A\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix}b\\d\end{pmatrix} \\ \det A &= ad - bc \end{aligned} \]

  • \(a,b,c,d\) are the entries of \(A\). The first column is \((a,c)\) and the second is \((b,d)\).
  • \(\det A\) is the signed area of the parallelogram with sides \((a,c)\) and \((b,d)\).

Why \(ad-bc\)? Take the parallelogram with sides \((a,c)\) and \((b,d)\), all four numbers positive. It fits inside a rectangle of area \((a+b)(c+d) = ac+ad+bc+bd\). Cutting away two triangles of area \(ac/2\), two of area \(bd/2\) and two rectangles of area \(bc\) removes \(ac+bd+2bc\), and \(ad-bc\) is left. Moves also multiply: if one move scales areas by 2 and the next by 3, together they scale by 6. In symbols, \(\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B\).

Common belief. A matrix can bend straight lines into curves.

Why it fails. A matrix keeps lines straight and parallel lines parallel, and the origin stays put. The grid in the figure never curves.

Better account. Curving needs a non-linear function, such as the sigmoid of chapter 0.3. Putting one between layers is the idea of B2.

Debug tip. The columns of a matrix, not its rows, are the images of the basis arrows. In NumPy the first column is A[:, 0]. Mixing rows and columns up gives the transpose, a different matrix.

M = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],

square = np.array([[0, 1, 1, 0],

Matrix products and transposes

Guess first. \(A\) is a \(2\times3\) matrix and \(B\) is a \(3\times4\) matrix. Can you form \(AB\)? What is its size? Can you form \(BA\)?

matrix product
\(AB\): entry \((i,j)\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\)
matching dimensions
the number of columns of \(A\) must equal the number of rows of \(B\)
transpose
\(A^\top\): swap rows and columns, so entry \((i,j)\) moves to \((j,i)\)

A product \(AB\) exists only when the columns of \(A\) match the rows of \(B\), and swapping the order can change the answer or make the product impossible.

The size rule is (m by n) times (n by p) gives (m by p), and the product of two square matrices usually changes when you swap them.

The answer to the guess: \(AB\) exists and is \(2\times4\), because the inner sizes 3 and 3 match. \(BA\) does not exist, because 4 and 2 differ. Use the sliders to set sizes. When the inner sizes differ the figure says so. Click an entry of the result to see which row and column make it, and the sum of products in the boxes.

A product is also a chain of transformations. \(AB\mathbf{x}\) means "apply \(B\) to \(\mathbf{x}\), then apply \(A\)", which is composition (0.3). Order matters for the same reason it does there: rotating then stretching differs from stretching then rotating.

The transpose appears when you need all dot products between two lists of vectors. If the rows of \(Q\) and the rows of \(K\) are vectors, then \(QK^\top\) contains the dot product of any row of \(Q\) with any row of \(K\) (D4).

A worked product. Let \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(B=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&0\\1&4\end{pmatrix}\), which are \(2\times3\) and \(3\times2\). Entry \((1,1)\) of \(AB\) pairs row 1 of \(A\) with column 1 of \(B\): \(1\cdot3 + 0\cdot2 + 2\cdot1 = 5\). Entry \((1,2)\) is \(1\cdot1 + 0\cdot0 + 2\cdot4 = 9\). Entry \((2,1)\) is \(0\cdot3 + 1\cdot2 + 1\cdot1 = 3\). Entry \((2,2)\) is \(0\cdot1 + 1\cdot0 + 1\cdot4 = 4\). So \(AB = \begin{pmatrix}5&9\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\), and its transpose \(\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\9&4\end{pmatrix}\) is also \(B^\top A^\top\).

\[ \begin{aligned} (AB)_{ij} &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik}B_{kj} \\ (AB)^\top &= B^\top A^\top \end{aligned} \]

  • \(A_{ik}\) is the entry in row \(i\), column \(k\) of \(A\).
  • \(n\) is the shared size: the columns of \(A\) and the rows of \(B\).
  • \(A^\top\) is the transpose. The order reverses when you transpose a product.

The chain view shows why a network needs more than matrices. Let layer 1 be \(W_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&2&1\end{pmatrix}\) and layer 2 be \(W_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}\), with no bend between them. For \(\mathbf{x}=(1,2,3)\), \(W_1\mathbf{x} = (3,7)\) and \(W_2\) turns that into \(3-7 = -4\). The single matrix \(W_2W_1 = (1,\ -1,\ -1)\) gives \(1-2-3 = -4\) directly. Two layers with no bend between them are one layer, and a bend such as ReLU stops the collapse (B2).

Common belief. Matrices multiply like numbers, so \(AB = BA\).

Why it fails. With the sizes in the guess, \(AB\) exists and \(BA\) does not. Even when both exist, the two products usually differ, as the \(P\) and \(Q\) example below shows.

Better account. Treat \(AB\) as "do \(B\) first, then \(A\)" and keep the order. Grouping is free, so \((AB)C = A(BC)\), but swapping is not.

The outer product

Guess first. A column \(\mathbf{u}\) has 3 numbers and a row \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) has 4. You form all the products \(u_i\times v_j\) and write them in a table. How many entries does the table have, and what pattern do you expect in it?

outer product
\(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top\): the table of all products \(u_iv_j\)
rank one
a matrix whose rows are all multiples of a single row
dot versus outer
the dot product of two vectors is a number, the outer product is a table

The outer product of a column and a row is a table whose rows are all multiples of the same row, so \(m\times n\) entries are set by only \(m+n\) numbers.

A table of 12 numbers is fixed by only 7: three in u and four in v, and a zero in u gives a row of zeros.

The answer to the guess: 12 entries, and the rows are the row \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) multiplied by the numbers of \(\mathbf{u}\), one number per row. Move the sliders and watch the pattern. Rows scale together, and if one number in \(\mathbf{u}\) is 0 its whole row is 0. Teal cells are positive and coral cells are negative, and the numbers are printed in the cells.

Outer products matter because of how learning changes a weight matrix. For one example, the change that a layer's weights need is the outer product of the error at the layer's output and the input to the layer (B4). Choose "add it to a weight matrix" to see one such update spread over all twelve weights at once.

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top)_{ij} &= u_i\,v_j \\ W &\leftarrow W + \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathbf{u}\) is a column with \(m\) entries and \(\mathbf{v}\) a column with \(n\) entries. \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) is the same numbers as a row.
  • \(W\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix of weights. The second formula adds the outer product to it.

A worked update for the two-neuron layer of the section on matrices, whose weights \(W\) are the rows \((0.05,\ 0.1,\ 0.1)\) and \((0.02,\ 0.5,\ 0)\). The error at the neurons is \(\boldsymbol\delta = (0.5,\ -0.2)\) and the input is \(\mathbf{x} = (1.0,\ 0.5,\ -1.0)\). The outer product \(\boldsymbol\delta\mathbf{x}^\top\) has the rows \(0.5\,\mathbf{x}^\top = (0.5,\ 0.25,\ -0.5)\) and \(-0.2\,\mathbf{x}^\top = (-0.2,\ -0.1,\ 0.2)\). With a step size of 0.1, the update subtracts a tenth of this table from \(W\). The first weight of neuron 1 falls from 0.05 to 0, and the third weight of neuron 2 falls from 0 to \(-0.02\).

Several examples add their tables. Entry \((j,k)\) of the sum is \(\sum_i \delta_{ij}x_{ik}\). That is entry \((j,k)\) of \(D^\top X\), when the rows of \(D\) are the errors and the rows of \(X\) are the inputs. So a sum of outer products over a batch is one matrix product, and the code checks it.

Analogy. An outer product is a multiplication table: the table for 1 to 9 against 1 to 9 is the outer product of the same two lists.

Where it breaks. A general matrix is not a multiplication table. Most matrices are not rank one, and need a sum of several outer products to build them.

Identity and inverse

Guess first. A matrix bends the grid. Is there always another matrix that straightens it again? Think about a matrix that squashes the whole plane onto one line.

identity
\(I\): \(I\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}\) for any \(\mathbf{x}\)
inverse
\(A^{-1}\): the matrix that undoes \(A\), so \(A^{-1}A=I\)
singular
a matrix with no inverse, which happens when \(\det A=0\)

The inverse undoes a matrix, and it exists only when the determinant is not zero.

Bending the grid by A and then by its inverse restores the grid, and the inverse disappears when the determinant reaches 0.

Slide the stage from 0 to 1 and the grid bends by \(A\). Slide it on to 2 and the inverse \(A^{-1}\) straightens it, so the whole move equals the identity. Now choose the "squash" preset. The determinant is 0, the grid collapses onto a line, and many points end up on the same spot. No matrix can send one spot back to many places, so there is no inverse.

This is the matrix version of the inverse function from chapter 0.3. For a \(2\times2\) matrix there is a short formula. Swap \(a\) and \(d\), negate \(b\) and \(c\), and divide by the determinant. The division shows why \(\det A=0\) breaks it.

\[ \begin{aligned} A^{-1} &= \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} \\ A^{-1}A &= AA^{-1} = I \\ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y} &\iff \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(ad-bc\) is the determinant of \(A\). It must not be 0.
  • \(I\) is the identity matrix. Multiplying by it has no effect.
  • The last statement says that you can solve \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}\) by applying the inverse.

The formula works because the product comes out as the identity. Multiply \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\) by \(\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}\) and the diagonal entries are \(ad-bc\), while the others are \(-ab+ba = 0\) and \(cd-dc = 0\). Dividing by \(ad-bc\) turns the diagonal into 1.

A worked solve. Take \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{y}=(5,10)\). The determinant is \(2\cdot3-1\cdot1 = 5\), so \(A^{-1} = \tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{y} = (0.6\cdot5 - 0.2\cdot10,\ -0.2\cdot5 + 0.4\cdot10) = (1,\ 3)\). Check: \(2\cdot1+1\cdot3 = 5\) and \(1\cdot1+3\cdot3 = 10\).

Near the edge the inverse exists and still misleads. Take \(N=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&2.002\end{pmatrix}\), whose determinant is 0.004. With \(\mathbf{y}=(3,\ 6.002)\) the solution is \((1,\ 1)\). Change 6.002 to 6.004, a change of 0.03 percent, and the solution becomes \((0.5,\ 2)\). The inverse divides by 0.004, so it magnifies small errors in \(\mathbf{y}\).

Common belief. All square matrices have an inverse.

Why it fails. A matrix with determinant 0 squashes the plane onto a line or a point. Different inputs share an output, as with \(x^2\) in chapter 0.3, so the output cannot tell you which input went in.

Better account. A square matrix has an inverse if and only if its determinant is not 0.

Eigenvectors: directions that only stretch

Guess first. A matrix bends and turns most arrows. Could any arrow come out lying along its original line, only longer or shorter? How many such directions could a \(2\times2\) matrix have?

eigenvector
a direction \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\): the matrix only stretches it
eigenvalue
\(\lambda\), the stretch factor of an eigenvector; negative means flipped
symmetric matrix
a matrix equal to its transpose, such as \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\)

An eigenvector is a direction on which a matrix acts as a plain stretch, and the eigenvalue is the stretch factor.

Only the directions of the eigenvectors stay on their line, a symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvectors, and a turn has none.

Spin the test arrow with the angle slider. The navy arrow is \(\mathbf{v}\), the coral arrow is \(A\mathbf{v}\). For most angles the coral arrow points elsewhere. At a few angles the two lie on one line, and the boxes show \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) with numbers. Those angles are the eigenvectors, drawn as sand lines. Try the presets. The "symmetric" matrix has two perpendicular directions. The "shear" has one. The "rotate" matrix has none, because it turns all arrows.

The answer to the guess: a \(2\times2\) matrix has at most two such directions, and sometimes none. Eigenvalues come from a short equation. The two eigenvalues add up to \(a+d\) (the trace) and multiply to \(\det A\).

\[ \begin{aligned} A\mathbf{v} &= \lambda\mathbf{v} \\ \lambda^2-(a+d)\,\lambda+(ad-bc) &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector and \(\lambda\) its eigenvalue.
  • \(a+d\) is the trace and \(ad-bc\) the determinant. The quadratic gives up to two eigenvalues that are ordinary numbers.

Where does the quadratic come from? Rewrite \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) as \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\). A nonzero \(\mathbf{v}\) can solve this only if \(A-\lambda I\) squashes the plane, so its determinant is 0: \((a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc=0\), which expands to the quadratic.

A second case, with a matrix that is not symmetric: \(N=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\). The trace is 7 and the determinant is 10, so \(\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0\) and \(\lambda=5\) or \(2\). For \(\lambda=5\), the rows of \(N-5I\) are \((-1,\ 1)\) and \((2,\ -2)\), so \(v_2=v_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(1,1)\). Check: \(N(1,1)=(5,5)\). For \(\lambda=2\), both rows are \((2,\ 1)\), so \(v_2=-2v_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(1,-2)\). Check: \(N(1,-2)=(2,-4)\). These eigenvectors are not perpendicular, since \(1\cdot1+1\cdot(-2)=-1\). Symmetric matrices give perpendicular ones.

Why this matters in AI. The curvature of a loss near its lowest point is described by a symmetric matrix. Its eigenvectors are the sharpest and flattest directions, and its eigenvalues say how sharp (0.6, B5). The Fisher information, which marks the weights that a trained model depends on most, is also a symmetric matrix of this kind (B7).

Common belief. All matrices have directions that they only stretch.

Why it fails. A rotation turns all directions, so no eigenvector exists in the plane. The eigenvalues of a turn are complex numbers.

Better account. Symmetric matrices always have eigenvectors in the plane, and they are perpendicular, and these are the matrices that curvature and the Fisher information give.

Eigenvectors also say what repeated multiplication does. Apply \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) again and again to \((1,0)\). The results are \((2,1)\), \((5,4)\), \((14,13)\) and \((41,40)\). The start is half of \((1,1)\) plus half of \((1,-1)\). One step multiplies the first part by 3 and the second by 1, so after \(k\) steps the arrow is \(\tfrac12 3^k(1,1) + \tfrac12(1,-1)\), and it turns towards \((1,1)\).

In practice. A signal that passes through many layers is multiplied by a matrix again and again. Eigenvalues larger than 1 in size make it grow and smaller ones make it fade, which is one view of exploding and vanishing gradients (B4).   2. theory Let \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{x}=(1,2)\). Compute \(A\mathbf{x}\) in two ways, by rows and by columns. Then find \(\det A\) and \(A^{-1}\).

    Hint

    For the inverse, swap \(a\) and \(d\), negate \(b\) and \(c\), and divide by the determinant.

  3. theory \(A\) is \(4\times3\), \(B\) is \(3\times2\), \(C\) is \(2\times5\). Which of \(AB\), \(BA\), \(ABC\), \(CB\) are defined, and what are their sizes? What is the size of \((AB)^\top\)?

  4. theory Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) by hand. Check that the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant.

    Hint

    Solve \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=0\) for both values of \(\lambda\).

Practice

  1. practice A query is \(\mathbf{q}=(3,1)\). Three stored vectors are \((6,2)\), \((1,3)\) and \((10,10)\). Rank them by dot product and by cosine similarity with NumPy. Do the rankings agree? Explain the difference.

  2. practice Apply a shear \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), a flip \(\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) and a squash \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\) to the corners of the unit square. For these three, print the determinant and compute the area of the new figure with the shoelace formula. Do they agree?

  3. practice Use np.linalg.eigh on \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), then on \(\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Verify \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) for all four pairs. Then build \(\mathrm{np.outer}(u,v)\) for two vectors of your choice and check that its rank is 1, and that the sum of two such outer products usually has rank 2.

Think deeper

  1. open The cosine similarity of Tag 4127 and Tag 5519 is 0.9985, although they differ a lot in size. Why is it so close to 1? How would you change the data so that cosine similarity compares body proportions? How would you check that your fix works?

  2. open A \(2\times2\) matrix has determinant 0.01. A measurement error of 1 percent in \(\mathbf{y}\) changes the solution of \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}\) a lot. Design a small experiment that shows this, and say what you would report to a colleague who uses this matrix.

  3. open An image has three colour numbers (red, green, blue) for a pixel. A grey value is the weighted sum \(0.3R+0.6G+0.1B\). Write this as a matrix with one row. Can you recover the colour from the grey value? What does this say about what a layer with fewer outputs than inputs keeps and loses?

Come back later

A neuron computes a sigmoid of a weighted sum. Which part is a dot product, which part is a composition, and what is a layer of such neurons in matrix terms?

The weighted sum \(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\) is the dot product. Feeding it into the sigmoid is a composition (0.3). A layer is a matrix whose rows are the neurons' weights, followed by the sigmoid on all the outputs.

A sequence has 1,000 items and you take the dot product of all items with all other items. How many dot products is that, and what is the size of the table you get from \(QK^\top\)?

\(1000\times1000 = 1{,}000{,}000\) ordered pairs, a \(1000\times1000\) table (0.4).

The inverse of a function and the inverse of a matrix: what do they have in common, and when does the inverse fail to exist?

Both undo the original. A function has an inverse only if different inputs give different outputs, and a matrix has one only if its determinant is not 0 (0.3).
