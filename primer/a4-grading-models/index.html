Grading a model fairly

How to score a model so that the score means something.

A model that scores well on the records it learned from has shown only that it can remember them. This chapter shows how to measure what matters for SharkCam: how the model does on sharks it has never seen. You will split the records into training, validation and test sets, average over folds, and spot the leaks that make a score look better than it is.

Then you will learn the vocabulary for grading a yes-or-no model: the confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1, the threshold, the ROC curve and AUC. The last sections ask when these numbers mislead. A class may be rare (the Ganges shark). The base rate may change (a screening clinic). A model may work better for one group than another.

You will need: the ideas of a model and a baseline from A1 and the logistic regression of A3, which supplies the scores graded here. From the toolkit: base rates, confidence intervals and reading a confusion matrix.

Overfitting and underfitting

Guess first. Fourteen spadenose sharks give you fourteen points of length and weight. A curve with enough bends can pass close to all fourteen. Will it predict the weight of the next shark better than a plain cubic curve, about the same, or worse?

training error
how far the model's predictions fall from the records it was fitted to
test error
the same measure on records the model never saw
underfitting
the model is too stiff for the data, so both errors are high
overfitting
the model follows the noise, so training error is low and test error is high
degree \(M\)
the highest power of length in a polynomial: 1 is a line, 2 a parabola, 3 a cubic

A more flexible model always fits its training records at least as well. How well it fits new records first improves and then gets worse.

With 14 training sharks, the test error is lowest at degree 3 (0.039 kg). At degree 9 the training error is still falling, to 0.023 kg, while the test error has grown to about 92 kg. The records are made-up, generated from published length-weight relationships; they are not field data.

The navy dots are the 14 training sharks. The coral dots are the other spadenose records in the made-up catch table, and the curve never saw them. Slide the degree up from 1. At degree 1 the line cannot follow the bend in the data, so both errors are high. That is underfitting. Around degree 3 the curve follows the trend and the two errors are close. At high degrees the curve bends to pass near the navy dots, and it swings far from the coral dots between them and beyond the ends.

Training error cannot rise as the degree goes up. A higher degree includes all the lower ones: set the extra weights to zero and you have the lower curve back. A model with more weights has more ways to match the training points, including the random scatter in the sharks' weights. That scatter does not repeat in new sharks. Test error measures how well the curve captured the part that does repeat.

Now raise the training sample size. With 60 sharks even degree 9 behaves, because there are enough points to pin the curve down. More data lets a model be more flexible before it overfits. Press "New sample" to see that the wild curve changes with the sample, while the straight line barely moves. A curve that changes that much with the sample cannot be trusted on new sharks. Weight grows like length cubed (A2), so a cubic is a natural choice here. You will rarely know the right flexibility in advance, which is why you need a way to measure it.

91.6test RMSE at degree 9, kg÷0.0234training RMSE at degree 9, kg≈3,900times larger on sharks it has not seen

\[ \hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 L + w_2 L^2 + \cdots + w_M L^M \qquad \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)}\bigr)^2} \]

  • \(L\) is the length in cm and \(\hat{y}\) the predicted weight in kg. \(w_0, \dots, w_M\) are the weights the fit chooses, and \(M\) is the degree.
  • RMSE is the square root of the mean squared error (A2), so it is in kg. \(m\) is the number of records it is computed on: the 14 training sharks for training error, and the other spadenose records for test error.

Common belief. The model with the lowest training error is the best model.

Why it fails. Training error never rises when you add flexibility, so it always favours the most flexible model, even when the extra flexibility only fits noise.

Better account. Judge a model on records it did not train on. Keep the model with the lowest error there.

Debug tip. Compare the two errors before you compare models. A low training error with a test error several times larger means overfitting: use more data, fewer weights or a simpler model. Both errors high and close together means underfitting: add a feature or allow more flexibility. B8 turns this into a full diagnosis with learning curves.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("catch.csv")            # the made-up catch table
sp = df[df.species == "spadenose"].reset_index(drop=True)
L, W = sp.length_cm.values, sp.weight_kg.values

# shuffle the 268 records: 14 to train on, the rest to test
idx = np.random.RandomState(2).permutation(len(sp))
tr, te = idx[:14], idx[14:]

def rmse(a, b):
    return np.sqrt(np.mean((a - b) ** 2))

for M in [1, 3, 9]:
    p = np.polynomial.Polynomial.fit(L[tr], W[tr], M)
    e_train = rmse(p(L[tr]), W[tr])
    e_test = rmse(p(L[te]), W[te])
    print(f"degree {M}:  train {e_train:.3f}   test {e_test:.3f}")

Quick quiz

The training error is 0.02 kg and the test error is 0.30 kg. Is the model over- or underfitting, and what could you try?

Overfitting, because the test error is 15 times the training error. Try more training records, a lower degree, or fewer features.

Why can the training error not rise when you go from degree 3 to degree 4?

The degree-4 family contains all the degree-3 curves (set \(w_4 = 0\)). The best degree-4 fit is therefore at least as good on the training records.

Training, validation and test sets

Guess first. You train four models on the 600 catch records and want to keep the best one. Would you score them on the records they learned from? If not, on which records? And which records should give the final number you report?

training set
the records the model learns from; its weights are fitted to them
validation set
records used to compare models and settings, such as the degree in the last section
test set
records locked away until the end and used once, for the number you report
shuffle and seed
put the records in random order before cutting them, with a fixed seed so you can repeat the cut (0.1)
stratified split
a split that gives the three parts about the same share of any one species

The three sets have different jobs. The training set teaches, the validation set chooses, and the test set grades once.

Shuffle again and the scores change. The records in the test set differ, so the score differs. It is an average over a small group, and sampling noise applies to it: a test accuracy near 80% on 120 records has a standard error of about 3.7 points. Switch to the 80/10/10 split and the wobble grows, because the test set shrinks to 60 records. Switch to 50/25/25 and the wobble shrinks, but the models train on fewer records. A split trades noise in the score against data for training.

Now look at the Ganges count in the three headers. There are only three Ganges records. A plain shuffle often leaves the test set with none, and sometimes puts all three in training. Switch to "Stratified by species" to deal the species out in proportion. With only three Ganges sharks even that cannot always give all three parts a share. At 50/25/25 the parts get one apiece. At 60/20/20 the test set can still get none, because a fifth of three is less than one.

\[ \mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\,(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \qquad \hat{p} \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE} \]

  • \(\hat{p}\) is the share of test records the model got right and \(n\) the number of test records. This is the standard error of a share from 0.7.
  • About 95% of test scores from sets like this one fall within 1.96 SE of the model's long-run accuracy. The figure shows the interval for the current split.

Common belief. If I use the test score to choose between two models, it is still a test score.

Why it fails. The choice now depends on the test set. The winner is the model that happened to do best on those records, so its score on them is a little too high. The more models you try, the larger the effect.

Better account. Choose with the validation set (or with cross-validation, next). Open the test set once, for the model you chose, and report that number.

Debug tip. Shuffle before you split. If the table is sorted by date, by site or by species, a plain cut puts one kind of record in the test set. Fix the seed so the same split can be rebuilt. And decide the split before you explore the data: anything you learn from the test records can leak into your choices.

Quick quiz

Why is a 100% training score no reason to choose the unlimited tree?

The model may have memorised the training records, noise included. Only the validation score says how it treats records it has not seen, and there the tree scores far lower.

You halve the test set from 120 to 60 records. By what factor does the standard error of the test score change?

It grows by \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.4\), since the standard error goes with \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Cross-validation

Guess first. With 600 records, a 20% test set gives a score that moves by three or four points between shuffles. How could you score the model on all 600 records without ever scoring it on a record it learned from?

fold
one slice of the shuffled records; the \(K\) folds together contain all of them
\(K\)-fold cross-validation
train \(K\) times, scoring on a different fold in turn and training on the rest
cross-validated score
the mean of the \(K\) fold scores
out-of-fold prediction
the prediction for a record from a model that never saw it

In \(K\)-fold cross-validation, all the records are scored once, by a model that did not train on them, so the whole table is used for scoring and for training.

FoldsShuffle
Model
trainingfold being scoredringed: the 3 Ganges sharks

Five folds give five scores for the logistic regression, from 75.0% to 80.8%. Their mean, 77.8%, is steadier than any single fold, and the same table ranks the depth-3 tree (80.2%) above the logistic regression and the unlimited tree (70.5%) below. The task is the species problem of 0.8 on the made-up catch records; seed 4127 gives the folds of that results table.

Press Step. In round 1 the sand dots (fold 1) are set aside, the model trains on the navy dots, and its accuracy on the sand dots appears in the dot plot. Round 2 sets aside fold 2, and so on. After \(K\) rounds all the records have been scored once, by a model that never saw them, and the mean of the \(K\) scores is the cross-validated score. Switch the model and step again to compare. Then shuffle: the individual fold scores move by a few points, but the means are far steadier than one held-out score.

Cross-validation costs \(K\) training runs, so it suits small data and fast models. Five or ten folds are common defaults. With ten folds the model trains on 90% of the records, which is closer to the final model than 80%. Use it on the training data to choose between models and settings. Keep the separate test set for one last, single check of the model you chose.

The fold scores in the dot plot are not independent, because any two training sets share most of their records. Treat their spread as a rough guide to the noise. For a formal answer about two models, use the paired test of 0.7 on the same folds.

0.758fold 1+0.808fold 2+0.792fold 3+0.750fold 4+0.783fold 5÷5folds=0.778cross-validated accuracy

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k \qquad \mathrm{SD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(a_k - \mathrm{CV}\bigr)^2} \]

  • \(K\) is the number of folds and \(a_k\) the accuracy on fold \(k\), scored by a model trained on the other \(K-1\) folds.
  • CV is the cross-validated score. SD is the standard deviation of the fold scores, the spread from 0.7.

Common belief. Cross-validation produces one final model that has been tested \(K\) times.

Why it fails. It trains \(K\) different models and throws them away. The score describes the recipe (the model type, settings and preprocessing), not any one trained model.

Better account. Use cross-validation to pick the recipe. Then train once on all the training records and grade that model on the test set.

Debug tip. Shuffle before you fold, or the folds are slices of a sorted table. With a rare class, deal the species out in proportion (stratify), or one fold may contain none of the three Ganges sharks and its score will not mean much.

import numpy as np

# ten made-up spadenose records: length (cm) and weight (kg)
L = np.array([22, 25, 28, 31, 33, 36, 38, 41, 44, 47])
W = np.array([0.03, 0.04, 0.06, 0.09, 0.10, 0.13, 0.16, 0.21, 0.27, 0.33])

def kfold(n, K, seed):
    idx = np.arange(n)
    np.random.RandomState(seed).shuffle(idx)    # shuffle first
    return np.array_split(idx, K)               # K nearly equal folds

scores = []
for k, fold in enumerate(kfold(len(L), 5, 4127)):
    train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(L)), fold)   # all records outside the fold
    w1, w0 = np.polyfit(L[train], W[train], 1)      # fit a line on them
    err = np.sqrt(np.mean((w0 + w1 * L[fold] - W[fold]) ** 2))
    scores.append(err)
    ids = sorted(fold.tolist())
    print(f"fold {k + 1}: records {ids} score {err:.3f} kg")
print(f"cross-validated RMSE: {np.mean(scores):.3f} kg")

fold 2: records [5, 9] score 0.044 kg
fold 3: records [2, 3] score 0.011 kg
fold 4: records [0, 4] score 0.040 kg
fold 5: records [1, 6] score 0.021 kg

Leakage

Guess first. A model scores 95% on the test set. You then find that the same shark appears in both the training set and the test set, in different frames of one dive video. Is 95% still a fair guess for sharks the camera has not seen?

leakage
information from the test rows reaching the model before the test, through the labels, the preprocessing or copies of the rows (Kaufman et al., 2012)
duplicate
the same row, or the same animal, in both the training and the test set
group split
a split that keeps all rows of one animal (or patient, or dive) together in one set
pipeline
all the steps from the raw table to a prediction: scaling, filling gaps, picking columns, fitting

A test score is fair only if the test rows had no influence on the model before the test.

Show

Splitting video frames at random lifts an unlimited decision tree from 83.6% on new sharks to 94.7% on the leaky test set. Scaling with the test rows included makes no visible difference, and picking columns with all the labels turns coin flips into a 75% score. The sharks, frames and filler readings are made up. Scores are means over 200 random splits.

Start with the first tab. A scaler learns a mean and a spread from data, so it is part of the model. If you fit it on the whole table, the test rows have influenced a number the model uses. Here the effect on the score is close to zero for both models, and the figure shows that plainly. That is no excuse to skip the rule. A leak can be harmless today and large next month, with a different model or a smaller table.

The second tab is the leak that matters for SharkCam. A dive video has many frames of one shark. Splitting frames at random puts the same shark on both sides, so the test frames are not new sharks. A model that can memorise, such as a tree with no depth limit, recognises the shark by its depth and temperature and reads off the answer. The logistic regression cannot memorise, so it scores the same either way. Treat a high score from a flexible model on a split like this as a warning. The fix is the group split: all frames of a shark go to one set.

The third tab is the largest. The filler readings have no link to the labels, yet the score is 75%. Choosing columns by looking at the labels of all the sharks, test sharks included, picks readings that match those labels by chance. Cross-validating afterwards cannot undo that. This is why preprocessing that uses the labels must be repeated inside the cross-validation loop, on the training rows of the current fold. In the language of sets (0.4), the training and test sets must have an empty overlap, and so must the information they pass to the model.

94.7%leaky test score−83.6%score on new sharks=11.1points of false confidence

\[ \mathrm{Train} \cap \mathrm{Test} = \varnothing \qquad \text{and all fitted steps see only Train} \]

  • \(\mathrm{Train}\) and \(\mathrm{Test}\) are the sets of animals (whole animals, and not single rows) used for training and for the test. \(\cap\) means the part they share, and \(\varnothing\) is the empty set.
  • "Fitted steps" means scaling, imputing, selecting columns and fitting. Any step that learns a number from data is fitted on the training rows only, then applied to the test rows.

Common belief. Scaling, filling gaps and picking columns are chores before modelling, so I can do them once on the whole table.

Why it fails. These steps all learn something from the data: a mean, a median, a ranking of columns. Learned on the whole table, it has seen the test rows, and with labels involved the effect can be large.

Better account. Treat the whole pipeline as the model. Fit all the steps on training rows only. A pipeline object, such as the one in scikit-learn, does this inside cross-validation for you.

Debug tip. A score that is too good is a clue. If a simple model suddenly scores 99%, look for leaks before you celebrate. Look for duplicate rows, several rows from one animal, a column built from the label, or a step fitted before the split. Check the overlap directly, as in the code below.

import numpy as np

shark = np.repeat(np.arange(6), 4)    # 6 sharks, 4 frames each
rng = np.random.RandomState(1)

# leaky: split the 24 frames at random
order = rng.permutation(len(shark))
test_f, train_f = order[:6], order[6:]
both = set(shark[train_f].tolist()) & set(shark[test_f].tolist())
print("sharks in both sets:", sorted(both))

# fair: split the sharks, then take all their frames
test_sharks = rng.permutation(6)[:2]
is_test = np.isin(shark, test_sharks)
both = set(shark[~is_test].tolist()) & set(shark[is_test].tolist())
print("sharks in both sets:", sorted(both))
sharks in both sets: [0, 3, 4, 5]
sharks in both sets: []

Quick quiz

Why does the logistic regression barely change between the leaky and the fair split, while the unlimited tree changes a lot?

The tree can memorise rows, so a test frame of a shark it trained on is easy. The logistic regression fits one smooth rule and has little capacity to memorise, so it gains very little from seeing the same shark.

You select the 20 best columns by correlation with the label on the whole table and then run 5-fold cross-validation. What goes wrong?

The selection used the labels of the records in all the test folds. Columns that match by chance look useful, and the fold scores are too high. Do the selection inside the training part of the folds.

The confusion matrix and its scores

Guess first. SharkCam's hammerhead detector flags 49 of the 600 catch records. Of the 49, 29 are hammerheads. The table has 57 hammerheads in all. What share of the hammerheads did the detector find: 29 of 49, 29 of 57, or 49 of 57?

positive
the class the detector looks for: here a hammerhead. All other records are negatives.
true positive (TP)
flagged, and it is a hammerhead
false positive (FP)
flagged, but not a hammerhead: a false alarm
false negative (FN)
not flagged, but it is a hammerhead: a miss
true negative (TN)
not flagged, and not a hammerhead

The confusion matrix counts the four kinds of answer. All the scores in this chapter are ratios of those four counts.

hammerhead (positive)any other shark (negative)teal outline: on top of the fractiondashed: the bottom (the top is included)

At a threshold of 0.30 the detector finds 29 of the 57 hammerheads (recall 50.9%), and 29 of its 49 flags are right (precision 59.2%). Its accuracy, 92.0%, hides both numbers. The records are made-up; the scores come from a logistic regression on length, weight, girth, depth and temperature. In this table the girth was computed from the weight.

The matrix puts the true class in the rows and the model's answer in the columns, as 0.8 shows. The 600 records are dots, and the cell a dot falls in says which of the four things happened to it. The answer to the guess-first question is 29 of 57. The 57 are all the hammerheads, so recall divides by the positive row (FN + TP). The 29 of 49 is a different question: of the flagged records, how many are right. That is precision.

Click the five scores in turn. Accuracy uses all four cells. Precision looks down the flagged column, so it counts the false alarms. Recall looks along the positive row, so it counts the misses. Specificity is recall's mirror for the negatives: of the sharks that are not hammerheads, how many were left alone. F1 combines precision and recall into one number. Recall is also called sensitivity, and in screening you will meet both words for the same ratio. The false positive rate is 1 minus specificity, and the ROC curve will need it.

29TP: hammerheads flagged÷57TP + FN: all hammerheads (29 + 28)=50.9%recall, also called sensitivity

\[ \text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + TN + FN} \qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \]

\[ \text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} \qquad F_1 = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} \]

  • TP, FP, TN, FN are the counts of true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives.
  • Recall is the share of positives found. Precision is the share of flags that are right. Specificity is the share of negatives left alone. A ratio is undefined if its bottom is zero: precision when no record is flagged, recall when there are no positives.
  • \(F_1\) is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The two forms above are the same number, because \(\text{precision} = TP/(TP+FP)\) and \(\text{recall} = TP/(TP+FN)\).

Why F1 and not the plain average?

Guess first. A detector flags all 600 records. Its recall is 100% and its precision is 9.5%. What do you think a fair single number for it is?

A detector that flags all 600 records has a plain average of 54.8% and an F1 of 17.4%. F1 is pulled towards the weaker number. Move the sliders to see that F1 can reach 100% only when both are 100%.

The figure uses the made-up table. F1 is the harmonic mean, which drags the result towards the smaller of the two numbers. It also ignores the true negatives, which makes it useful when negatives are plentiful and uninteresting. That is a limit as well: a model can change its TN count and F1 will not notice.

Common belief. A detector that is 92% accurate finds 92% of the hammerheads.

Why it fails. Accuracy counts all 600 records, and most are not hammerheads. Recall counts only the 57 that are. Here 92.0% accuracy goes with finding 50.9% of the hammerheads.

Better account. Say which cell matters to you, then read its row or column. Recall for the animals you must not miss, precision for the alarms you must not waste.

Debug tip. Tools disagree on the layout of the matrix. Scikit-learn puts the true class in the rows and the predicted class in the columns, with the negative class first. Its matrix reads TN, FP in the top row and FN, TP in the bottom row. A swapped matrix turns recall into precision. Read the axis labels first, and check that the four counts add up to the number of records.

import numpy as np

# 12 made-up records; 1 = hammerhead
y = np.array([1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0])
score = np.array([.9, .2, .6, .4, .1, .05, .3, .8, .7, .02, .55, .1])
flag = score >= 0.5                     # the threshold

tp = np.sum(flag & (y == 1))
fp = np.sum(flag & (y == 0))
fn = np.sum(~flag & (y == 1))
tn = np.sum(~flag & (y == 0))
print("TN FP / FN TP:", tn, fp, "/", fn, tp)

print("accuracy   ", (tp + tn) / len(y))
print("precision  ", tp / (tp + fp))
print("recall     ", tp / (tp + fn))
print("specificity", tn / (tn + fp))
print("F1         ", round(2 * tp / (2 * tp + fp + fn), 3))

Quick quiz

Which score would you watch if a false alarm costs the crew an hour, and which if a missed hammerhead is the worse outcome?

Precision for the false alarms, because it falls as FP rises. Recall for the misses, because it falls as FN rises.

A detector has TP = 8, FP = 2, FN = 12, TN = 78. Find precision, recall and F1.

Moving the threshold

Guess first. The detector gives the records scores from 0 to 1 and flags a record if its score reaches a threshold. If you lower the threshold, does it find more hammerheads, fewer, or the same number? What happens to the false alarms?

score
the number the model outputs; higher means more likely to be a hammerhead. For logistic regression it is a probability.
threshold
the score at which the model starts to say yes. Written \(\tau\) (tau).
trade-off
lowering the threshold removes misses and adds false alarms; raising it does the reverse
cost
what one miss or one false alarm costs you, in time, money or harm

A threshold is a choice that you make. It moves the model along a trade-off between misses and false alarms.

Lowering the threshold from 0.30 to 0.10 raises recall from 50.9% to 87.7% and cuts precision from 59.2% to 34.5%. Records outlined in black are errors. Drag the line, or use the slider, and watch the dot move along the ROC curve. The records are made-up, not field data.

The strip shows the 600 scores. Hammerheads (coral, top lane) mostly score higher than other sharks (navy, bottom lane), but the lanes overlap, so no threshold separates them cleanly. Records to the right of the line are flagged. Move the line left and it takes in more hammerheads and more other sharks. The matrix, the five scores and the dot on the ROC curve all follow. The horizontal axis is the score drawn on a log-odds scale, as in A3, so that the many low scores spread out; the labels give the probabilities.

The right threshold depends on what the two errors cost. Say a missed hammerhead is five times as bad as a false alarm. The scalloped hammerhead is listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN, so a crew that misses one may keep an animal it should have released. A false alarm costs a short stop. Set the cost slider to 5. The sand curve under the strip adds up the errors for the different thresholds, and its lowest point is the cheapest. If the scores are well calibrated, a short calculation gives that point directly. Take one record whose chance of being a hammerhead is \(p\). If you flag it, you pay for a false alarm (1 unit) with chance \(1-p\). If you do not flag it, you pay for a miss (5 units) with chance \(p\). Flag it when \(5p\) is larger than \(1-p\), which is when \(p\) is above 1/6.

With a cost of 5 the formula and the data agree to three decimals. Try 10 or 20, and they drift apart. The formula assumes the probabilities are well calibrated, and a model can rank records well and still give probabilities that are too high or too low. The cost curve is also flat near its bottom, so several thresholds cost almost the same. Choose the threshold on validation scores (or on out-of-fold scores, as here), freeze it, and only then open the test set.

28misses (FN)×5cost of a miss+20false alarms (FP)×1cost of a false alarm=160total cost

\[ \text{cost} = c_{FN}\,FN + c_{FP}\,FP \qquad \text{flag when } p \ge \tau^{*} = \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}} \]

  • \(c_{FN}\) and \(c_{FP}\) are the cost of one miss and one false alarm, in any unit you choose. Only their ratio matters.
  • \(p\) is the model's probability for a record and \(\tau^{*}\) the threshold that minimises the expected cost, assuming \(p\) is calibrated. With a miss costing 5 false alarms, \(\tau^{*} = 1/6 = 0.167\).

Common belief. A model has one accuracy, one precision and one recall.

Why it fails. Those numbers belong to a model and a threshold together. The same model gives 50.9% recall at 0.30 and 87.7% at 0.10, and the default of 0.5 is only a habit.

Better account. Report the threshold with the scores, or report a curve that covers all thresholds. Then pick the threshold from the costs.

Debug tip. If recall looks too low, check the threshold before you retrain. A model trained on a rare class often gives low probabilities to all its records, so the default threshold of 0.5 flags almost no records. Lower the threshold on validation data and look again.

import numpy as np

Quick quiz

A missed hammerhead costs 9 false alarms. What threshold does the formula give, and is it above or below 0.5?

\(\tau^{*} = 1/(1+9) = 0.10\), below 0.5. When misses cost more, the model should flag more readily.

Lowering the threshold never reduces recall. Why can it reduce precision?

Records flagged before are still flagged, so TP cannot fall, and recall cannot fall. The extra flags may be mostly other sharks, so FP can grow faster than TP and precision can fall.

The ROC curve and AUC

Guess first. Pick one hammerhead and one other shark at random from the catch table. How often will the hammerhead have the higher score, if the scores are no better than coin flips? And if they are perfect?

true positive rate (TPR)
the share of positives flagged. The same number as recall.
false positive rate (FPR)
the share of negatives flagged. It equals 1 minus specificity.
ROC curve
the curve of TPR against FPR as the threshold moves from high to low (receiver operating characteristic, a name from radar; see Fawcett, 2006)
AUC
the area under the ROC curve, from 0 to 1

The ROC curve shows all the thresholds at once, and the area under it is the chance that a random positive scores above a random negative.

Scores from

The five-measurement scorer has an AUC of 0.920: in 28,467 of the 30,951 hammerhead-and-other-shark pairs, the hammerhead scores higher (ties count half). Length alone gives 0.802, and a coin flip about 0.5. The records are made-up; the scores are out-of-fold.

Read the curve from the bottom left. A threshold above all the scores flags no record, which puts you at (0, 0). As the threshold falls, the dot climbs when it flags a hammerhead and moves right when it flags another shark. A scorer that ranks well climbs steeply before it moves right, so its curve hugs the top left corner. A coin flip climbs and moves right at the same pace, along the dashed diagonal. Pick the scorers in turn and drag the threshold slider to see the dot trace the curves.

Now the area. Press "Draw 100 pairs". A draw picks a random hammerhead and a random other shark and checks which has the higher score. The share of draws the hammerhead wins settles near the AUC. "Check all pairs" runs through all of them and gets the AUC itself. This is why AUC has a plain meaning (Hanley and McNeil, 1982): it is the chance that the model ranks a random positive above a random negative. It does not depend on one threshold, and it does not depend on how common the positives are, because it compares positives only with negatives.

That second fact is a strength and a limit. A model with an AUC of 0.92 ranks well. It does not say how many false alarms you will meet at the threshold you pick, and that number depends on the base rate. The next sections come back to this. An AUC below 0.5 means the scores are upside down, so flipping the sign of the score would help.

28,468.5pairs ranked right: 28,467 wins + half of 3 ties÷30,95157 hammerheads × 543 others=0.920AUC

\[ \mathrm{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \qquad \mathrm{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} \qquad \mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{n_{+}\,n_{-}} \sum_{i \in \text{pos}} \sum_{j \in \text{neg}} \Bigl[\mathbf{1}(s_i > s_j) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{1}(s_i = s_j)\Bigr] \]

  • TPR is recall, and FPR is 1 minus specificity. The ROC curve plots one against the other for all thresholds.
  • \(n_{+}\) and \(n_{-}\) are the numbers of positives and negatives, and \(s_i\) is the score of record \(i\). The sums run over all pairs of one positive and one negative. \(\mathbf{1}(\cdot)\) is 1 if the statement inside is true and 0 if not.

Common belief. An AUC of 0.92 means the model is right 92% of the time.

Why it fails. Accuracy needs a threshold and counts all records. AUC compares positives with negatives, over all thresholds. The same model has an AUC of 0.920 and an accuracy of 83.0% at threshold 0.10.

Better account. AUC measures how well the scores order the records. You still need a threshold, chosen from the costs, to turn scores into decisions.

Debug tip. If you get an AUC well below 0.5, the sign of the score or the labelling of the positive class is probably flipped. If you get an AUC of 1.0 on a new dataset, suspect a leak (earlier in this chapter) before you suspect a perfect model.

import numpy as np

# 8 made-up records: 3 positives, 5 negatives
y = np.array([1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])
s = np.array([.9, .7, .4, .8, .5, .3, .2, .1])

pos, neg = s[y == 1], s[y == 0]
# every positive against every negative
wins = (pos[:, None] > neg[None, :]).sum()
ties = (pos[:, None] == neg[None, :]).sum()
pairs = len(pos) * len(neg)
print("AUC from pairs:", (wins + 0.5 * ties) / pairs)

# the same number from the ROC curve: sort by score,
# step up for a positive or right for a negative, add trapezoids
order = np.argsort(-s)
tpr = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(y[order]) / y.sum()])
fpr = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(1 - y[order]) / (1 - y).sum()])
area = np.sum((fpr[1:] - fpr[:-1]) * (tpr[1:] + tpr[:-1]) / 2)
print("AUC from curve:", area)
AUC from pairs: 0.8
AUC from curve: 0.8

Quick quiz

What AUC would you expect from scores that are random and unrelated to the labels, and what from scores that are perfectly upside down?

About 0.5 for random scores. About 0 for upside-down scores: the positive nearly always scores lower, so reversing the sign gives about 1.

Does AUC change if you double the number of negatives by adding more records of the same kind?

Not in expectation. AUC compares a positive with a negative, so the mix of the data does not matter, as long as the new negatives are like the old ones. Precision at a fixed threshold would fall.

Rare classes

Guess first. The made-up catch table has 600 records, and 3 of them are Ganges sharks. A model that never says "Ganges" gets 597 of the 600 right. What is its accuracy, and how many Ganges sharks does it find?

rare class
a class with few records. Here the Ganges shark is 0.5% of the table.
lazy model
a model that never flags the positive class. Its accuracy is the share of negatives. It is the baseline of A1 for a yes-or-no question.
precision-recall (PR) curve
precision against recall as the threshold moves from high to low
random flagging
flagging records at random. Its precision equals the base rate, so it is the floor of a PR curve.

When one class is rare, accuracy mostly measures the common class. Recall and precision for the rare class tell you whether the model is useful.

ClassModel
positive record (flagged or not)all other recordsringed: flagged by the model

A model that never flags a Ganges shark is 99.5% accurate and finds none. A model that finds all three at a threshold of 0.10 raises four false alarms, and its accuracy falls to 99.3%, below the lazy model. The records are made-up. With three Ganges records, any recall is a rough estimate.

Start with "Never flags" on the Ganges class. The accuracy box reads 99.5%, the highest number on the page, and the recall box reads 0%. The model is useless for the one thing SharkCam must not miss, and accuracy cannot tell. Then choose "Flags all records". Recall jumps to 100% and precision drops to the base rate, 0.5%. Finally choose the logistic regression and move the threshold. A model that is useful for Ganges sharks has lower accuracy than the lazy model, because a false alarm costs accuracy and the lazy model makes none.

The PR curve shows what the ROC curve can hide. The logistic regression has an AUC of 0.997 for the Ganges class, which looks excellent. At the threshold that finds all three sharks, its false positive rate is 4 of 597, or 0.7%, which also looks small. Its precision is 3 of 7, or 43%. The false positive rate divides by the 597 negatives, so a few false alarms barely move it. Precision divides by the number flagged, so the same false alarms matter a great deal. When the positives are rare, read the PR curve and compare it with the dashed base-rate line (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015).

Switch to the hammerhead class, where 57 positives give a smoother curve. The same effect appears in a milder form, with a lazy accuracy of 90.5%. With only three Ganges records, recall can take only four values: 0%, 33%, 67% or 100%. Its 95% interval is wide: finding 2 of 3 gives an interval from 21% to 94% (0.7). The data cannot say how well the model finds Ganges sharks. It can only say that the lazy model finds none.

0Ganges sharks found÷3Ganges sharks=0%recall

\[ \text{accuracy of the lazy model} = 1 - \frac{n_{+}}{n} \qquad \text{random flagging: precision} = \frac{n_{+}}{n} \]

  • \(n_{+}\) is the number of positive records and \(n\) the number of all records. For the Ganges class \(n_{+}/n = 3/600 = 0.005\), so the lazy accuracy is 99.5%.
  • Any model should beat these two baselines on the score that matters. The first baseline is the lazy accuracy. The second is the dashed line in the PR plot.

Common belief. A model that is 99.5% accurate works.

Why it fails. A model that flags no record is 99.5% accurate on the same table. Accuracy adds up the correct answers, and the 597 common records dominate the sum.

Better account. Compare with the lazy baseline before you celebrate. Then read recall and precision for the rare class, with an interval that shows how few records lie behind them.

Debug tip. Put the lazy baseline beside any score you report, for the metric you care about. It takes one line: the share of the majority class, or zero recall. The same habit helped in the results table of 0.8, where the tree scored 80.2% and never found a Ganges shark.

Quick quiz

A model flags 7 records and finds all 3 Ganges sharks. What are its precision, recall and accuracy?

Precision is 3 of 7, 43%. Recall is 3 of 3, 100%. It makes 4 false alarms, so 596 of the 600 calls are right: accuracy 99.3%, which is below the 99.5% of the lazy model.

Why can a ROC curve look excellent when the PR curve for the same scores looks poor?

The false positive rate divides by all the negatives, a large number, so many false alarms still give a small rate. Precision divides by the flagged records, so the same false alarms weigh heavily.

Base rates in screening

Guess first. Imagine a clinic in Mumbai that wants to screen women for diabetes with a model trained on the Pima data. The model finds 57% of the women who have diabetes and clears 88% of those who do not. In the Pima sample, 71% of flagged women have diabetes. Where only 10 in 100 women have it, will a flagged woman be more likely, equally likely or less likely to have diabetes?

screening
testing people who have no symptoms, to find the few who need a closer look
prevalence (base rate)
the share of the people tested who have the condition (0.7)
positive predictive value (PPV)
the chance that a flagged person has the condition. It is the same number as precision.
sensitivity and specificity
properties of the test in the group it was measured on. Sensitivity is recall, and specificity is 1 minus the false positive rate.

Sensitivity and specificity describe the model. Precision also depends on how common the condition is where the model is used.

The clinic is a story. The data are a published dataset: it has 768 women of Pima heritage, aged 21 or older, measured in the United States (Smith et al., 1988). It does not come from Mumbai or from India. The model is a logistic regression on six of the measurements, scored out of fold.

Where the model is used
has diabetes, flagged (TP)has diabetes, missed (FN)no diabetes, flagged (FP)no diabetes, cleared (TN)

The same model is right for 71% of the women it flags in the Pima sample. It is right for 34% at a prevalence of 10% and for 9% at 2%. Sensitivity (57%) and specificity (88%) did not change. The 10% and 2% towns are made-up examples, not measurements.

A circle is a woman. The coral circles have diabetes and the navy ones do not. A filled circle means the model flagged her. Slide the prevalence down. The coral share falls, the number of navy filled circles (false alarms) changes little, and the share of filled circles that are coral shrinks. The false alarms come from the many women without diabetes, who are flagged at a rate of 12% whatever the prevalence. This is the Bayes calculation of 0.7, written with the confusion matrix: TP grows with the number of sick women and FP with the number of healthy women.

The practical consequence is for the person who gets a flag. At 2% prevalence, about 9 in 100 flagged women have diabetes, and the other 91 are sent for more tests they did not need. That is not a reason to drop screening. It is a reason to know the number. Design the follow-up test for a high share of false alarms. Avoid telling a flagged woman that the model is 88% right about her. The model also assumes its sensitivity and specificity still apply in the new population. A model trained on one population may not suit another, a lesson Rayhaan drew in his diabetes screening project. The next section checks that assumption inside the data you have.

57TP: 100 sick × 0.571÷169TP + FP: 57 + 112 (900 healthy × 0.124)=34%of flagged women have diabetes

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{\mathrm{sens}\cdot p}{\mathrm{sens}\cdot p + (1-\mathrm{spec})(1-p)} \]

  • sens is the sensitivity and spec the specificity of the model. \(p\) is the prevalence where the model is used.
  • The top is TP per woman (sens times \(p\)). The bottom adds FP per woman (1 minus spec, times the healthy share \(1-p\)). It is Bayes' rule from 0.7 with the model's own numbers.

Common belief. A good test is good wherever it is used.

Why it fails. Sensitivity and specificity are measured on a sample with a given mix of sick and healthy people. Precision depends on that mix. Move the model to a town with a different mix and precision changes, even if the model itself does not.

Better account. Report sensitivity and specificity. Work out precision for the place where the model will be used, with that place's prevalence, and check that sensitivity and specificity still apply there.

Debug tip. If a paper or a test set was built by choosing equal numbers of sick and healthy people, its precision is for a 50% prevalence. Ask what the prevalence is where you will deploy, then recompute precision from sensitivity and specificity. Never read precision from a test set whose class balance somebody chose.

Quick quiz

A screening model has sensitivity 90% and specificity 90%. At 1% prevalence, what share of flagged people have the condition?

Per 1,000 people, 10 are sick and 9 of them are flagged. Of the 990 healthy, 99 are flagged. Precision is \(9/(9+99) = 8.3\%\).

Does moving the threshold change the model's precision in the clinic? Does it change the prevalence?

It changes sensitivity and specificity, so it changes precision. It does not change the prevalence, which describes the women, not the model.

Subgroup analysis

Guess first. At a threshold of 0.5, the model finds 57% of the women with diabetes overall. Do you expect it to find about 57% in women aged 21 to 29, and in women aged 50 and over?

subgroup
part of the data picked out by one column, such as an age band
subgroup analysis
computing the same scores inside the subgroups, at one threshold
95% interval
the range a score could plausibly span given how few women lie behind it (0.7)
disparity
a gap between the scores of two groups

One overall score averages over groups, and it can hide a group for whom the model works much worse.

Women
sensitivity (circle)specificity (diamond)
Show the working for

At one threshold of 0.5, sensitivity rises from 46% in women aged 21 to 29 to 79% in women aged 50 and over. Specificity falls from 94% to 65%. The intervals for the older groups are wide. In the 154 held-out women, the group aged 50 and over has only 7 women with diabetes. The women are the Pima records from the United States, not a Mumbai clinic.

This is the kind of check Rayhaan ran in his diabetes screening project, where he compared results across demographic subgroups to look for bias. Here the groups are age bands, and the scores are those of the last section. The dots are sensitivity (circles) and specificity (diamonds), and the bars are 95% intervals. Under the plot, the table gives the counts behind the dots. Look at the table first: the youngest group has 396 women, over half the data, so the overall score is mostly the score for women aged 21 to 29.

The model treats the groups differently at one threshold. It flags 15% of women aged 21 to 29 and 56% of those aged 50 and over. Diabetes is more common in the older groups (21%, 46%, 55% and 48%), and age is one of the model's inputs, so the older groups get higher scores. At the same threshold, the model misses more of the younger women who have diabetes and raises more false alarms for the older women who do not. Neither error is a mistake of arithmetic. They follow from the data, and a clinic would want to know both.

Now change to the 154 held-out women. The intervals widen, and the group aged 50 and over has 7 women with diabetes, all found: a sensitivity of 7 of 7, or 100%. A plain interval around that gives zero width, which is nonsense. The Wilson interval gives 65% to 100%. This is why a subgroup result needs its counts and an interval. A gap between two groups is a finding only if the intervals do not overlap much. Even then, the groups must not have been chosen after the fact from many possible splits. Woman A, the 28-year-old with glucose 158 who has diabetes, is in the youngest group, where the model misses more cases. At 0.5 she is found.

7 of 7sensitivity in the oldest held-out group→100% to 100%plain: 1 ± 1.96 × 065% to 100%Wilson interval

\[ \frac{\hat{p} + \dfrac{z^2}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \dfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}} \qquad z = 1.96 \]

  • \(\hat{p}\) is the observed share (sensitivity or specificity in the group) and \(n\) the number of women it is computed on: those with diabetes for sensitivity, those without for specificity.
  • The plain interval \(\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\) from 0.7 fails when \(n\) is small or \(\hat{p}\) is near 0 or 1. The formula above (Wilson, 1927) stays inside 0 and 1 and does not collapse to zero width at 100%.

Common belief. If the overall accuracy is good, the model works for all groups.

Why it fails. An overall score is an average, and the largest group dominates it. A group with a different base rate or different features can have a much lower sensitivity and the overall number will hardly move.

Better account. Compute the same scores inside the groups, show the counts and the intervals, and compare them at the threshold you will use.

Debug tip. When a group looks worse, check its size before you tell a story about it. Then check its base rate and whether the model sees the column that defines the group. A gap in sensitivity with a different base rate is not the same finding as a gap in AUC. The fairness chapter (F1) returns to what a fair model means.

A group has 4 women with diabetes and the model finds 4. Is the model perfect for that group?

No. Four cases cannot show that. The 95% Wilson interval for 4 of 4 runs from about 51% to 100%, so the true sensitivity could be much lower.

A model gets 121 of 154 held-out women right (78.6%). Use the standard error formula to give a 95% interval. Can you tell it apart from a second model that scores 80.5% on the same women? (0.7)

In the polynomial figure the best degree was about 3, because weight grows like length cubed. What degree would you expect to work best if you fitted the logarithm of weight to the logarithm of length, and why? (A2)

Degree 1. Taking logs turns \(W = aL^b\) into \(\log W = \log a + b \log L\), a straight line. The flexibility a cubic gave in the original units is already built into the log-log scale.

In 0.8 the depth-3 tree scores 80.2% on the species table, and its confusion matrix has one hammerhead labelled "Ganges" and no true Ganges found. What is its accuracy on the yes-or-no question "is this a Ganges shark?", and how does it compare with the lazy model? (0.8)

It makes 3 misses and 1 false alarm, so 596 of 600 calls are right: 99.3%. The lazy model gets 597 of 600, 99.5%, so on accuracy the tree is slightly worse and finds no Ganges shark either.
