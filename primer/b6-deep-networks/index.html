Making deep networks trainable

Starting values, normalisation, shortcuts and noise that let twenty layers learn

Stack twenty layers and training can fail before it starts. The signal shrinks to zero or blows up from layer to layer, and the slopes of B4 do the same on the way back. This chapter shows the problem layer by layer. Then it covers the remedies in the order people reach for them. First comes a good scale for the starting weights (Xavier and He). Then come normalisation (batch, layer, RMS and group norm), residual connections, and dropout, which adds noise on purpose.

The figures use random networks with made-up weights, and a made-up batch of hidden values for six sharks of the SharkCam catch table.

You will need: backpropagation and the vanishing slope (B4), the variance and standardising (0.7), a neuron with an activation (B1) and feature scaling (A2).

Signals through twenty layers

Guess first. A network has 20 layers of 100 tanh units. Its weights are random numbers with spread 0.01, which sounds like a safe, small choice. The input has typical size 1. About how large are the activations in layer 20: about 1, about 0.001, or smaller than \(10^{-15}\)?

initialisation
choosing the starting values of the weights, usually at random
weight spread \(\sigma\)
the standard deviation of the random starting weights (0.7)
vanishing and exploding
a signal or slope that shrinks or grows layer after layer (B4)
symmetry
units in a layer that start with the same values and so stay identical

At the start of training, the size of the signal in layer 20 is set by the spread of the weights, and only a narrow band of spreads keeps it near its starting size.

With spread 0.01 the activations fall to about \(10^{-20}\) by layer 20. With the He spread (0.141) both the activations and the slopes keep a size near their start. A row of a table is one layer; darker cells contain more units. The line plot gives the typical size on a log axis, so a straight line down is a constant factor per layer.

Pick the initialisations in turn, then switch between tanh and ReLU. With zeros all tables are one stripe at 0. With too small the activations collapse toward 0 layer after layer. With too large a tanh network saturates (units pile up at \(\pm1\), where the slope is near 0), and a ReLU network explodes. Xavier suits tanh and shrinks a ReLU network, and He suits ReLU. The slope tables (right) are the backward pass of B4 on random numbers. They show how big the slope reaching a layer is, from a slope of size 1 at layer 20.

The size of the signal follows a short calculation. A unit adds up \(n\) inputs times weights: \(z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i\). If the weights have mean 0 and spread \(\sigma\), and are independent of the inputs, the terms are independent, so their variances add: \(\mathrm{Var}(z) = \sum_i \mathrm{Var}(w_i x_i) = n\,\sigma^2\,\mathbb{E}[x^2]\). One layer multiplies the typical squared size by \(n\sigma^2\) (before the activation). That is a constant factor per layer, which is why the lines in the plot are straight.

\[ z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i \qquad \mathrm{Var}(z) = n\,\sigma^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[x^{2}] \]

  • \(n\) is the number of inputs of a unit, \(w_i\) a random weight with spread \(\sigma\) and \(x_i\) an input. \(\mathbb{E}[x^{2}]\) is the average squared size of an input.
  • The factor \(n\sigma^{2}\) multiplies the typical squared size of the signal in a layer, and the next layer starts from the result.

The box is a worked example for ReLU units (the factor \(n\sigma^2/2\) is explained in the next section). With spread 0.01, a layer cuts the variance to 0.5% of its size, so 20 layers leave a size of about \(10^{-23}\). With spread 1 a layer multiplies the variance by 50, and the size after 20 layers is about \(10^{17}\). The same factor, raised to the 20th power, explains both ends. The tanh case with spread 0.01 is the guess-first question: \(n\sigma^2 = 0.01\) per layer, so the size falls by a factor of \(0.1\) per layer, and \(0.1^{20}\) gives \(10^{-20}\), which matches the \(1.1\times10^{-20}\) of the figure.

Why zeros fail. With all weights 0 the units compute 0. The slope that reaches a layer's weights is the layer's input times a slope that is also 0, so no weight moves. Even if all weights were set to one shared nonzero value, all units in a layer would compute the same number and get the same slope. They would stay identical after all updates (symmetry). A layer of 100 identical units is one unit. Random starting values break the tie. This is why a network starts from small random numbers and why the spread of those numbers matters.

Common belief. Starting all weights at zero is the neutral choice, and small random numbers are a safe second best.

Why it fails. Zero weights give zero signal and zero slope, and any equal starting values give units that never differ. A random start with a small spread still shrinks the signal by a constant factor from layer to layer, and that factor compounds over 20 layers.

Better account. Choose the spread from the width of the layer so that the factor per layer is close to 1. The next section gives the rule.

Debug tip. Before training, push one batch through the network and print the typical size (the root mean square) of the activations in the layers. If the last layers are near \(10^{-3}\) or \(10^{3}\), fix the initialisation first. In practice, frameworks pick a sensible default for the layer type. This fails mostly in hand-built or very deep models, or after a change of a layer's width with no change to its initialisation (B8).

Quick quiz

With \(n = 100\) and spread 0.1, what is the variance factor per layer before the activation, and what does it predict for a tanh network?

\(n\sigma^2 = 100 \times 0.01 = 1\). The variance is kept, so the signal should stay near its size. (In the figure it shrinks slowly, because tanh squashes large values.)

Why must the factor of a single layer be close to 1, when only the product of all 20 matters?

The signal after 20 layers is the starting size times the product of 20 factors. If the factor is 0.9 in all layers the product is 0.12, and if it is 0.5 in all layers the product is \(10^{-6}\). Small deviations per layer grow exponentially with depth.

Choosing the scale: Xavier and He

Guess first. A layer adds up \(n = 100\) inputs and the right weight spread for it is about 0.1. You make the layer 4 times wider, \(n = 400\). By what factor should the spread change: multiply by 4, by 2, by 1/2 or by 1/4?

fan-in \(n_{\text{in}}\)
the number of inputs a unit adds up
fan-out \(n_{\text{out}}\)
the number of units a unit's output feeds
Xavier (Glorot) initialisation
weight variance \(2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})\), for tanh and similar activations
He initialisation
weight variance \(2/n_{\text{in}}\), for ReLU

Keeping the variance of the signal constant from layer to layer fixes the weight spread at about \(1/\sqrt{n}\) for tanh and \(\sqrt{2/n}\) for ReLU.

The signal stays near its starting size only for spreads close to the Xavier and He values. The sweet spot moves with the width: at width 100 it is near 0.1 to 0.14, and at width 400 near 0.05 to 0.07. The shaded band marks sizes between 0.1 and 10. The 36 spreads were computed offline on random networks, three runs per spread.

Switch the width and watch the dip move left as \(n\) grows. The spread must shrink like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so the guess-first answer is "multiply by 1/2" (\(1/\sqrt{4} = 1/2\)): 0.1 becomes 0.05. Both curves are steep. A spread 3 times too small gives a loss of about 20 powers of ten at width 100, and a spread 3 times too large explodes by a similar amount. The band is narrow because the factor is raised to the 20th power.

\[ \mathrm{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}\ \ \text{(Xavier)}\qquad\qquad \mathrm{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}\ \ \text{(He)} \]

  • \(\mathrm{Var}(w)\) is the variance of a random starting weight, so the spread is \(\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(w)}\).
  • \(n_{\text{in}}\) and \(n_{\text{out}}\) are the fan-in and fan-out of the layer. For a square layer \(n_{\text{in}} = n_{\text{out}} = n\) and Xavier gives \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{n}\).

Where the two rules come from. Forward: the variance factor is \(n_{\text{in}}\mathrm{Var}(w)\) (the calculation of the last section). For tanh near 0 the activation changes little, so the factor should be 1: \(\mathrm{Var}(w) = 1/n_{\text{in}}\). A ReLU keeps the positive half of a distribution that is symmetric around 0, so \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ReLU}(z)^2] = \tfrac12 \mathbb{E}[z^2]\). The factor becomes \(n_{\text{in}}\mathrm{Var}(w)/2\), and setting it to 1 gives \(\mathrm{Var}(w) = 2/n_{\text{in}}\), the He rule. Backward: the slope sent to a layer's inputs is \(W^\top\boldsymbol\delta\) (B4), a sum over \(n_{\text{out}}\) terms, so its variance factor is \(n_{\text{out}}\mathrm{Var}(w)\), and a factor of 1 needs \(\mathrm{Var}(w) = 1/n_{\text{out}}\). The forward rule wants \(1/n_{\text{in}}\), the backward rule \(1/n_{\text{out}}\). Xavier takes \(2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})\), which is the harmonic compromise between them (it equals both when \(n_{\text{in}} = n_{\text{out}}\)).

variance 2 ÷ (100 + 50)Xavier, 100 in, 50 out=0.0133spread 0.115|variance 2 ÷ 100He=0.02spread 0.141|√(6 ÷ 150)Xavier bound if uniform=0.2weights in −0.2 to 0.2

The box is a second worked example, for a layer with 100 inputs and 50 outputs. The last cell uses the uniform version of Xavier: weights drawn evenly from \(-a\) to \(a\) have variance \(a^2/3\), so \(a = \sqrt{6/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})}\) gives variance \(2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})\). Some libraries draw from this uniform range and some from a normal distribution with the same variance.

Where this stops being right. The derivation assumes independent weights with mean 0 and inputs of a steady size, and it treats the activation as linear (tanh) or as a clean half (ReLU). Actual tanh units saturate when inputs are large, so the figure's tanh curve shows the signal shrinking a little even at Xavier. After training starts, weights are no longer random. Normalisation layers (next section) make the starting scale matter much less. In practice, use He for ReLU-type activations and Xavier for tanh and sigmoid, and trust the library defaults unless you change the width or the activation. PyTorch's default for nn.Linear is a uniform draw with variance \(1/(3\,n_{\text{in}})\), which the code below confirms. A common mistake is to copy the spread of a narrow layer into a wide one.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, depth = 100, 20
x0 = rng.standard_normal((256, n))                      # inputs of typical size 1

def run(sigma):
    x = x0
    for _ in range(depth):
        W = rng.standard_normal((n, n)) * sigma         # one layer: random weights with spread sigma
        x = np.maximum(0, x @ W)                        # ReLU
    return np.sqrt((x ** 2).mean())

for name, s in [("too small", 0.01), ("Xavier", np.sqrt(2 / (n + n))), ("He", np.sqrt(2 / n)), ("too large", 1.0)]:
    print(f"{name:9s} spread {s:.3f}: size at layer 20 = {run(s):.1e}")

Common belief. One good starting spread works for any network.

Why it fails. The spread has to shrink like \(1/\sqrt{n}\): 0.1 at width 100 becomes 0.05 at width 400. The band is narrow because the factor is raised to the 20th power.

Better account. Set the spread from the width of the layer: He for ReLU and Xavier for tanh.

Debug tip. If a hand-made network trains in one width and not in another, print the activation size per layer in both. The mismatch is often a spread that did not change with the width. Biases are usually started at 0, which causes no symmetry problem because the weights are random.

Used in: the starting-loss check (B8), the layer-size checks (B8) and the residual and normalisation choices of the next two sections.

Quick quiz

A ReLU layer has 256 inputs. What is the He spread?

\(\sqrt{2/256} = 0.0884\).

Why does the He rule have a 2 where the tanh rule has a 1?

A ReLU sets the negative half of its inputs to 0, which halves the second moment of the signal. A weight variance twice as large makes up for it.

Normalisation

Guess first. The grid below is the output of one layer for a batch of 6 sharks. Rows are sharks and columns are the 8 hidden units. Layer norm averages a set of cells together. Is that set a column (one hidden unit across the sharks) or a row (all hidden units of one shark)? And does batch norm use the same set?

normalisation
shifting and scaling a set of numbers so that they have mean 0 and variance 1, then applying a learned scale \(\gamma\) and shift \(\beta\)
batch norm
normalises a hidden unit over the sharks of the batch
layer norm
normalises a shark over all of its hidden units
RMS norm
layer norm without subtracting the mean: divides by the root mean square
group norm
layer norm within a few groups of hidden units

The four norms use one formula and differ in one choice: which cells are averaged together.

Layer norm, RMS norm and group norm give a shark the same result whatever else is in the batch. Batch norm does not: change the number of sharks and the result for one shark changes. The values are made up (hidden values for six sharks, with the hidden units on their own scales).

Click any cell. The outlined cells share one mean and one variance. Choose a norm and see its set: a column for batch norm, a row for layer norm and RMS norm, a part of a row for group norm. Then press "After normalising": in the outlined set the mean is 0 and the variance is 1 (RMS norm instead has root mean square 1). The equation boxes work the chosen cell through step by step. Try these: slide the batch down to one shark with batch norm selected; then switch to "Stored averages (test time)".

\[ \hat x = \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\mathrm{var} + \epsilon}},\qquad y = \gamma\,\hat x + \beta \qquad\qquad \hat x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\mathrm{mean}(x^2) + \epsilon}},\qquad y = \gamma\,\hat x\ \ \text{(RMS norm)} \]

  • \(\mu\) and \(\mathrm{var}\) are the mean and variance of the cells in the set (0.7). \(\epsilon\) is a tiny constant (PyTorch uses \(10^{-5}\)) that keeps the division safe when the variance is 0.
  • \(\hat x\) is the normalised value. \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) are learned numbers, one pair per hidden unit. Setting \(\gamma = \sqrt{\mathrm{var}}\) and \(\beta = \mu\) would undo the normalisation, so the layer can keep the original scale if training prefers it.

Why the result has mean 0 and variance 1. The numerator \(x - \mu\) sums to 0 over the set, so the mean of \(\hat x\) is 0. Its mean square is \(\mathrm{mean}[(x-\mu)^2]/(\mathrm{var} + \epsilon) = \mathrm{var}/(\mathrm{var}+\epsilon)\), which is 1 up to \(\epsilon\). A worked example by hand, with the row \((1, 3, 5, 7)\): the mean is 4, the variance is \((9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5\), the standard deviation 2.236, and the normalised row is \((-1.342, -0.447, 0.447, 1.342)\). RMS norm of the same row divides by \(\sqrt{(1 + 9 + 25 + 49)/4} = \sqrt{21} = 4.583\) and gives \((0.218, 0.655, 1.091, 1.528)\): its mean is 0.873, not 0, and its root mean square is 1. The code below prints both and compares with PyTorch.

import numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn

x = np.array([[1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0]])                  # one shark, four hidden values
mu, var = x.mean(), x.var()                           # statistics of this one row
ln = (x - mu) / np.sqrt(var + 1e-5)                   # layer norm, scale 1 and shift 0
rms = x / np.sqrt((x ** 2).mean() + 1e-5)             # RMS norm, scale 1
print("layer norm:", ln.round(3), " mean", ln.mean().round(3), " var", ln.var().round(3))
print("RMS norm:  ", rms.round(3), " mean", rms.mean().round(3), " rms", np.sqrt((rms ** 2).mean()).round(3))

t = torch.tensor(x, dtype=torch.float32)
print("torch LayerNorm:", nn.LayerNorm(4)(t).detach().numpy().round(3), " eps =", nn.LayerNorm(4).eps)
bn = nn.BatchNorm1d(4)
print("BatchNorm1d eps =", bn.eps, " momentum =", bn.momentum)
try:
    bn.train(); bn(t)
except ValueError as e:
    print("batch of one in training mode:", e)

Why normalising helps. A layer then receives inputs of a fixed scale, whatever the weights before it did. The signal does not drift to 0 or to huge values, and the slopes of B4 stay in range. This allows a larger learning rate and makes the starting scale matter less. It is the same idea as standardising the inputs of A2, applied to the hidden values inside the network. The original batch-norm paper explained the benefit as a reduction of "internal covariate shift", the change in a layer's input distribution during training. Whether that is the reason is debated (Santurkar and others, 2018, argue against this account), so treat the effect as better established than the mechanism.

The differences, with numbers. Take the figure's default cell, Tag 3305 and \(h_3\), with value 1.6. Batch norm uses the six values in its column: mean 3.38, variance 1.72, so \(\hat x = (1.6 - 3.38)/1.31 = -1.36\). Layer norm uses the eight values in its row: mean 0.30, variance 2.50, so \(\hat x = (1.6 - 0.30)/1.58 = +0.82\). The same cell becomes negative under one norm and positive under the other, because it is compared with different cells. Layer norm does not change when the other sharks change. Group norm uses 4 of the 8 values (2 groups) or 2 (4 groups). It is a middle way, useful when a batch is too small for batch norm and the features are too varied for one shared mean. Batch norm in training mode uses the batch statistics. At test time a single shark has no batch. The layer uses stored averages kept during training as an exponential moving average (0.7), with PyTorch's default momentum 0.1. The figure shows these stored values below the grid.

Where they break. Batch norm needs a batch bigger than 1, and its error grows as the batch shrinks (Wu and He, 2018). The stored averages can also differ from the statistics of new data, which is one more way a model can fail when the water changes (C3). Layer norm does not depend on the batch and is the usual choice for text, where a token is normalised on its own (D5). RMS norm drops the mean subtraction. Zhang and Sennrich (2019) report that it works about as well as layer norm and is cheaper, with 7% to 64% less running time in their models. In practice, use batch norm for image networks with ordinary batch sizes, group norm when the batch is small, and layer norm or RMS norm for sequences. A common mistake is forgetting model.eval(): the layer then keeps using batch statistics at test time, and one shark's answer changes with its neighbours.

Common belief. Normalisation treats a shark on its own, so its output depends only on the shark.

Why it fails. Batch norm uses the other sharks in the batch. The cell of Tag 3305 and \(h_3\) becomes \(-1.36\) under batch norm and \(+0.82\) under layer norm.

Better account. Layer, RMS and group norm use only the shark's own row. Put a batch-norm model in evaluation mode at test time, so that it uses its stored averages.

Debug tip. If a model works in training and gives odd outputs on a single test image, check that it is in evaluation mode and that the stored averages were updated. PyTorch raises an error for a batch of one in training mode, as above. If outputs for the same shark differ between runs with different batches, a batch statistic is leaking in.

Quick quiz

Which of batch, layer, RMS and group norm give a shark the same output whatever else is in the batch?

Layer norm, RMS norm and group norm, because a shark's cells are normalised using only that shark's own row. Batch norm in training mode uses the other sharks.

What does batch norm do for a batch of one shark in training mode, and why?

A hidden unit has a single value, so its variance is 0 and \(x - \mu = 0\): the normalised value is 0 for all cells and the output is just \(\beta\). The layer has no information to work with, and PyTorch raises an error instead, as the code shows.

Residual connections

Guess first. In a stack of 30 plain blocks, the slope reaching block 0 is a tiny fraction of the slope at the output (at weight scale 0.6). Now let a block output \(x + F(x)\) instead of \(F(x)\). By roughly how much does the slope reaching block 0 change: about the same, ten times larger, or more than a million times larger?

residual connection
a shortcut that adds a block's input to its output: \(y = x + F(x)\)
block
one stage of the network, here a layer with an activation, written \(F\)
identity path
the shortcut route through the network that skips the blocks

A shortcut that adds the input to the output gives the slope a direct path back, so it does not have to pass through all the blocks' slopes.

Without shortcuts the slope reaching block 0 is less than a millionth of the slope at the output. With shortcuts it is larger than at the output, not smaller. Random tanh blocks of width 64 with made-up weights at weight scale 0.6.

Toggle the switch and watch the bars at the left of the plot, the blocks nearest the input. The dotted line keeps the other setting visible. Then raise the weight scale to 1. The plain stack now passes on more of the slope (0.16 of the output's), while the shortcut stack grows (13 times the output's). The shortcut brings the other problem with it, and the readout below the plot shows the activations growing too.

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} + F(\mathbf{x}) \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{y}}\Big(I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\Big) \]

  • \(F\) is what the block computes (a weight matrix and an activation). \(I\) is the identity: the slope passed straight through the shortcut.
  • \(\partial F/\partial\mathbf{x}\) is the slope of the block. In the right-hand formula the two terms are the two paths of B4: through the shortcut (the \(I\)) and through the block.

Why the slope persists. A block \(y = x + F(x)\) uses \(x\) twice, so by the sum rule of B4 the slope to \(x\) is the sum of two paths. Through \(L\) blocks the slope picks up a product \(\prod_l (I + J_l)\) with \(J_l = \partial F_l/\partial \mathbf{x}\). Multiply out the product: it contains the term \(I\) (all shortcuts), plus terms with one or more \(J_l\). The identity term does not shrink with depth. In a plain stack the slope is the product \(\prod_l J_l\) alone, which is the chain of small factors of B4. With scalar numbers: if a block's slope is \(f = 0.8\), a plain stack gives \(0.8^{29} \approx 0.0015\) over 30 blocks (the ReLU example of B4). With a shortcut the per-block factor is \(1 + f\); for a block slope of \(+0.05\) it is \(1.05^{29} \approx 4.1\), and for \(-0.05\) it is \(0.95^{29} \approx 0.23\). The shortcut keeps the factor near 1 when the blocks are gentle.

plain: 0.829 ≈ 0.0015block slope 0.8|shortcuts: 1.0529 ≈ 4.1block slope +0.05|shortcuts: 0.9529 ≈ 0.23block slope −0.05

Where it breaks. The activations also add up, so with blocks of gain near 1 the size of \(x\) grows with depth, and the slope can grow too (the figure shows this at larger weight scales). Deep residual networks therefore combine shortcuts with normalisation layers or with small starting weights in the last layer of a block (D5 shows the arrangement in a transformer block). The shortcut also needs \(x\) and \(F(x)\) to have the same size. When a block changes the width, the shortcut passes through a small learned layer instead. He and others (2016) reported that residual learning made much deeper networks easier to optimise, up to 152 layers on ImageNet. In practice, nearly all very deep networks (image models with tens or hundreds of layers, and transformers) use residual connections. A common mistake is a shortcut that adds tensors of different sizes, which fails loudly, or one that skips the normalisation and lets the scale drift.

Common belief. A shortcut only adds a copy of the input, so it cannot change how the network learns.

Why it fails. The shortcut gives the slope a direct path back. At weight scale 0.6 the slope reaching block 0 is about \(10^{-7}\) of the output's without shortcuts and 6.0 times the output's with them.

Better account. The shortcut is a second path in the sum of B4. It also lets activations and slopes grow, so deep stacks add normalisation.

Debug tip. If a very deep plain stack does not train at all, add the shortcuts before changing anything else, and check the activation size at the last block. If the loss becomes NaN soon after, the activations are growing: lower the block weights or add normalisation.

Quick quiz

Why is the slope through a residual block a sum of two terms?

The block's input \(x\) is used twice: once added directly to the output and once inside \(F\). By the rule of B4, a value used twice gets the sum of the slopes along both paths: \(I\) and \(\partial F/\partial x\).

If all blocks of a 30-block residual stack have slope +0.1, what is the slope reaching block 0 compared with the output?

\(1.1^{29} \approx 15.9\). The slope grows, which is why shortcuts need small blocks or normalisation.

Dropout

Guess first. During training you switch off a hidden unit with probability 0.5. At test time you switch none off. Suppose you made no other change. Would the total input to the next layer at test time be about the same as in training, about twice as large, or about half?

dropout
setting a random share \(p\) of the hidden units to 0 on a training step
mask
the random 0-or-1 choice for a unit on one step
inverted dropout
multiplying the kept units by \(1/(1-p)\) during training, so that no change is needed at test time
thinned network
the smaller network that is left after a mask removes some units

Dropout trains a random sub-network that changes from step to step, and scaling the kept units by \(1/(1-p)\) keeps the average signal the same, so the full network can be used at test time.

With the \(1/(1-p)\) scaling, the average total input to a unit over 2,000 masks stays near its test-time value (1.27 against 1.28 at \(p = 0.5\)). Without scaling it falls to about half (0.63). The histogram shows the total input to one unit of the second hidden layer over 2,000 masks. The dashed coral line is its mean and the navy line is its value in the full network at test time. Switch the scaling off and the mean drops to about half of the navy line, because the kept units no longer make up for the dropped ones. The answer to the guess-first question follows: without scaling the test-time input is about twice the training input. With 12 hidden units there are \(2^{12} = 4096\) possible masks (0.4), so one network trains as a very large set of sub-networks that share their weights.

\[ \tilde a_i = \frac{m_i\, a_i}{1-p},\qquad m_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p) \qquad\qquad \mathbb{E}[\tilde a_i] = a_i,\quad \mathrm{Var}[\tilde a_i] = a_i^2\,\frac{p}{1-p} \]

  • \(a_i\) is the value of hidden unit \(i\) and \(m_i\) its mask: 1 (kept) with probability \(1-p\), 0 (dropped) with probability \(p\). \(\tilde a_i\) is the value passed on in training.
  • At test time \(m_i\) is always 1 and the factor \(1/(1-p)\) is left out, so the full network uses \(a_i\) itself.

Why the average is right. With probability \(1-p\) the unit passes \(a/(1-p)\), and with probability \(p\) it passes 0. The mean is \((1-p)\cdot\tfrac{a}{1-p} + p\cdot 0 = a\). The variance is \((1-p)\big(\tfrac{a}{1-p} - a\big)^2 + p\,a^2 = a^2\tfrac{p^2}{1-p} + p\,a^2 = a^2\tfrac{p}{1-p}\), the noise that dropout adds. Take a unit with \(a = 0.8\). With \(p = 0.5\) it passes 1.6 half the time and 0 half the time: mean 0.8, variance \(0.64 = 0.8^2\). With \(p = 0.1\) it passes 0.889 nine times in ten and 0 once in ten: mean 0.8, variance \(0.8^2 \times 0.1/0.9 = 0.071\). A small \(p\) adds little noise, and \(p = 0.5\) adds as much noise as signal in a unit. The code confirms 0.8 and 0.64 on 10,000 draws. The total input to a unit in the next layer is a weighted sum of such values, so its mean is also unchanged, which is what the figure shows.

torch.manual_seed(1)
drop = nn.Dropout(p=0.5)
a = torch.full((8,), 0.8)
print("training:", drop(a))

drop.eval()
print("eval:    ", drop(a))
drop.train()

Analogy. A team of many small networks, trained on random subsets of units, whose answers are averaged at test time. The original dropout paper (Srivastava and others, 2014) describes it this way.

Where it breaks. The sub-networks are not independent models. They share all their weights, and a full network with scaled weights only approximates the average of their predictions. The picture also hides why dropout helps: a unit cannot rely on a particular partner being present, so it has to learn features that work with many different partners.

In practice. Common values of \(p\) run from 0.1 to 0.5, and the largest are used on wide fully connected layers. Dropout helps most when the training set is small for the size of the network, as with a table of 600 records. It is often reduced or switched off when data is plentiful. It slows training because a step sees a noisier network, and it can interact badly with batch norm, since dropped units shift the variance that batch norm estimates. A common mistake is to leave the model in training mode at test time: the predictions are then random, and the same shark gets different answers on different runs.

Debug tip. If a model's test accuracy changes from run to run on fixed data, check model.eval() first. If training loss is much higher than expected and does not fall, try \(p = 0\): dropout may be too strong for a small network.

Quick quiz

Why multiply the kept units by \(1/(1-p)\) during training?

To keep the average input to the next layer equal to what the full network produces at test time. Without it, the next layer would see about \((1-p)\) times the test-time signal in training.

With \(p = 0.2\) and a unit value of 1.0, what are the mean and variance of what the unit passes on?

Mean 1.0. Variance \(1.0^2 \times 0.2/0.8 = 0.25\).

Try these after two days and again after a week, before you open the answers.

In B4, a chain of 30 ReLU layers with weight 0.8 gave a slope of 0.0015 at layer 1. Which fix in this chapter changes that factor, and which one adds a second path?

Choosing the spread (He) moves the weights toward a factor of 1 per layer. A residual connection adds a second path through the identity, so the factor per block is \(1 + f\) rather than \(f\).

Standardising the inputs (0.7, A2) and layer norm do the same thing to different numbers. What is the difference, and why does a hidden layer need its own?

Standardising rescales the input columns once, using the training set. A hidden layer's values change whenever the earlier weights change, so they have to be rescaled again at all steps, from numbers computed as the network runs.

Adam's \(v\) (B5) tracks the squared slope of a weight. If activations explode by a factor of \(10^{6}\), what happens to \(v\) for the weights of that layer, and to Adam's step?

The slope is large, so \(v\) is large, and the step \(\eta\,\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}\) stays about \(\eta\). Adam is scale-free in the slope, so the step size does not explode. The exploded activations still break the forward pass, which Adam cannot repair.
