Decision trees

Grow a tree one question at a time, and find the depth where more questions stop helping.

A decision tree sorts examples into boxes by asking yes/no questions about measurements. In this chapter you grow one by hand on the SharkCam catch table and watch the computer search for the best question. You also see why a very deep tree memorises the training sharks.

SharkCam uses the question "is this shark a spadenose?", asked from a shark's length and the depth where it was found. The tree is also the building block of the boosted models in A6.

You will need: recursion and trees from the toolkit, the idea of a model and a baseline, and the idea of a held-out set from A4. A short reminder appears where the held-out set is used.

The data. The sharks here come from the made-up SharkCam catch table: 600 records generated from published length-weight relationships, not field data. Sharks whose tag number ends in 0, 1 or 2 (164 sharks) are held out and never used to grow a tree. The other 436 sharks, 196 of them spadenose, are the training sharks.

Reading a tree

Guess first. A shark is 41 cm long and was found at 18 m. Is it a spadenose shark? If you could ask one question about the shark first, would you ask about its length or its depth?

decision tree
a chain of yes/no questions, where the answer to one question picks the next
split
one question about one measurement, such as "length at most 50.35 cm?"
leaf (box)
where a path of questions ends; on the plot it is a rectangle
call
the answer a leaf gives: the class most common among the training sharks in its box
depth
the most questions asked in a row on the longest path

A tree turns a shark's measurements into a path of yes/no answers, and the box where the path ends gives the call.

The questions cut the plot into four boxes with straight edges, and all sharks in a box get the same call. Drag the ring, or press a tag, and watch the highlighted path in the tree.

The tree was grown on the 436 training sharks and is only two questions deep. Coral diamonds are spadenose sharks and navy dots are other sharks (milk, bamboo, hammerhead and Ganges). The ring starts on Tag 3305, the Sassoon spadenose from the made-up table: 41 cm long, found at 18 m. It answers "yes" to "length ≤ 50.35 cm?" and "no" to "depth ≤ 5.65 m?", so it ends in box 2, where 89% of the training sharks are spadenose. The call is spadenose.

Now press Tag 4127, the juvenile bamboo shark from the Juhu rock pool. Its length is also under 50.35 cm, but it was found at 0.4 m, so it falls in box 1, which has no spadenose at all. Tag 5519, the young hammerhead, is longer than 59.85 cm and ends in box 4. The shallow-water question works in this table because the made-up dives in rock pools are shallow and mostly record bamboo sharks. A tree learns whatever pattern the table has, including quirks of how the table was made.

A tree is a recursive object, as in the toolkit chapter on recursion. Below the first question hangs a smaller tree for the sharks that said yes, and another for the sharks that said no. Predicting walks down one path, and the number of questions asked is at most the depth.

Analogy. A tree resembles the game of twenty questions. The answers narrow the set of animals one by one, and the last answer names one.

Where it breaks. In the game a person invents a new question while thinking about the animal. A tree may only ask "is this measurement at most some threshold?", and all its questions were fixed during training. The 89% is also only the share of training sharks in the box, and nobody has tested it as a chance for a new shark (A4).

Debug tip. Check how many training sharks are in the box that makes a call. A box with 3 sharks that "shows 100% spadenose" says much less than a box with 160 sharks at 89%.

Quick quiz

A shark is 70 cm long and was found at 3 m. Which box does it end in, and what is the call?

Box 4. It answers "no" to "length ≤ 50.35 cm?" and "no" to "length ≤ 59.85 cm?". The depth question is never asked, because the path never reaches it. The call is "other shark", and box 4 has 161 training sharks, none of them spadenose.

Why does the tree call all sharks in box 2 spadenose when 11% of the training sharks there are not?

The call is the majority class. A leaf gives one answer to all sharks in its box, so the sharks of the minority class in that box are called wrongly.

Scoring a question: Gini impurity

Guess first. Box A has 10 spadenose and 10 other sharks. Box B has 18 spadenose and 2 other sharks. Which box is "more mixed", and would you say it is twice as mixed or more?

impurity
how mixed a box is: 0 for a box with one class, larger for a more even mix
Gini impurity
\(G\): the chance of a wrong label when you pick a shark from the box and give it the label of another shark drawn at random from the same box
weighted impurity
the impurity of the two sides of a split, both counted in proportion to their numbers of sharks
drop
the impurity of the box before the split minus the weighted impurity after it

A good question leaves two boxes that are purer than the box it started from, and Gini impurity puts a number on that.

The weighted impurity is lowest, at 0.144, with 9 sharks on the yes side, because that side is then pure. The divider starts at 12 sharks, with a weighted impurity of 0.215. Can you find a better position?

The row shows 24 training sharks under 100 cm, drawn at random and placed by length. Drag the divider, or move the slider, and read the equation boxes. On the right, the curve shows \(G\) for any possible share of spadenose in a box. It is 0 for a box with only one class and reaches 0.5 for an even mix. The black dot is the box before the split, and the two coloured dots are the two sides after it.

The dashed line joins the two sides. The weighted impurity after the split is the point on that line directly under the black dot, because the weights are the sizes of the sides. The coral bar is the drop. The curve bends downward, so that point can never lie above the curve. A split therefore never raises the weighted impurity. It lowers it unless both sides have the same share as the box did.

Here is the same arithmetic for the first question of the shark tree, "length at most 50.35 cm?", on all 436 training sharks. Both sides happen to have 218 sharks.

G =1whole−0.303other share 240/436, squared−0.202spadenose share 196/436, squared=0.495G before
0.5yes side, 218 of 436×0.294G, yes side (39 other, 179 spadenose)+0.5no side, 218 of 436×0.144G, no side (201 other, 17 spadenose)=0.219G after⇒0.276drop

\[ G = 1 - \sum_{k} p_k^{2}, \qquad G_{\text{after}} = \frac{n_L}{n}\,G_L + \frac{n_R}{n}\,G_R, \qquad \text{drop} = G_{\text{before}} - G_{\text{after}} \]

  • \(p_k\) is the share of class \(k\) in the box. With two classes, \(G = 1 - p_0^2 - p_1^2 = 2p_1(1-p_1)\).
  • \(n\) is the number of sharks in the box. \(n_L\) and \(n_R\) are the numbers that answer yes (left) and no (right).
  • \(G_L\) and \(G_R\) are the impurities of the yes side and the no side, found with the same formula.

Common belief. The best question is the one that gets the most sharks right.

Why it fails. Take box 2 of the first figure, with 22 other and 179 spadenose training sharks. The question "length at most 42.65 cm?" sends 4 other and 130 spadenose sharks to the yes side, and 18 other and 49 spadenose to the no side. Both sides call spadenose, so the split leaves the number of right answers at 179 of 201. Yet the weighted Gini falls from 0.195 to 0.170, because the yes side became almost pure.

Better account. Gini notices progress that accuracy cannot yet see. A split that changes no call can still set up purer boxes for the questions that follow.

Debug tip. If a split gives a drop of 0 or close to it, check the threshold. A cut at the very edge of the range sends nearly all sharks to one side, so the weighted Gini stays at its old value.

Used in: Gini impurity is the score the greedy search lowers in the next section. Boosted trees in A6 use a different score, built from the leftover errors, but the splitting search is the same.

Quick quiz

What is \(G\) for a box with 5 spadenose and 5 other sharks, and for a box with only spadenose?

For 5 and 5: \(1 - 0.5^2 - 0.5^2 = 0.5\). For a box with one class: \(1 - 1^2 - 0^2 = 0\).

Why is the weighted impurity after a split never above the impurity before it?

The Gini curve bends downward. The weighted average of the two sides is a point on the straight line between them, and that line stays on or below the curve at the box's own share.

Growing a tree: the greedy search

Guess first. To find the best question about length for the 436 training sharks, the computer tries cut points between neighbouring lengths. About how many cut points: 5, 50, 300 or 4,000? And would you cut length first, or depth?

threshold
the number in a question, such as 50.35 in "length at most 50.35 cm?"
scan
trying all thresholds of a measurement, in order, and scoring them as it goes
greedy
choosing the best question for the box in front of you, without looking ahead
depth limit
a cap on the number of questions in a row, set so that a tree stops growing

A tree is grown greedily: for one box, try all thresholds of all measurements, keep the one with the lowest weighted Gini, and repeat inside the two new boxes.

Scanning all thresholds of length picks 50.35 cm for the first question, and scanning depth first would pick 5.65 m with a much worse score. Grow a tree yourself, then press the button and compare your weighted Gini with the computer's.

Start with one box that has all 436 training sharks. Choose a measurement, drag the line, and read the three rows of boxes under the plot. When you like the line, press "Split here". Click any box, on the plot or in the tree, to pick the next one to split. The small charts score all thresholds in the chosen box. Their lowest point is the best question for that measurement.

Now press "Let the computer choose". It sweeps through the thresholds of length, then of depth, and draws their scores. At the end it moves to the lowest point of either chart. For the first box, that is "length ≤ 50.35 cm" with a weighted Gini of 0.219. Try the exercise in the caption: can you beat the computer's total score for four boxes, shown at the bottom of the figure? Greedy search is not guaranteed to find the best possible tree, so a patient reader sometimes can.

How does the computer try 300 thresholds so fast? It sorts the lengths once. A cut between neighbouring lengths is a position in the sorted row, and moving the cut one place to the right changes only one shark's side. So the counts on both sides update with a running total, and a threshold costs one small calculation. The total work for one measurement is about the cost of the sort, \(n \log n\) steps (toolkit chapter on Big-O). Cuts between equal lengths are skipped, because no question can separate two sharks of the same length. The 436 training sharks have 305 different lengths, so there are 304 cut points.

\[ (j^{*}, t^{*}) = \arg\min_{j,\,t}\; \Big(\tfrac{n_L}{n}\,G_L + \tfrac{n_R}{n}\,G_R\Big) \]

  • \(j\) is the measurement to ask about (length or depth) and \(t\) the threshold.
  • \(j^{*}, t^{*}\) are the winning choices: the pair with the lowest weighted Gini over all measurements and all thresholds.
  • The terms inside the bracket are the weighted impurity of the yes side and the no side, as in the last section.

Here is the scan in NumPy, on ten made-up sharks. The function sorts the lengths, keeps running counts of spadenose on the yes side, and scores all cuts at once.

import numpy as np

def gini(n0, n1):
    n = n0 + n1
    return 1 - (n0 / n) ** 2 - (n1 / n) ** 2

def best_split(x, y):
    order = np.argsort(x)
    x, y = x[order], y[order]
    n = len(y)
    n1_left = np.cumsum(y)[:-1]             # spadenose among the first i sharks
    n_left = np.arange(1, n)                # size of the yes side for each cut
    n1_right = y.sum() - n1_left
    n_right = n - n_left
    g_left = 1 - (n1_left / n_left) ** 2 - (1 - n1_left / n_left) ** 2
    g_right = 1 - (n1_right / n_right) ** 2 - (1 - n1_right / n_right) ** 2
    score = (n_left * g_left + n_right * g_right) / n
    score[x[1:] == x[:-1]] = np.inf         # no cut between equal values
    i = score.argmin()
    return (x[i] + x[i + 1]) / 2, score[i]

# ten sharks, made up for this example
length = np.array([36.0, 41.0, 44.4, 47.2, 52.0, 55.3, 61.8, 64.3, 70.1, 82.0])
spade  = np.array([1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0])
t, s = best_split(length, spade)
print(round(float(t), 2), round(float(s), 3))
print(round(gini(5, 5), 3))

The best cut is at 49.6 cm. It puts four spadenose on the yes side, and the one spadenose in the other six sharks leaves the no side mixed. The weighted Gini drops from 0.5 to 0.167. Now the same function on the training table:

import pandas as pd                                   # best_split and gini come from the block above
df = pd.read_csv("catch.csv")                         # made-up records
train = df[df.record_id % 10 >= 3]                    # tags ending in 0, 1, 2 are held out
y = (train.species == "spadenose").to_numpy().astype(int)
print(len(y), y.sum(), round(gini(len(y) - y.sum(), y.sum()), 3))
for col in ["length_cm", "depth_m"]:
    t, s = best_split(train[col].to_numpy(), y)
    print(col, round(float(t), 2), round(float(s), 4))

depth_m 5.65 0.4745

Length wins with 0.2188 against 0.4745, as in the figure. After the first split, the same search is run again in both new boxes, and again in their children. That is recursion (0.2): the base case is a box that is pure, too small, or at the depth limit.

Common belief. The computer finds the best possible tree.

Why it fails. It finds the best next question and never looks ahead. Take four points with the pattern XOR from the logic section, where two measurements matter only together. A first question of any kind leaves both sides as mixed as the box before it, so the search has no reason to prefer one. Two questions in a row would separate the points perfectly.

Better account. Greedy search is fast and usually good. Check its tree on held-out sharks, and expect that a better tree may exist.

Debug tip. A split that scores well because one side has a single shark is a trap. A box of one shark is always pure, and it says little about the next shark. Set a smallest box size, as in the next section, before trusting a deep tree.

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text
df = pd.read_csv("catch.csv")
df["spadenose"] = (df.species == "spadenose").astype(int)
held = df.record_id % 10 < 3
X = ["length_cm", "depth_m"]
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=0).fit(df[~held][X], df[~held].spadenose)
print(export_text(tree, feature_names=X))
print("train", round(tree.score(df[~held][X], df[~held].spadenose), 4), "held-out", round(tree.score(df[held][X], df[held].spadenose), 4))

Quick quiz

Why does a tree scan thresholds only between neighbouring different values?

Any threshold between the same two neighbouring values gives the same two groups. A cut between two equal values cannot separate them at all.

The first split gives a weighted Gini of 0.219 and the best second split gives 0.15. What did the second split improve, and is the search done?

It lowered the weighted Gini of the whole tree by further splitting one box. The search is done only when the depth limit, the smallest box size, or purity stops it. A box that is already pure needs no more questions.

Depth, memorising and held-out accuracy

Guess first. You let the tree ask 12 questions in a row instead of 2. Will it do better on sharks it has never seen? What do you expect its accuracy to be on the sharks it trained on?

held-out accuracy
the share of sharks right among sharks the tree never saw while growing (A4)
overfitting
fitting the quirks of the training sharks, so that training accuracy climbs while held-out accuracy does not (A4)
smallest box
the fewest training sharks a box may have; a larger number stops the tree from making tiny boxes

A deeper tree fits the training sharks better and the held-out sharks no better, because the extra boxes capture noise.

Training accuracy climbs from 91% at depth 2 to 99.5% at depth 12, while held-out accuracy stays between 83% and 86%. Press Play, then raise the smallest box size and watch the training curve fall back.

With no depth limit the tree grows to 51 leaves for 436 training sharks, so several boxes have only one or two sharks. A box of one shark is right about that shark by construction. The faint coral lines show the held-out accuracy of the same recipe on 20 other random splits of the 600 sharks into training and held-out sets. The dashed line is their average. They rise steeply from depth 0 to 2 and then drift sideways. The gap between the training and held-out lines is the sign of overfitting. Here it grows from 5 points at depth 2 to about 15 points at depth 12.

One held-out set of 164 sharks is also a noisy referee. The held-out accuracy at depth 2 is 86.0%, and the sampling noise on that number is about 2.7 points (0.7):

0.027standard error=√(0.86accuracy p×0.141 − p÷164held-out sharks)

So the curves at depth 2, 3, 4 and 5 are the same within noise. Do not read a gap of half a point between two depths as a finding. Choose a depth from the curves, take the simplest tree on the plateau, and test it once on data that played no part in the choice.

Trees also need no feature scaling. A question compares one measurement with one threshold. If length were written in inches, all thresholds would be divided by 2.54 and no shark would change boxes. This differs from the models of A2, where the scale of a feature changes how fast gradient descent moves.

Common belief. A tree with 100% training accuracy is a perfect model.

Why it fails. The deepest tree is right about all but one of its 436 training sharks, and wrong about roughly one in seven new ones. It memorised the training sharks, with the quirks of their lengths and depths, instead of learning what separates the species.

Better account. Only held-out accuracy tells you how the tree will do on new sharks, and a baseline tells you whether that number is good (A1). Here the lazy answer "other shark" is right 56% of the time.

Debug tip. When training accuracy is near 100% and held-out accuracy is far lower, cut the depth, raise the smallest box size, or both. Then compare the gap again.

Quick quiz

The tree has 99.8% training accuracy and 85% held-out accuracy. What is the gap, and what does it suggest?

About 15 points. It suggests overfitting: many small boxes that capture noise in the training sharks.

Why are depth 3 and depth 5 hard to tell apart in the figure?

Their held-out accuracies differ by less than the noise of a 164-shark held-out set, which is about 2.7 points.

Trees that predict a number

Guess first. A tree predicts a spadenose shark's weight from its length. Sketch what its graph looks like. Is it a line, a curve, or something else?

regression tree
a tree whose leaves give a number instead of a class
step function
a graph that is flat in pieces and jumps between them
squared error
the squared gap between a prediction and the true value, as in A2

A regression tree answers with the average weight of the training sharks in a box, so its graph is a staircase.

A depth-3 tree with 8 steps predicts weight far better than the average and a little better than a straight line. It goes flat beyond the longest shark it saw. Slide the depth, then switch on the straight line and the rule behind the data.

The figure uses the 196 spadenose sharks in the training sharks and 72 held-out spadenose sharks. The tree is grown by the same search as before, with two changes. The score of a box is its squared error around the box's average, and a leaf answers with that average. A good first question makes the squared error of the two halves added together much smaller than the squared error of the whole. The two rows of boxes below the plot show both numbers.

At depth 3 the tree has an error of 0.036 kg on its training sharks and 0.045 kg on held-out sharks. Always answering with the average weight gives 0.134 kg and 0.158 kg, and a straight line (A2) gives 0.047 kg and 0.048 kg. Deeper trees keep lowering the training error and stop helping the held-out error after depth 4, the same pattern as in the last section.

Move the "ask the tree" slider to 70 cm. The made-up table has no spadenose shark above 60 cm, so the tree repeats the last step, about 0.70 kg. The rule that made the data, \(W = 0.0044\,L^{2.923}\) grams, gives about 1.1 kg. A tree cannot extend a trend beyond its data, which is a weakness a straight line or a formula does not share.

\[ \text{leaf value} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\,\in\,\text{box}} y_i, \qquad \text{error of a box} = \sum_{i\,\in\,\text{box}} \big(y_i - \text{leaf value}\big)^{2} \]

  • \(y_i\) is the weight of training shark \(i\), and \(n\) the number of training sharks in the box.
  • The leaf value is the average of those weights. The best single number for a box under squared error is its average.
  • The search picks the question that makes the error of the two new boxes, added together, as small as possible.

Analogy. A regression tree is a staircase laid against a smooth hill. More steps follow the hill more closely.

Where it breaks. A staircase has a last step. Beyond the last training shark the tree has no slope, so it says the same weight for a 60 cm and an 80 cm shark.

Debug tip. If a regression tree has many steps and the held-out error is much higher than the training error, plot the steps against the held-out points. Steps that follow single noisy sharks show that the smallest box size is too low.

Used in: In A6, small regression trees are added one after another to fit what the earlier trees missed. That is boosting.

Quick quiz

What does a leaf of a regression tree answer, and why that number?

The average of the training values in its box, because the average is the single number with the smallest squared error for those values.

Why does the tree's line go flat at 80 cm?

No training shark was longer than about 60 cm, so the last box covers all lengths above its threshold and answers with one average.

Exercises

Theory

  1. theory A box has 12 spadenose and 4 other sharks. A question sends 10 spadenose and 1 other shark to the yes side, and the rest to the no side. Find \(G\) before, \(G\) of both sides, the weighted \(G\) after, and the drop.

    Hint

    Write the shares \(p_0\) and \(p_1\) of all three boxes first. The weights are \(n_L/n\) and \(n_R/n\).

  2. theory For two classes show that \(G = 2p(1-p)\), find the value of \(p\) where \(G\) is largest, and give that largest value. Then explain in two sentences why a split can never raise the weighted \(G\).

    Hint

    Expand \(1 - p^2 - (1-p)^2\). For the second part, think about where a weighted average of two points on a downward-bending curve lies.

  3. theory For \(n\) training sharks a computer scores all cut points of one measurement. Compare two methods for scoring all cuts: recounting the classes on both sides from scratch for the cut being tried, and keeping running counts. How many steps does the first need, and how many the second, as functions of \(n\), once the sharks are sorted?

  4. theory Four points lie at the corners of a square: (0, 0) and (1, 1) are class 0, and (0, 1) and (1, 0) are class 1. Show that any first question leaves the weighted \(G\) at 0.5. Then show that a depth-2 tree gives \(G = 0\).

Come back later

Try these after two days, and again after a week. Answer first, then open the box.

A tree's held-out accuracy is 86.0% on 164 sharks. About how far could the true accuracy be from that number, and which toolkit idea tells you? (0.7)

About 2.7 points either way for one standard error, from \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). The same sampling noise that makes a few coin flips unreliable makes a small held-out set unreliable.

Growing a tree is recursive. What are the base cases, and how is that like the function total(n) in 0.2?

Stop when a box is pure, when it has too few sharks, or when the depth limit is reached. These are the cases answered directly, as total(0) is. The recursive case splits the box and grows a smaller tree in both halves.

The path to box 2 is "length ≤ 50.35 AND depth > 5.65". Which logic operator joins the questions on a path, and which joins the paths that make the same call? (0.4)

AND joins the questions along one path. OR joins the different paths that end in boxes with the same call. A tree is a set of AND rules combined with OR.
