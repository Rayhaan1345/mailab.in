Self-attention

How a token collects what it needs from the tokens before it

A word rarely means much alone. In “We photographed it and put it back”, the word “it” points to a shark named earlier. Self-attention is the step that lets a token look at the tokens before it, decide how much the others matter, and take a blend of what they offer. This chapter builds it from a four-token example you can edit. Then it shows attention in GPT-2 heads on SharkCam sentences. It explains the causal mask that keeps a token from reading the future, and it puts many heads side by side.

The sentences are written for this primer. The attention weights come from GPT-2 small (MIT licence), computed offline. The vectors in the small examples are made up. The idea is from Vaswani and others (2017). The figures follow the spirit of Jay Alammar's Illustrated Transformer and of the attention chapter in Grant Sanderson's 3Blue1Brown series.

You will need: the dot product and matrix products with transposes (0.5), softmax and variance (0.7), tokens (D1), embeddings (D2) and the context window (D3).

What a token needs from the others

Guess first. In the sentence below the writer reaches the second “Tag” and is about to write the number again. Which earlier token would help a model most to guess the next token: “young”, “We” or “▁4” (the first piece of 4127)? Why?

attention
a step in which a token takes a weighted blend of information from other tokens
self-attention
attention in which the tokens of one text read one another
attention weight
a number from 0 to 1 that says how much a token reads from another; the weights of one token add up to 1
head
one complete attention computation; GPT-2 small has 12 per layer

Attention lets a token read from the tokens before it. It gives them weights and takes the blend of what they offer.

In head L5H5 the second ▁Tag puts 94% of its weight on ▁4, the token that followed the first Tag. Most other tokens in this head rest on the first token. The mark ▁ means a leading space (D1). Weights are from GPT-2 small, computed offline.

Click a token. Lines run from it to the earlier tokens it reads, thicker for more weight, and the numbers are the weights. Tokens below it are faded because it cannot read them (the causal mask, section 5). A name such as L5H5 means layer 5, head 5, counting from 0. Switch the head and compare. L4H11 looks at the token just before. L0H1 looks mostly at the token itself. L1H10 spreads its weight over the neighbours.

The first token often gets a large share even where it has no useful content. A head that finds no match still has to put its weights somewhere, because they must add up to 1. Xiao and others (2024) report that models place such weight on the first tokens, and they call these tokens attention sinks. L5H5 has exceptions to this habit. When a token has appeared before, the head reads the token that came after the earlier copy.

Olsson and others (2022) describe an induction head in two parts. It attends to the token that followed an earlier copy of the current token, and its output makes that token more likely. We tested only the first part, and the test is our own. We drew 20 random sequences of 40 tokens and placed two copies of the sequence one after the other. For the tokens of the second copy, we averaged the weight on the token after the earlier copy. L5H5 scores 0.93. The other three heads of this chapter score 0.00 to 0.01. So L5H5 matches the pattern of an induction head. We did not test the second part, and we did not remove the head to see what changes. Such a head could help a model repeat a tag number it has seen.

Common belief. Attention weights show what the model is thinking about, so a large weight means that token explains the answer.

Why it fails. A weight says how much one head reads from a token at one step. Many large weights fall on the first token, which says little about the sentence. Later layers also change what a head read before the answer appears. Jain and Wallace (2019) found that attention weights often do not match other measures of which inputs matter. Wiegreffe and Pinter (2019) replied that the answer depends on what you mean by explanation.

Better account. The weights describe one step of the computation. To say what a model used, change the input or the head and watch the output (B9).

Quick quiz

The second ▁Tag gives 94% to ▁4. What do all its weights add up to, and where is the rest?

They add up to 100%. The remaining 6% is shared by the other tokens it can read, mostly the first token.

Why are the tokens below the clicked one faded?

A token reads itself and the tokens before it, so later tokens get no weight. The reason is in section 5.

Queries, keys and values

Guess first. A token has one vector, its embedding. Attention asks it to do three jobs: say what it needs, show a label for what it offers, and hand over its content. Would one vector do all three jobs well, or should the model make three different vectors from it?

query \(\mathbf{q}\)
the vector a token uses to search; what it looks for
key \(\mathbf{k}\)
the vector a token shows to the searches of others, like the label on a folder
value \(\mathbf{v}\)
the vector a token hands over when it is read
projection
a vector made from another by multiplying with a learned matrix (0.5)
key size \(d_k\)
the number of entries in \(\mathbf{q}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\); 64 in GPT-2 small

The embedding \(\mathbf{x}\) of a token is turned into three vectors by three learned matrices: \(\mathbf{q} = W_Q\mathbf{x}\), \(\mathbf{k} = W_K\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = W_V\mathbf{x}\).

One embedding gives three different vectors, and a change in the embedding changes all three. Here \(\mathbf{x}\) has 3 numbers and \(\mathbf{q}\), \(\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) have 2. The embeddings and the matrices are made up.

Pick a token and a role. The boxes show the working: the two output numbers are dot products of a row of the matrix with the embedding (0.5). Move a slider and see which outputs change. The table lists all four tokens, so you can see that one set of matrices gives different \(\mathbf{q}\), \(\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) to different tokens.

Why not use \(\mathbf{x}\) itself for all three jobs? Then a token's score with itself would be \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}\), the squared length of \(\mathbf{x}\). Its score with another token would be at most the product of the two lengths. A token would favour itself and its lookalikes. It could not ask a question such as “which earlier word names an animal?”. Separate matrices make the question, the label and the content three different things.

In GPT-2 small the embedding has 768 numbers. A head uses \(\mathbf{q}\), \(\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) of 64 numbers, and 12 heads run side by side (section 6). The software stores the matrices of a layer as one block of 768 rows and 2,304 columns, since \(3 \times 12 \times 64 = 2{,}304\). The matrices are learned by backpropagation like other weights (B4).

Analogy. A query is a search term, a key is the label on a folder and a value is the folder's content.

Where it breaks. Nobody writes the labels. The model learns \(W_Q\), \(W_K\) and \(W_V\) from the loss, so a “label” is whatever helped predict tokens, and it rarely has a neat name. A paper folder also keeps one label. Here a token's key and value are two separate vectors, and both are computed again in all layers.

Debug tip. The score \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}\) needs \(\mathbf{q}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\) of the same length \(d_k\). The value \(\mathbf{v}\) may have another length \(d_v\), and the output has the length of \(\mathbf{v}\). When a matrix product fails with a size error, print the sizes of all three first.

Quick quiz

A token has \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 0.5, 1)\) and \(W_Q\) has the rows \((1, 0, 1)\) and \((0, 1, 0)\). What is \(\mathbf{q}\)?

The first number is \(1\times 1 + 0 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 1 = 2\). The second is \(0 + 0.5 + 0 = 0.5\). So \(\mathbf{q} = (2, 0.5)\).

Why does a token need a key and a value as two separate vectors?

The key is used only to compute weights, by comparison with queries. The value is what gets blended into the output. A token can be easy to find and still offer something that differs from its label.

Scores, softmax and the weighted sum

Guess first. The token “it” has a query. Its dot products with the keys of “the”, “young”, “hammerhead” and “it” are 0.11, 1.41, 1.98 and 0.80. About what share of its weight goes to “hammerhead”: 25%, 40% or 70%?

score \(s\)
the dot product of a query and a key, divided by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)
softmax
turns a row of scores into positive weights that add up to 1 (0.7)
attention weight \(a\)
the softmax of the scores of one query
weighted sum
add up the values \(\mathbf{v}\) after multiplying them by their weights

The output of a token is a blend of the values it can read. The blend weights are the softmax of its query-key scores.

Queries and keys: drag the tips

Values: the output is their blend

Moving the query of “it” toward the key of “young” shifts weight from “hammerhead” to “young”, and the output arrow follows the values. The dotted pieces, which are a weight times a value, lie end to end and add up to the output. All vectors are made up, with \(d_k = 2\).

Read the figure from left to right. The coral arrow is the query of the chosen token and the navy arrows are the keys. A dot product is large when two arrows point the same way and are long (0.5). The table gives the dot product, the score after dividing by \(\sqrt{2}\), and the softmax weight. The boxes below show the working for the row you click. The default answer to the question above is 42%, so the guess of 40% was the closest.

Now a task. Drag the query of “it” until “young” has the largest weight. Which way did you move it? When the query points between two keys, the longer key of “hammerhead” still wins, because length counts as well as direction. For a second worked case, choose the query of “young”. It can read only “the” and itself, and its query is \((0.4, 0.5)\). The dot products are \(0.4\times0.3 + 0.5\times(-0.5) = -0.13\) and \(0.4\times0.8 + 0.5\times0.9 = 0.77\). Divided by 1.414 these are \(-0.09\) and \(0.54\). Then \(e^{-0.09} = 0.91\) and \(e^{0.54} = 1.72\), which sum to 2.63, so the weights are 35% and 65%. The output is \(0.35\times(0.2, 0.9) + 0.65\times(-0.8, 0.5) = (-0.45, 0.64)\), the same as the figure.

Softmax makes the weights positive and gives them a sum of 1. Only differences between scores matter: adding 5 to all the scores leaves the weights unchanged (0.7). This is why a token's weights always add up to 1, even when no key matches its query.

\[ s_{ij} = \frac{\mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}},\qquad a_{ij} = \frac{e^{s_{ij}}}{\sum_{j'\le i} e^{s_{ij'}}},\qquad \mathbf{o}_i = \sum_{j\le i} a_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_j \]

  • \(s_{ij}\) is the score of token \(i\) for token \(j\). \(\mathbf{q}_i\) is the query of token \(i\), \(\mathbf{k}_j\) the key of token \(j\), and \(d_k\) the key size.
  • \(a_{ij}\) is the weight token \(i\) gives to token \(j\): between 0 and 1, and the weights of one \(i\) add up to 1. The sum runs over \(j'\le i\), the tokens that \(i\) may read.
  • \(\mathbf{o}_i\) is the output of token \(i\): a vector with the length of \(\mathbf{v}\). The next chapter adds it to the token's running vector.

Analogy. A Python dictionary (0.2) looks up a key and returns its value. Attention is a dictionary that returns a blend of all its values, weighted by how well its key matches the query.

Where it breaks. A dictionary key must equal the query, and the dictionary returns that one value or reports a miss. Attention never says “not found”: the weights always add up to 1, so it returns a blend even when no key matches. The blend also cannot be taken apart afterwards, so the output does not tell you which value contributed what.

The same steps in NumPy, with the numbers of the figure. The code blocks of this page share their variables, so press Run on them from the top.

import numpy as np
# keys and values of: the, young, hammerhead, it
K = np.array([[0.3, -0.5], [0.8, 0.9], [1.4, 0.6], [0.5, 0.4]])
V = np.array([[0.2, 0.9], [-0.8, 0.5], [1.1, -0.2], [0.3, -0.7]])
q = np.array([1.2, 0.5])               # query of "it"
d_k = len(q)

scores = K @ q / np.sqrt(d_k)          # one score per key
w = np.exp(scores - scores.max())
w = w / w.sum()                        # softmax
out = w @ V                            # weighted sum of the values
print("scores ", scores.round(3))
print("weights", w.round(3), w.sum().round(3))
print("output ", out.round(3))
scores  [0.078 0.997 1.4   0.566]
weights [0.113 0.282 0.422 0.183] 1.0
output  [0.316 0.03 ]

Debug tip. Print the weights of one token and add them up. They must add up to 1 along a row. If the columns add up to 1 instead, softmax ran over the wrong axis (axis=0 where a row needs axis=-1). The output still looks plausible, so this bug is easy to miss.

Quick quiz

Scores 2, 2 and 3 for three keys. What are the weights?

\(e^2 = 7.39\) twice and \(e^3 = 20.09\), which sum to 34.87. The weights are 0.212, 0.212 and 0.576.

The weights are 0.5 and 0.5 and the values are (2, 0) and (0, 4). What is the output?

\(0.5\times(2,0) + 0.5\times(0,4) = (1, 2)\), halfway between the two values.

Why the scores are scaled

Guess first. A query and a key are two lists of 64 random numbers with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is a sum of 64 products. About how large is a typical dot product: about 1, about 8 or about 64?

variance, spread
the average squared distance from the mean, and its square root (0.7)
saturated softmax
a softmax with one weight near 1 and the others near 0; its slopes are near 0 (B4)
scaling
dividing the dot products by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) before softmax

A dot product of \(d_k\) random numbers has a spread of \(\sqrt{d_k}\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the spread near 1 whatever the key size, so softmax stays soft.

At \(d_k = 64\) the unscaled scores have a spread of about 8 and the largest of 8 weights averages about 88%. After dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) the spread is about 1 and the largest weight stays between 34% and 38% from \(d_k = 4\) up to 1,024.

Switch the scaling off and slide \(d_k\) up. The histogram widens like \(\sqrt{d_k}\), and the bars on the right turn into a single tall bar. Where does the width come from? Take \(d_k = 64\). The score is \(q_1k_1 + q_2k_2 + \dots + q_{64}k_{64}\). A product of two independent numbers with mean 0 and variance 1 has mean 0 and variance \(1\times1 = 1\). Variances of independent terms add (0.7), so the sum has variance 64 and spread \(\sqrt{64} = 8\). Dividing by 8 divides the variance by 64, which is back to 1. The counting is the same as the one that fixed the weight spread of a layer in B6.

64terms in the sum, dk×1variance of one product=64variance of q·k→8spread, √64÷8√dk=1spread after scaling

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = \sum_{m=1}^{d_k}\operatorname{Var}(q_m k_m) = d_k\cdot 1\cdot 1 = d_k \]

  • \(q_m, k_m\) are the \(m\)-th numbers of the query and the key, assumed independent with mean 0 and variance 1 (the assumption in Vaswani and others, 2017).
  • \(\operatorname{Var}\) is the variance. The first equality follows because the variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances.

Why does a large spread hurt? Two scores that differ by 8 give weights in the ratio \(e^{8} = 2{,}981\) to 1. The top key takes almost all the weight. The slope of a softmax weight with respect to its own score is \(p(1-p)\). At \(p = 0.999\) it is 0.001, so the gradient hardly passes (B4), and training learns very little about the other keys. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is the same as softmax with temperature \(T = \sqrt{d_k}\) (D3), here \(T = 8\).

Common belief. Scaling forces attention to stay soft.

Why it fails. It fixes the spread of the scores only at the start, when the numbers are random. A trained model can learn larger queries and keys, and so sharper attention. In the heat map of the next section the scores of GPT-2 reach 9 in head L1H10 and 35 in head L4H11.

Better account. Scaling sets a good starting point, as Xavier and He initialisation do for layers (B6). Training can then move away from it where that helps.

Debug tip. Divide by the square root of the key size of one head (64 in GPT-2 small), not of the whole embedding (768). If a model's attention is nearly one-hot from the first step, or its loss turns into NaN, check the scale first. PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention applies \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) by default; the NumPy code in this chapter gives the same result (Practice 3).

Quick quiz

With \(d_k = 16\), what are the spread of \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}\) before and after scaling?

Before: \(\sqrt{16} = 4\). After dividing by 4: 1.

Two scores differ by 4. What is the ratio of their weights, and what is it if we had not divided by \(\sqrt{16}\) when the raw gap was 16?

\(e^{4} = 55\) to 1. With the raw gap 16 it would be \(e^{16} = 8.9\) million to 1.

All tokens at once, and the causal mask

Guess first. While GPT-2 learns from the text “We put it back”, it predicts the next word for all tokens in one pass. At the token “it” the next word, “back”, is already in the input. If attention may read it, what would the model learn, and what would happen when it must write a new report?

attention matrix
the table of \(n \times n\) weights; row \(i\) lists the weights of token \(i\)
causal mask
a rule that sets the scores of later tokens to \(-\infty\), so that a token reads only itself and earlier tokens
\(-\infty\)
a score smaller than any number; \(e^{-\infty} = 0\), so the weight is 0
leak
information about the answer reaching the model's input

One matrix product, \(QK^{\top}\), scores all pairs at once. The causal mask replaces the scores above the diagonal by \(-\infty\), so the softmax of a row ignores the later tokens.

With the mask, the rows of head L4H11 keep only the tokens up to the diagonal. Without it, the same scores would send 69% of a row's weight, on average, to later tokens in sentence 1. Step through the three stages, then switch the mask off. The model itself always runs with the mask, so the unmasked view shows what would leak, not what GPT-2 computes.

Stack the queries as the rows of a matrix \(Q\) with \(n\) rows and \(d_k\) columns, and the keys as the rows of \(K\). Then \(QK^{\top}\) has \(n\) rows and \(n\) columns, and its entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is \(\mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{k}_j\) (0.5). Divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), add the mask, run softmax along the rows and multiply by \(V\). That is the formula of the paper in one line.

\[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\Big)V,\qquad M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & j \le i\\ -\infty & j > i\end{cases} \]

  • \(Q, K, V\) have one row per token: the queries, keys and values. \(M\) is the mask: 0 where a token may read, \(-\infty\) where it may not.
  • Softmax runs along the rows. The result has one output row per token, so all tokens are handled in one pass.

The mask keeps \(n(n+1)/2\) of the \(n^2\) entries. For the sentence above, 210 of 400. For GPT-2's full window of 1,024 tokens, it is 524,800 per head, and 75,571,200 over the 144 heads. Doubling the length of a text makes the table four times as large. That is the \(n^2\) growth of 0.2, and D6 returns to it.

Now the question at the top. We trained two copies of a tiny model, with one attention head, on 400 made-up reports. One copy used the mask. The other did not.

Without the mask the model's training loss falls to 0.06, below the 0.28 of the masked model, because it reads the answer. On new reports with the mask on, as at generation time, it scores 2.49 against 0.30.

The unmasked model found that position \(t+1\) contains the label. Take the roughly 1,500 of 11,715 word positions where the masked model finds the next word hard (loss above 1 nat). There the unmasked model puts 39% of its weight on the next word, which is the answer itself. At generation time that word does not exist yet, so the model has learned a skill it cannot use and has not learned the skill it needs.

Common belief. The mask matters when we generate text, and training can do without it.

Why it fails. In training the whole text is known. Without the mask, the token at position \(t\) can read position \(t+1\), which is the label. The loss falls for the wrong reason, as the figure shows.

Better account. The mask makes training match use: at any position the model sees only what it will see when it writes. Encoder models such as BERT drop the mask on purpose and change the task to avoid a leak (D5).

The same steps in NumPy. The first block is the whole of attention for a text, with the mask as an option. Its output for the four-token example matches the figure of section 3. Row 4 is the case worked there.

# queries of the four tokens
Q = np.array([[0.6, -0.2], [0.4, 0.5], [0.8, 0.2], [1.2, 0.5]])

def attention(Q, K, V, causal=True):
    n, d_k = Q.shape
    S = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)             # n x n table of scores
    if causal:
        keep = np.tril(np.ones((n, n), bool))
        S = np.where(keep, S, -np.inf)     # hide the future
    W = np.exp(S - S.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    W = W / W.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)   # softmax along the rows
    return W, W @ V

W, out = attention(Q, K, V)
print(W.round(3))
print(out.round(3))

Other common mask bugs are these. The mask is added after softmax, so the weights no longer add up to 1. The mask is transposed, which hides the past and shows the future. Or a padding mask that hides all the tokens of a row, which gives a row of NaN. Keep at least one visible token per row.

Quick quiz

Row 3 of the masked table is 0.209, 0.337, 0.454, 0. Why is the last entry 0, and why do the others still add up to 1?

Token 3 may not read token 4, so its score was set to \(-\infty\) and \(e^{-\infty}=0\). Softmax then runs over the three remaining scores, and those weights add up to 1.

A text has 6 tokens. How many entries of the score table does the mask keep?

\(6\times7/2 = 21\) of 36.

Many heads

Guess first. GPT-2 small has 144 heads: 12 per layer, in 12 layers. Do you expect them all to behave alike, or to differ widely? How would you tell?

head
one set of matrices \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) and the attention it computes
concatenate
join vectors end to end into one longer vector
output matrix \(W_O\)
a learned matrix that mixes the joined outputs of the heads
model width \(d\)
the length of a token's vector; 768 in GPT-2 small (D2)

A layer runs several heads in parallel on short vectors. It joins their outputs end to end and mixes them with \(W_O\).

The 144 heads differ widely. In our texts 29 of them put more than 80% of their weight on the first token, and L4H11 puts all of it on the previous token. Squares are heads, darker for a larger average. Pinned heads show their attention tables for the sentence with Tag 4127 twice.

Switch the measure and click squares to pin up to four heads. The tables show lower triangles, as the mask requires. L4H11 gives a bright line just below the diagonal. L0H1 gives a bright diagonal (press “Pin the four heads” to see it). L5H5 gives a bright first column, and a short bright diagonal of three cells near the end, for the repeated Tag 4127. L1H10 gives a soft blur. The names describe where the weights fall on these sentences. We chose these four because their patterns are easy to describe. Most of the 144 heads are not.

12heads, H×64numbers per head, dk=768joined vector, d×768 × 768output matrix WO=768mixed output

\[ \mathrm{head}_h = \mathrm{Attention}\big(XW_Q^{h},\,XW_K^{h},\,XW_V^{h}\big),\qquad \mathrm{MultiHead}(X) = \big[\mathrm{head}_1;\ \dots;\ \mathrm{head}_H\big]\,W_O \]

  • \(X\) is the matrix with the token vectors as rows (\(n\) rows, \(d\) columns). \(W_Q^{h}, W_K^{h}, W_V^{h}\) are the matrices of head \(h\), all with \(d\) rows and \(d_k\) columns.
  • \(H\) is the number of heads and \(d_k = d/H\). The square brackets with semicolons join the head outputs end to end. \(W_O\) has \(d\) rows and \(d\) columns.

Count the weights. One head has \(3 \times 768 \times 64 = 147{,}456\) in its three matrices. Twelve heads have \(1{,}769{,}472\), and \(W_O\) adds \(768^2 = 589{,}824\), so a layer has \(2{,}359{,}296 = 4\times768^2\) weights, plus 3,072 biases in GPT-2. The cost stays about the same: 12 heads of 64 numbers cost about as much as one head of 768 (Vaswani and others, 2017). Several heads give several blends of the same text at once. A token may need the previous token and an earlier twin together, and one head makes only one blend.

Common belief. All heads have a clear job, such as “subject”, “noun” or “previous word”.

Why it fails. The grid shows many heads that spend most of their weight on the first token. The sharp heads of this chapter are the easy cases, picked because they are clear. A name only says where the weight fell on a few sentences.

Better account. Heads are learned ways to blend. Some can be described, many cannot, and to say what a head does you must test it by changing it (B9).

Cost matters for the next chapters. Attention takes about \(n^2 d\) steps per layer (Vaswani and others, 2017, Table 1), so a longer text gets expensive quickly. D5 puts attention inside a transformer block, and D6 deals with long texts.

def multi_head(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, H):
    n, d = X.shape
    dk = d // H
    heads = []
    for h in range(H):
        sl = slice(h * dk, (h + 1) * dk)   # the columns of head h
        q, k, v = X @ Wq[:, sl], X @ Wk[:, sl], X @ Wv[:, sl]
        heads.append(attention(q, k, v)[1])
    return np.concatenate(heads, axis=1) @ Wo   # join, then mix

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = rng.normal(size=(4, 8))
Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo = (rng.normal(0, 0.3, (8, 8)) for _ in range(4))
out = multi_head(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, H=2)
print(X.shape, "->", out.shape, "with 2 heads of", 8 // 2, "numbers")
print("weights in the four matrices:", 4 * 8 * 8)
(4, 8) -> (4, 8) with 2 heads of 4 numbers
weights in the four matrices: 256

Quick quiz

A model has width 512 and 8 heads. What is \(d_k\), and what is the length of the joined vector?

\(d_k = 512/8 = 64\). The joined vector has \(8\times64 = 512\) numbers, the same as the width.

A head puts 95% of its weight on the first token for most tokens. Does it matter to the output?

Probably little for those tokens: the head returns about the value of the first token, which is the same for all of them. A few tokens may still matter, as L5H5 shows for repeated tags. To know, remove the head and watch the output.

Come back later

Try these after two days, and again after a week, without looking back.

Dividing the scores by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is a softmax temperature. Which \(T\) is it for \(d_k = 64\), and does it make the weights sharper or flatter than without scaling? (Chapter D3)

\(T = 8\). A higher temperature flattens the weights, so scaling makes them flatter than the raw scores would.

In a saturated softmax one weight is 0.999. What is its slope \(p(1-p)\), and why does that matter for training? (Chapter B4)

\(0.999 \times 0.001 \approx 0.001\). A slope this small multiplies the gradient that passes back through the softmax by about a thousandth, so the other keys learn very little.

In D2, “shark” and “whale” had a dot product of 22.27 in 50 numbers. If those vectors were a query and a key with \(d_k = 50\), what is the score? (Chapter D2)

\(22.27/\sqrt{50} = 22.27/7.07 = 3.15\). (A hypothetical case: an actual model makes \(\mathbf{q}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\) with learned matrices.)
