A model turns inputs into a prediction. Its parameters are the dials that decide which prediction. Supervised learning sets the dials from examples that come with answers. This chapter builds those ideas on the SharkCam catch records, then introduces the lazy model, the first score any model must beat. The chapters after it fit the dials (A2, A3), grade the result (A4) and try other kinds of rules (A5, A6).

All the sharks here are made up: records generated from published length-weight relationships, not field data.

You will need: functions and exponents, the mean and reading charts. The code blocks use a little NumPy.

A model is a rule with dials

Guess first. You want to estimate a spadenose shark's weight from its length, without a scale. A simple rule says: weight = a × length × length × length, where a is a number you can choose. A 41 cm shark has a length cubed of 68,921. Weights here are a fraction of a kilogram. Should a be about 0.0000033, about 0.033 or about 3.3?

model
a rule that turns inputs into a prediction
input (feature)
a number you give the model, such as a length
prediction \(\hat y\)
the model's output, its guess at the answer
parameter
a dial inside the model whose value is set from data

A model has a fixed form and adjustable parameters. The form says how inputs become a prediction. The parameters say which prediction.

Dial a: 7.00 millionthsAverage miss of the rule: 0Average miss of the lazy model: 0

Near a = 3.3 the rule misses by about 0.025 kg on average, against 0.113 kg for the lazy model. At a = 7 it does worse than the lazy model. That answers the guess: about 0.0000033, or 3.3 millionths.

The dots are made-up spadenose records. The teal curve is the model's prediction for any length. The thin grey lines show the miss for a shark, and the average miss is their mean length: it says how far off the model is in kilograms. Slide a and watch the curve swing. The coral ring is Tag 3305, a 41.0 cm adult. The boxes under the plot work out the prediction for it, step by step.

The lazy model is a dashed line. It ignores the length and says the average weight, 0.226 kg, for all sharks. It is the simplest model there is, and the next sections use it as a yardstick. A model with the dial at zero predicts 0 kg for all sharks. That does worse than the lazy model.

\[ \hat y = f(\mathbf{x};\,\theta) \qquad\text{here}\qquad \hat y = a\,L^{3} \]

  • \(f\) is the model: the form of the rule. \(\mathbf{x}\) is the input, here the length \(L\) in cm.
  • \(\theta\) (theta) is short for all the parameters together. This model has one, \(\theta = (a)\).
  • \(\hat y\) (read "y hat") is the prediction. The true answer is \(y\). A hat always marks a model's guess.

A person chose the form \(a L^3\), because weight grows like volume and volume grows like length cubed (0.3). Nobody chose the value of \(a\) by looking at single sharks. Its value comes from the data. That split of work is the core of machine learning: people choose the form, and data sets the parameters.

A second worked example. The figure tests the dial on 268 sharks. Here is the test by hand on three records from the code block below. Cube the length, multiply by the dial (in millionths, as in the figure), and compare with the weight. The miss is the gap with its sign dropped.

At 3.3 millionths the misses are small and similar. At 7 millionths the miss grows with the length, because a wrong dial multiplies the length cubed and the longest shark has the biggest cube.

Where the form breaks. The best dial still leaves an average miss of 0.025 kg. The exponent in these records is 2.92 (A2 measures it), so the cube is slightly too steep. For the 31 sharks of 50 cm or more, the rule at 3.3 millionths is too heavy by 0.032 kg on average. Moving the dial cannot repair a form that is a little wrong. The dial also belongs to one species. For the 82 cm hammerhead Tag 5519, 82 cubed is 551,368, and times 3.3 millionths gives 1.82 kg against a recorded 2.610 kg. The dial that fits Tag 5519 alone is 4.7 millionths.

In practice. Choose the form from what you know about the problem, start with the simplest one, and count its parameters. Write the model as a function with the parameters as arguments, as the code below does. A common mistake is to fit a dial on one group and apply it to another, as with Tag 5519.

Common belief. A model is a long list of rules that a programmer wrote, one per case.

Why it fails. Nobody wrote "a 41 cm spadenose weighs 0.225 kg". The model has one dial, and it gives an answer for lengths nobody recorded.

Better account. A programmer writes the form. The data sets the parameters. A change in the data changes the behaviour without any new code.

Debug tip. Parameters have units. The dial \(a\) here has units of kg per cm3. If the predictions are all about a thousand times too large or too small, look for grams mixed with kilograms.

import numpy as np

# 8 made-up spadenose records: length in cm, weight in kg
length = np.array([18.2, 29.2, 33.2, 36.5, 39.4, 42.8, 46.4, 51.3])
weight = np.array([0.018, 0.066, 0.128, 0.169, 0.167, 0.287, 0.297, 0.413])

def model(length, a):
    return a * length ** 3          # the form of the rule: weight = a * length^3

for a in [7e-6, 3.3e-6]:
    guess = model(length, a)
    print(f"a = {a:.1e}: average miss = {np.abs(guess - weight).mean():.3f} kg")

lazy = weight.mean()                 # the lazy model: always the average weight
print(f"lazy model says {lazy:.3f} kg: average miss = {np.abs(lazy - weight).mean():.3f} kg")
a = 7.0e-06: average miss = 0.236 kg
a = 3.3e-06: average miss = 0.020 kg
lazy model says 0.193 kg: average miss = 0.104 kg

Quick quiz

The dial is set to a = 0. What does the rule predict for a 50 cm shark, and why?

It predicts 0 kg. The prediction is \(a\) times the length cubed, and anything times zero is zero. A lazy model that says 0.226 kg would do better.

Which part of the model is set from data: the form \(a L^3\) or the number \(a\)?

The number \(a\). The form was chosen by a person. This is why the same form can be reused for milk sharks, with a different value of \(a\).

At the best dial the average miss is 0.025 kg. Can a further move of the dial lower it?

No. The average miss is smallest at the best dial, so any move raises it. To lower it you must change the form, for example by making the exponent a second dial, as A2 does.

Learning from examples with answers

Guess first. Below is a small table of made-up sharks. You want a model that names the species from the other columns. Which column is the answer, and which would you give the model? If you wanted the weight instead, what changes? Check your plan with the buttons.

learning from examples that come with the right answer
example
one row: a shark with its measurements and its answer
label

classification
the label is a category, such as a species

the label is a number, such as a weight

Supervised learning starts from examples that pair inputs \(\mathbf{x}\) with a label \(y\). The model must predict \(y\) from \(\mathbf{x}\), including for sharks it has not seen.

Question

The question you ask picks the label. The type of the label decides the task: a category gives classification, a number gives regression.

The coral column is the answer the model must learn to give. The navy columns are its inputs. The last row is a new shark whose answer is unknown. Filling in the question mark is what a trained model does. Untick an input to see how the table changes, and untick all of them to see what a model without inputs is left with.

The tag number is a name. It has no link to the animal's size or species, and a model that used it would learn to recognise tags it has seen, not sharks. The word "supervised" refers to the labels: someone supplied the right answer for the training examples, so the model has something to be graded against.

\[ \bigl\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\, y^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{m} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \mathbf{x}^{(i)} = (x^{(i)}_1,\, \dots,\, x^{(i)}_d) \]

  • \(m\) is the number of examples, here 600 in the full table. \(i\) numbers them, so \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\) is the inputs of the \(i\)-th shark and \(y^{(i)}\) its label.
  • \(d\) is the number of inputs for one shark. The subscript \(j\) in \(x_j\) numbers the inputs.

One table, two questions. Take Tag 4127, the 44.0 cm juvenile bamboo shark. If the question is the species, its label is the word "bamboo" and the task is classification. If the question is the weight, its label is 0.281 kg and the task is regression. The inputs are the same. Only the label column changes. In code, the table becomes a matrix \(X\) with one row per shark and one column per input, and a vector \(\mathbf{y}\) with one label per shark.

The same layout fits the clinic story. The Pima data is a published dataset of 768 women of Pima heritage, measured in the United States, not data from Mumbai. Woman A is one row, and her label is the diagnosis:

(158, 31.6, 28)x of Woman A: glucose, BMI, age→1y: she has diabetesd = 3inputsm = 768women in the table

When it fails. Supervised learning works when the label depends on the inputs and the examples look like the cases you will meet later. It fails when labels are wrong, when the inputs lack the information, or when the future differs from the past. A label also costs effort: a weight needs a scale and a species needs an expert.

In practice. Before you fit anything, count the rows and columns, and look for values that mean "not measured". The Pima file stores 374 missing insulin readings as 0, and a model that reads them as measurements learns nonsense. Ask who produced the label, and when (A4 covers leaks).

Common belief. A trained model stores the table and looks up the most similar shark.

Why it fails. The cube rule keeps one number, \(a\), and the 268 records can be deleted after training. It still answers for a length that was never recorded.

Better account. The models in this primer keep parameters, a summary of the examples, and not the examples themselves. A few other methods do store examples, and this primer leaves them out.

Debug tip. If a model scores suspiciously well, look for an input that is a copy of the label or built from it. In this made-up table, girth was computed from weight, so girth nearly gives the weight away. Names such as tag numbers can leak in other ways. A4 treats leaks in detail.

Quick quiz

You want to predict whether a shark is an adult from its length and girth. What is the label, and is this regression or classification?

The label is "adult or juvenile". It has two possible values, so this is classification.

A table lists 768 women with glucose, BMI, age and a yes or no for diabetes. Which column is the label, what are \(m\) and \(d\), and what is the task?

The label is the yes or no column, with \(m = 768\) and \(d = 3\). The label is a category, so the task is classification.

A model keeps learning from the sharks it sees at the dock, after training is finished. True or false?

False for the models of this primer. After training, the parameters stay fixed. The model reads them and does not change them. Learning happens again only when someone runs training on new labelled examples. C3 covers methods that adapt during use as a special case.

The lazy model

Guess first. The made-up table has 600 sharks: 268 spadenose, 200 milk sharks, 72 bamboo sharks, 57 hammerheads and 3 Ganges sharks. A model that ignores the shark and always says one species is right how often, if it picks the best species? And can the month of the catch help a rule do better?

baseline
a simple model you must beat before you trust a complicated one
lazy model
the baseline that ignores the inputs: the commonest label for a category, the average for a number
base rate
the share of examples that have a given label (0.7)
accuracy
the share of predictions that are right
cut-off
a value of one input that splits the sharks into two parts

A model is worth keeping only if it beats the lazy model. On these records the lazy model, which always says spadenose, is right for 268 of 600 sharks: 44.7%.

One cut-off on length lifts accuracy from 44.7% to 73.7%. No cut-off on the month of the catch beats the lazy model at all. Faded bars are sharks the rule gets wrong.

Build a rule. Pick an input, drag the coral cut-offs along the axis, and choose the species to say in the parts. Solid bars are sharks your rule gets right. Dashed faded bars are mistakes. The first task is to reach 70% with one cut-off on length. The second is to beat the lazy model using the month. The button "Show the best rule for this input" searches many cut-offs and shows the best one it finds, so use it after you have tried.

The lazy model's score depends on the data, not on the model. If 90% of the catch were spadenose, the lazy model would score 90%, and a rule at 74% would be worse than a rule that ignores the shark. That is why a score needs its baseline beside it. For a number such as weight, the lazy model says the average, as in the first figure.

Notice what the best rules never do. None of them says "Ganges shark", because three sharks in 600 are too few to be the commonest species in any wide part. A4 returns to this: a model can score well and still miss the animal you care about.

What the best-rule button does. For a cut-off on length, say the commonest species on the two sides and count the sharks this gets right. Then move the cut-off and count again. At 30 cm both sides have spadenose as the commonest species, so the rule repeats the lazy model. As the cut-off moves right, the long side turns into mostly milk sharks while the short side stays spadenose, and the count peaks near 55 cm. After that it falls, because milk sharks start to fall on the short side (16 below 54.8 cm, 40 below 60 cm).

A second baseline, from the clinic. In the published Pima data, 268 of 768 women have diabetes. The lazy model says "no diabetes" for all of them, so it scores:

When it misleads. The baseline depends on how the labels are spread, so it changes from table to table. It also ignores cost. When missing a case is worse than a false alarm, a lazy model with a high score can be useless.

In practice. Keep a ladder of baselines: the lazy model, a one-cut rule, then the method in use today. Beat the highest rung that is cheap to run, and score all of them on the same sharks.

\[ \text{accuracy} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathbf{1}\bigl[\hat y^{(i)} = y^{(i)}\bigr] \qquad \text{lazy accuracy} = \frac{\max_k n_k}{m} \]

  • \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is 1 when the statement inside is true and 0 when it is false, so the sum counts the correct predictions.
  • \(n_k\) is the number of examples with label \(k\). The largest \(n_k\) over \(m\) is the base rate of the commonest label: \(268/600 = 0.447\).

Common belief. A model that is right 74% of the time is a good model.

Why it fails. Whether 74% is good depends on the baseline. It is a large gain over 44.7%. It would be a loss against 90%.

Better account. Report a score next to the lazy model's score on the same data, and read the gap between them.

Debug tip. Before you celebrate a score, compute the lazy model's score on the same data. If your model is within a few points of it, the model has learned little. If it is far above and surprises you, look for a leak (A4).

import numpy as np

species = ["spadenose", "milk", "bamboo", "hammerhead", "ganges"]
# how many of the 600 made-up sharks fall below and above a 54.8 cm cut-off
below = np.array([258, 16, 57, 13, 0])
above = np.array([10, 184, 15, 44, 3])
total = below.sum() + above.sum()

lazy = (below + above).max() / total
rule = (below.max() + above.max()) / total
print("lazy model:", round(lazy, 3), "- always says", species[(below + above).argmax()])
print("one-cut rule:", round(rule, 3), "- says", species[below.argmax()], "below and", species[above.argmax()], "above")

Quick quiz

A table has 1,000 sharks and 620 of them are adults. What does the lazy model score when it predicts adult or juvenile?

It always says "adult" and scores 62%. A model with 65% accuracy has barely improved on the lazy model.

Learning as a search for good dials

Guess first. The cube rule has one dial, so trying a few hundred settings is quick. A small neural network has thousands of dials. Suppose you try 10 settings of a dial, and a computer can test a million combinations per second. How long does a model with 20 dials take?

loss
one number that says how badly a model does on the examples; lower is better (A2 gives the standard formula)
training
searching for the parameter values with the lowest loss
random search
trying settings at random and keeping the best

Training is a search for the parameter values with the lowest loss. Random tries work for one dial. With many dials they cannot finish, and a smarter search is needed.

Settings tried: 0Best so far: none yet

A few random tries, then tries near the best, find a good setting of one dial. With 20 dials, testing all combinations would take about 3 million years, and with 24 dials longer than the age of the universe.

The loss here is the average miss of the cube rule on the 268 made-up spadenose records. A press of "Try one random a" tests one setting and plots it. The curve is hidden by default, because an actual search sees only the points it has tried. Press "Try 10 near the best so far" to zoom in. That is a search with two stages, wide and then narrow, and it works well for one dial. Switch on the whole curve to check your best against the true lowest point.

The boxes below count the combinations. With \(d\) dials and \(k\) settings per dial, there are \(k^d\) combinations to test. The count grows by a factor of 10 with a new dial (0.3). The searches that make training practical use the slope of the loss to decide which way to turn the dials, so they never test all combinations. A2 builds the first one, gradient descent.

\[ \text{combinations} = k^{d} \qquad\qquad \theta^{\star} = \arg\min_{\theta}\; L(\theta) \]

  • \(k\) is the number of settings tried per dial and \(d\) is the number of dials.
  • \(L(\theta)\) is the loss for the parameters \(\theta\). \(\arg\min\) means "the argument that gives the smallest value", so \(\theta^{\star}\) is the best setting of the dials.

The arithmetic behind the caption. Its numbers come from one division:

1020combinations: 20 dials, 10 settings÷106tests per second=1014 sseconds in all÷3.16×107seconds in a year=3.2 millionyears

Adding four dials multiplies the count, and the wait, by 10,000. That gives \(3.2\times 10^{10}\) years, and the universe is about 14 billion years old.

A search you can run. The code below repeats the two stages of the figure on the eight records, with a fixed seed. The best of five wide tries has an average miss of 0.040 kg. Five more tries within one millionth of it find 3.15 millionths and 0.017 kg. The grid of the first code block, with 801 settings, reaches 0.015 kg.

When it breaks. The loss curve of one dial is a single valley, so narrowing the range around the best try makes sense. With several valleys the second stage could settle in the wrong one. A try also reveals only the loss at that setting, not which way is downhill. Gradient descent adds that missing piece, the slope.

In practice. Random search is still a standard way to set hyperparameters, the settings chosen before training such as a learning rate, with a few dozen tries. Draw a dial that spans orders of magnitude on a log scale: pick the exponent at random, then raise 10 to it. On a linear scale from \(10^{-7}\) to \(10^{-4}\), nine tries in ten exceed \(10^{-5}\).

Common belief. A deployed model keeps learning from the sharks it sees.

Why it fails. Using a model reads its parameters and leaves them alone. Training changes them, and training is a separate step with labelled examples.

Better account. Training and use are two phases. A model that changes during use needs a method built for it (C3).

Debug tip. When you search, look at where the best value falls. If it falls at the edge of the range you tried, the true best may lie outside it, so widen the range.

import numpy as np

length = np.array([18.2, 29.2, 33.2, 36.5, 39.4, 42.8, 46.4, 51.3])
weight = np.array([0.018, 0.066, 0.128, 0.169, 0.167, 0.287, 0.297, 0.413])

settings = np.linspace(0, 8e-6, 801)          # 801 settings of the dial a
miss = [np.abs(a * length ** 3 - weight).mean() for a in settings]
best = settings[int(np.argmin(miss))]
print(f"best of 801 settings: a = {best:.2e}, average miss = {min(miss):.3f} kg")
print("settings to try with 20 dials at 10 settings each:", 10 ** 20)

Quick quiz

You try 100 settings for 5 dials. How many combinations is that?

\(100^5 = 10^{10}\), ten billion. At a million per second that is about 3 hours.

Your best setting of a dial is the largest value you tried. What do you do?

Try larger values. The true best may lie beyond the edge of the range.

You try only 3 settings for 20 dials. How many combinations is that, and how long at a million per second?

\(3^{20} = 3{,}486{,}784{,}401\) combinations, which take about 3,487 seconds, a little under an hour. With 30 dials the same grid would take over six years.

Exercises

Theory

  1. theory A table has 1,000 sharks and 620 are adults. What accuracy does the lazy model score for adult or juvenile? A second table has 1,000 sharks and 50 of one rare species. What does the lazy model score for "is it this rare species", and what does that say about quoting accuracy alone?

    Hint

    The lazy model says the commonest label. Count the sharks it gets right.

  2. theory How many parameters do these models have? (a) \(\hat y = a L^3\). (b) \(\hat y = w x + b\). (c) \(\hat y = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b\). (d) \(\hat y = a L^{k}\), where both \(a\) and \(k\) are dials. Which of these have the same form but different numbers of parameters?

  3. theory Explain in two or three sentences why the tag number should not be an input to a species model. What would happen to a new shark's tag?

Practice

  1. practice Load catch.csv with pandas. Compute the lazy model's accuracy for the column mature on all 600 records. Then compute the accuracy of the rule "mature if length is at least 40 cm" and compare.

  2. practice For the spadenose records only, search settings of \(a\) in \(\hat y = a L^3\) from 1e-6 to 8e-6 and report the setting with the smallest average miss. How far is it from 3.3e-6, and why might it differ?

  3. practice Build the best one-cut rule for the species using the weight instead of the length. Report the cut-off, the two species it says and the accuracy.

    Hint

    Try all the midpoints between sorted weights, and in the two parts say the commonest species.

Think deeper

  1. open SharkCam looks at 2,400 video frames from one dive. Suppose a shark is in view in 199 of them. What is the lazy model for "is a shark in view", and what does it score? What would you want to know about a detector besides its accuracy?

  2. open A teammate says: "My rule gets 74%, so it has understood what separates the species." How would you test the claim? Which simpler explanations would you rule out first?

  3. open Name two inputs SharkCam has on the boat at the dock that it would not have on a dive. How would that change what a model trained on dock records can do underwater?

Come back later

Try these after two days and again after a week, without looking back.

A shark is twice as long as another. The cube rule predicts how many times the weight? (0.3) And what does the dial \(a\) do to that ratio?

Eight times, since \(2^3 = 8\). The dial \(a\) cancels in the ratio: it scales all predictions together and leaves the ratio alone.

A bar chart shows a model's accuracy as 74% next to a bar for the lazy model, and the axis starts at 40%. What are two things to check? (0.8)

Check that the axis starts at zero, because a truncated axis makes a gap look bigger. Check that both bars are scored on the same data. The gap between 74% and 44.7% is large on a fair axis too, but its size depends on the baseline.

The average of the nine spadenose lengths in 0.7 is 37.2 cm. If your model had no inputs, would it predict this value for a shark's length? Why is that the lazy model for a number?

Yes. With no inputs, a model for a number can say only one thing for all sharks, and the mean is the single value with the smallest squared miss (A2). That is the lazy model for numbers.
