Why non-linearity matters

One neuron draws one straight line. Lift the inputs into a new dimension, or stack neurons with a bend between them, and the line becomes a curve.

A single neuron can only cut the plane with a straight line, so it fails on XOR and on a disc inside a ring. This chapter shows two ways out. The first lifts the circles into 3D, where a flat plane separates them. The second stacks neurons into hidden layers, which learn such lifts by themselves. It also shows why the stack needs a bend, a non-linear activation: without one, any number of layers collapses into a single layer. You train a small network in your browser and watch what its hidden units learn.

The points in the figures are toy points made up for this chapter. The sharks return in the code, where a small network sorts milk sharks from bamboo sharks.

You will need: B1 (the neuron and its activations), the XOR truth table, the matrix product, and the decision boundary of A3.

One neuron is not enough

Guess first. In 0.4 the best straight line for the four XOR corners got 3 of 4 right. Here are three sets of 200 made-up points: two blobs, the XOR pattern, and a disc inside a ring. What share of the points would you expect the best straight line to get right in the three sets? Would ten times more training time change the answer?

The best straight line gets 100% of the two blobs, about 75% to 80% of the XOR points (which random points you draw changes it a little) and about 70% of the circles.

Navy circles are class 0 and coral diamonds are class 1. The two sliders place a line, and the coral shading is the side that the neuron calls 1. A dark ring marks a point called wrong. Press "Find the best line" and the computer tries 360 directions, and for any direction it picks the best position of the cut. The boxes under the plot give the dials of the neuron that draws your line. The line \(\cos\theta\; x + \sin\theta\; y = c\) is where \(z = 0\), for the weights \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and the bias \(-c\). So all you see here is one neuron, with the activation left out because it does not move the line (B1).

\[ z = w_1 x + w_2 y + b \qquad \text{call 1 when } z \ge 0 \qquad (w_1, w_2, b) = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta,\ -c) \]

  • \(x, y\) are the two inputs of a point, \(w_1, w_2\) the weights and \(b\) the bias of the neuron (B1).
  • \(\theta\) is the direction of the line (the first slider) and \(c\) its position (the second slider). The line is where \(z = 0\).

Why can no line work for XOR? Write the four corners as inputs \((x_1, x_2)\). Ask for \(z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b\) to be negative for class 0 and zero or positive for class 1. The four demands contradict one another:

  1. The corner \((0,0)\) is class 0, so \(b < 0\).
  2. The corners \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) are class 1, so \(w_1 + b \ge 0\) and \(w_2 + b \ge 0\). Add them: \(w_1 + w_2 + 2b \ge 0\).
  3. The corner \((1,1)\) is class 0, so \(w_1 + w_2 + b < 0\). Add \(b < 0\) from step 1: \(w_1 + w_2 + 2b < 0\).
  4. Steps 2 and 3 say that one number is both at least 0 and below 0. No weights and bias exist.

Minsky and Papert analysed this limit of single-layer perceptrons in their 1969 book Perceptrons, and XOR is the smallest example. The circles have the same flaw, seen as a picture. Take two ring points on opposite sides, such as \((0.8, 0)\) and \((-0.8, 0)\). If one side of a line contains both, it also contains all the points between them, because a straight line cannot bend away from the segment. The midpoint \((0, 0)\) is in the disc, which is class 0. So no side of any line contains all the ring points and none of the disc points. The XOR proof and the circle argument both use one fact: a flat side contains the middle of any two of its points.

Common belief. If a neuron scores badly, give it more data or train it for longer.

Why it fails. Training only searches over lines. The best line is already in the search, and the search finds it quickly. After that the score cannot rise, and 75% for XOR is the ceiling.

Better account. Change what the model can express: add an input that bends the problem (next section), or add more neurons with a bend between them (the last two sections).

Debug tip. If training accuracy stalls at a number that is not close to 100% and does not move when you train longer, ask whether any straight line could do better. Plot the two classes. Corners, rings and spirals are the usual reasons.

In practice: a straight-line model is the first baseline to try (A1, A3). If it already scores near 100%, a network adds cost and no benefit. If it stalls well below, the data has a bend in it. The brute-force search below is a quick way to learn a line's ceiling.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(200, 2))                # 200 made-up points
y = (x[:, 0] * x[:, 1] < 0).astype(int)              # XOR: class 1 when the signs differ

angles = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, 20000)                # 20,000 random lines
cuts = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 20000)
best = 0.0
for a, c in zip(angles, cuts):
    side = x @ np.array([np.cos(a), np.sin(a)]) >= c
    acc = max(np.mean(side == y), np.mean(side != y))    # allow either side to be class 1
    best = max(best, acc)
print(f"best of 20,000 lines on XOR: {best:.3f}")
best of 20,000 lines on XOR: 0.730

The best of 20,000 random lines scores 0.73. The ceiling is about 0.75: a line can get three of the four corner regions right, and the random scatter moves the score a little either way.

Quick quiz

A friend trains a sigmoid neuron on the circles for a million steps and gets 70%. What would a billion steps give?

About 70% again. The best line is already found, so the score is capped by the model and not by training time.

Which points do the circles and XOR arguments both use to show that no line works?

The midpoint of two points on the same side. In XOR it is \((0.5, 0.5)\) between the two class 1 corners, and for the circles it is the centre between two ring points. The midpoint lies on the same side as both, but belongs to the other class.

Lift the circles

Guess first. The disc and the ring lie flat on a table. Now lift the points to heights equal to their squared distance from the centre. The centre stays low and the ring rises. Now the maths, one step at a time. The lifted feature is the squared distance from the centre. A neuron that reads the three coordinates \((x, y, z)\) computes \(s = w_1 x + w_2 y + w_3 z + b\). Choose \(w_1 = w_2 = 0\), \(w_3 = 1\) and \(b = -c\). Then \(s = z - c\), which is positive above the plane. Replace \(z\) by its definition and the same rule reads \(x^2 + y^2 - c > 0\): outside the circle of radius \(\sqrt{c}\). A flat cut in 3D is a curved cut in 2D.

\[ z = x^2 + y^2 \qquad s = w_1 x + w_2 y + w_3 z + b \qquad (w_1, w_2, w_3, b) = (0,\,0,\,1,\,-c) \;\Rightarrow\; s = x^2 + y^2 - c \]

  • \(x, y\) are the two inputs and \(z\) is the new feature. A neuron on \((x, y, z)\) is the same neuron as in B1 with one more input.
  • \(c\) is the height of the plane. The radius of the cut is \(\sqrt{c}\). With the points of this figure, any \(c\) between 0.19 and 0.41 works, so the best choice is the middle, 0.30.
  • For point A = (0.30, 0.20): \(z = 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.13\), below 0.30, so class 0. For point B = (0.80, \(-0.50\)): \(z = 0.64 + 0.25 = 0.89\), above 0.30, so class 1.

The same trick works on XOR. Give the four corners the coordinates \((\pm 1, \pm 1)\) and add the product \(z = x\,y\) as a feature. The corners \((1,1)\) and \((-1,-1)\) get \(z = +1\), and \((1,-1)\) and \((-1,1)\) get \(z = -1\). The classes are now two layers: class 1 is the lower layer, \(z = -1\). The neuron with weights \((0, 0, -1)\) and bias 0 separates them, since \(s = -x\,y\) is positive where the signs differ. The inputs "x, y, x², y², xy" in the playground of the last section try this on a network.

Common belief. Lifting adds information, so the new dimension makes the problem easier because it contains more data.

Why it fails. The new feature is computed from \(x\) and \(y\), so it brings no new measurement. It helps because it puts the information in a form that a straight cut can use.

Better account. A feature is a better view of the same data. The risk is the other side: an added feature is another number to fit, and with few points it can fit noise (A4).

Debug tip. Scale a lifted feature like any input. The values of \(x^2 + y^2\) run from 0 to 1, but a squared length in centimetres can reach thousands, and a neuron that reads it saturates (A2).

In practice: you have lifted before. In A2, taking the log of both length and weight turned a curve into a straight line: log length is a feature. Engineers add features when they know the physics, such as a squared distance, a ratio or a log. Add one at a time and check them on held-out data. When the right feature is not obvious, as for photos or words, the network has to find it, which is the last section.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 200
radius = np.r_[np.sqrt(rng.uniform(0, 1, n // 2)) * 0.42, rng.uniform(0.65, 0.95, n // 2)]   # disc, then ring
angle = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n)
X = np.c_[radius * np.cos(angle), radius * np.sin(angle)]
y = np.r_[np.zeros(n // 2), np.ones(n // 2)]

def fit(F, steps=3000, eta=0.5):                      # logistic regression, as in A3
    F = (F - F.mean(axis=0)) / F.std(axis=0)
    w, b = np.zeros(F.shape[1]), 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(F @ w + b)))
        w -= eta * F.T @ (p - y) / n
        b -= eta * (p - y).mean()
    return np.mean((F @ w + b > 0) == y)

lifted = np.c_[X, (X ** 2).sum(axis=1)]               # add z = x^2 + y^2
print("one neuron on (x, y):        ", round(fit(X), 3))
print("one neuron on (x, y, x^2+y^2):", round(fit(lifted), 3))
one neuron on (x, y):         0.545
one neuron on (x, y, x^2+y^2): 1.0

One neuron on the plain inputs gets 54.5%, little better than a guess. With the single added feature it gets all 200 right. The second line of code shows the whole idea: the new column is the only change.

Two layers need a bend

Guess first. A network has two layers of neurons and no activation function between them. The input has 2 numbers, the hidden layer has 2 units and the output has 1. Can it draw anything a single neuron cannot?

hidden layer
a layer between the inputs and the output; its outputs are not shown to the user

one neuron in a hidden layer

an activation whose graph is not a straight line, such as ReLU or tanh (B1)

Two layers with no bend between them are one layer, with the product of the two weight matrices as its weights. A bend between the layers stops the collapse, and XOR becomes possible with two hidden units.

Between the layers
Input at a corner
XOR corners called right: 0 of 4

With the numbers that solve XOR, the two layers with ReLU give 0, 1, 1, 0 at the four corners. Without the bend they give 2, 1, 1, 0, and the single layer \(y = 2 - x_1 - x_2\) is the same plane.

The boxes are a complete network. Layer 1 turns the input \((x_1, x_2)\) into two hidden sums. The switch decides whether ReLU is applied. Layer 2 turns the two hidden values into one output. The last row of boxes is the single layer that should be the same network: it uses the matrix \(W^{[2]}W^{[1]}\). With "no bend" the two outputs always agree, and you can change any number to check. Click the corner chips with ReLU on. The left map is the output over the square, and the right plot is where the hidden layer sends the four corners. In the right plot the dashed line is where the output is 0.5.

The right plot shows what the hidden layer does. Without a bend, the four corners fall on one straight line in the hidden plane, in the order class 0, 1, 0, and no line can separate that order. With ReLU, the corner \((0,0)\) moves from \((0,-1)\) to \((0,0)\), the corner \((1,1)\) stays at \((2,1)\), and the two class 1 corners are both at \((1,0)\), with or without ReLU. Now one line separates them: it is the dashed line. The hidden layer has changed the points into a form that a single neuron can read.

The algebra of the collapse takes three lines. Write the two layers, put the first into the second, and multiply out:

\[ \mathbf{y} = W^{[2]}\bigl(W^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]}\bigr) + \mathbf{b}^{[2]} = \underbrace{W^{[2]}W^{[1]}}_{W}\;\mathbf{x} + \underbrace{W^{[2]}\mathbf{b}^{[1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[2]}}_{\mathbf{b}} \]

  • \(W^{[1]}\) and \(\mathbf{b}^{[1]}\) are the weights and biases of layer 1, and \(W^{[2]}\) and \(\mathbf{b}^{[2]}\) those of layer 2 (0.5).
  • The result has the form \(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\), which is one layer with no bend. Any number of layers collapse the same way, because a product of matrices is a matrix.
  • With ReLU in between, the hidden vector is \(\max(0, W^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]})\), and the step that multiplies the two matrices is not allowed, since the maximum is in the way.

The numbers in the boxes show it. Here \(W^{[1]} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{b}^{[1]} = (0, -1)\), \(W^{[2]} = (1, -2)\) and \(b^{[2]} = 0\). The product is \(W^{[2]}W^{[1]} = (1\cdot 1 + (-2)\cdot 1,\; 1\cdot 1 + (-2)\cdot 1) = (-1, -1)\). The new bias is \(W^{[2]}\mathbf{b}^{[1]} + b^{[2]} = 1\cdot 0 + (-2)\cdot(-1) + 0 = 2\). So the whole network is \(y = -x_1 - x_2 + 2\), as the caption says. A bigger case: a network with 6 inputs, two hidden layers of 10 and one output has \((6\cdot 10 + 10) + (10 \cdot 10 + 10) + (10 + 1) = 191\) numbers. With no bend between the layers, the 191 numbers do the work of 7: six weights and a bias.

These numbers are the standard XOR network from Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville's book on deep learning. With ReLU the first hidden unit computes \(\max(0, x_1 + x_2)\) and the second \(\max(0, x_1 + x_2 - 1)\). The output subtracts the second unit twice from the first. The result is a tent: it rises from 0 to 1 as \(x_1 + x_2\) goes from 0 to 1, then falls back to 0 at 2. Class 1 is the band where the tent is above 0.5, between two parallel lines. XOR needed two lines, in two steps, which is what 0.4 said it would take.

Common belief. More layers mean a more powerful network.

Why it fails. With no bend between them, extra layers add numbers and add no power: the 191 numbers above do the work of 7. Even with a bend, a layer helps only if the problem has a layered structure to find.

Better account. Power comes from the bends between layers. Depth multiplies what bends can do, and all the layers need their bends.

Debug tip. If a network scores the same as a linear model, check the bends first. A forgotten activation is a classic bug: the code runs, the loss falls, and the answer is a straight line. Train a linear model beside the network. A tie is a warning.

In practice: hidden layers all have an activation, usually ReLU or GELU, and the last layer has none for a number, a sigmoid for a probability, or softmax for classes. Two linear layers are sometimes used on purpose, to save numbers. A \(1000 \times 1000\) matrix has a million numbers. The product of a \(1000 \times 10\) and a \(10 \times 1000\) matrix has 20,000 numbers and a rank of at most 10. That trick is called a low-rank layer.

import numpy as np

W1 = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]])    # layer 1: two hidden units, one row each
b1 = np.array([0.0, -1.0])
W2 = np.array([[1.0, -2.0]])               # layer 2: one output unit
b2 = np.array([0.0])

X = np.array([[0, 0], [1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]], dtype=float)   # the four XOR corners, one per row

def forward(X, bend):
    z1 = X @ W1.T + b1                       # hidden sums
    h = np.maximum(0, z1) if bend else z1    # ReLU between the layers, or no bend
    return (h @ W2.T + b2).ravel()

print("with ReLU:    ", forward(X, True))
print("without bend: ", forward(X, False))
print("W2 @ W1 =", W2 @ W1, "  W2 @ b1 + b2 =", W2 @ b1 + b2)
with ReLU:     [0. 1. 1. 0.]
without bend:  [2. 1. 1. 0.]
W2 @ W1 = [[-1. -1.]]   W2 @ b1 + b2 = [2.]

The first output row is XOR. The second is the plane \(2 - x_1 - x_2\), and the last line gives its two numbers, the weights \((-1, -1)\) and the bias 2.

Quick quiz

A layer has weights \(W^{[1]} = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}\), no bias, and no activation. The next layer has \(W^{[2]} = (1, 1)\). What single layer is it?

\(W^{[2]}W^{[1]} = (1\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 0,\; 1\cdot 0 + 1\cdot 3) = (2, 3)\). The network is \(y = 2x_1 + 3x_2\).

Why can ReLU between the layers not be folded into the matrix product?

The product rule needs a linear map between the matrices. ReLU keeps positive values and cuts negative ones, so what reaches layer 2 depends on the signs of the hidden sums, and no single matrix does that for all inputs.

Write what the figure computes. The input vector \(\mathbf{x}\) goes through a layer, a bend, and a second layer. The output is a sigmoid, so it is a probability of class 1, and training lowers the cross-entropy of A3. The boxes under the diagram count the numbers for your choices. A network with 2 inputs, 4 hidden units and 1 output has \((2 \times 4 + 4) + (4 \times 1 + 1) = 17\) numbers. Add a second hidden layer of 4 units and it has \(12 + 20 + 5 = 37\).

\[ \mathbf{h} = g\bigl(W^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]}\bigr) \qquad \hat{y} = \sigma\bigl(W^{[2]}\mathbf{h} + b^{[2]}\bigr) \qquad \text{numbers} = \sum_{l}\,(n_{l-1} + 1)\,n_l \]

  • \(\mathbf{x}\) is the input vector, \(\mathbf{h}\) the vector of hidden outputs, \(g\) the activation you chose and \(\hat{y}\) the probability of class 1.
  • \(n_l\) is the number of units in layer \(l\), with \(n_0\) the number of inputs. A layer has \(n_l\) rows of weights, with rows of length \(n_{l-1}\), plus \(n_l\) biases.
  • The update is mini-batch gradient descent. The gradients come from backpropagation, which B4 explains. Here it runs as a black box.

Why does it work? A hidden unit with tanh or sigmoid is a soft step across a line, and with ReLU it is a ramp. The output unit adds a weighted sum of these steps or ramps. Three steps placed around the disc make a bump with three sides, and more units make the bump rounder. A known theorem of the 1980s says that one hidden layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region (Cybenko, 1989, for sigmoid units). It does not say how many units are enough or how to find the weights. Training does that, and it can fail, as the XOR starts show.

Common belief. The network has learned what a circle is.

Why it fails. Look at the hidden maps. They are straight stripes or bent sheets, and the ring is built from a few of them, so the boundary is a polygon with rounded corners. Outside the data, where the network never saw points, the stripes keep going in straight lines, and the map there is a guess with no support.

Better account. The network has learned a patchwork of simple cuts that fits the points it saw. It may not describe the world beyond them (A4).

Debug tip. A loss that stays near \(\ln 2 = 0.69\) means the network is guessing. The usual causes are a learning rate that is too small, hidden units that are dead (ReLU), a missing bend (activation "none"), or a start that is stuck. A loss that jumps up and down points to a learning rate that is too high: set it lower. Perfect scores on training points do not show the network will do well on new points (A4).

In practice: start with a small network and grow it only while the training loss stays high. Widths of 4 to 64 units are common for small tables of numbers, and learning rates between 0.001 and 0.1 are typical. Extra input features can make a small network succeed where a plain one needs many units. On the spiral, the "x, y, sin 3x, sin 3y" inputs cut the time from about a thousand passes to 150 to 450, from all six starts tried here. That is why people spend time on features.

import numpy as np

# a small network trained on the milk and bamboo sharks (weights rounded to two decimals)
mean, spread = np.array([61.571, 25.191]), np.array([12.151, 13.246])   # length (cm), depth (m)
W1 = np.array([[2.49, 1.73], [2.83, -0.16], [1.52, 0.37]])               # 3 hidden units
b1 = np.array([-2.18, -1.75, 2.0])
W2 = np.array([[-2.58, -1.94, -3.72]])                                   # 1 output unit
b2 = np.array([-2.22])

sharks = {"Tag 4127 (bamboo)": (44.0, 0.4), "Tag 1224 (milk)": (67.8, 39.4)}
for name, (length, depth) in sharks.items():
    x = (np.array([length, depth]) - mean) / spread     # scale the inputs
    h = np.tanh(W1 @ x + b1)                            # layer 1: three tanh units
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(W2 @ h + b2)))                # layer 2: sigmoid output = chance of bamboo
    print(f"{name}: hidden = {np.round(h, 2)}, chance of bamboo = {p[0]:.3f}")
print("numbers in the network:", W1.size + b1.size + W2.size + b2.size)

This is a two-layer forward pass: scale, one matrix product, tanh, a second matrix product, sigmoid. The weights come from training offline on the 272 made-up milk and bamboo sharks. The network gets 90.4% right. The single neuron of B1 got 91.5%. When one line is nearly enough, the extra units add numbers to fit and no gain.

Press "Restart with new weights", since some starts leave units dead. Or raise the learning rate a little, or switch the units to tanh. If the activation were "none", the fix would be to put a bend back.

In A3 the decision boundary of logistic regression was a straight line. Why is it always straight, whatever the weights? (A3)

The boundary is where the score \(z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b\) is 0. That is one linear equation in \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), and its solutions form a straight line. The sigmoid only reads \(z\), so it cannot bend the line.

A ReLU hidden unit has a negative weighted sum for all 200 training points. What happens to its weights during training, and what does the playground show? (B1)

Its slope is 0 for all points, so no update reaches its weights. It stays dead. In the playground its map is a flat navy or neutral square, and the loss may stay on a plateau.

In A2 you took logs of length and weight and got a straight line. In the language of this chapter, what was that step? (A2)

A feature lift. Log length is a new coordinate computed from the old one. In it the relation became a straight line, which a linear model can fit.
