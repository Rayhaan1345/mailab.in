A picture is a grid of numbers. This chapter reads a handwritten tag number and a shark photo as arrays. Then it slides a filter of nine weights over the digit, one dot product at a time, and fills a feature map. You will change the filter, the stride and the padding, watch the output size follow a formula, and shrink the map with max pooling. C2 stacks these pieces into a network that reads the digit.

The digits are handwritten MNIST digits (LeCun et al., 1998). They play the part of the tag numbers that SharkCam researchers write in logbooks. The photos are openly licensed and credited under the figures, and the depth effect on them comes from a simple model, not from a measurement of Mumbai water.

A picture is a grid of numbers

Guess first. Researchers write tag numbers by hand in a logbook. A scanner stores one digit as a grid with 28 rows and 28 columns. How many numbers is that for one digit? And what number would you expect for a square the pen never touched?

pixel
one square of the grid, holding one number
grey value
the number in a square: 0 is no ink, 255 is full ink
array
a grid of numbers with a size such as (28, 28) (0.1)
flatten
unroll the grid row by row into one vector of 784 numbers (0.5)

A grey picture is a grid of numbers from 0 to 255, so reading a handwritten digit means doing arithmetic on 784 numbers.

Move over the picture.

A digit is 784 numbers, and most of them are 0, the empty paper. The pen path is a thin ridge of values near 255. The framed 5 by 5 block on the left is written out as numbers on the right.

Hover over, or tap, any square and read its number. Squares are counted by row from the top, then by column from the left, so the top-left square is row 0, column 0. A file stores a value in one byte, which allows 256 values, 0 to 255. Machine learning code usually divides by 255 so that values lie between 0 and 1. This chapter keeps 0 to 255 because those numbers are easier to read. The digits come from MNIST, a public set of 70,000 handwritten digits. Tag 4127 is four digits: 4, 1, 2 and 7. Switch to Add ink and draw on the grid. The count of zeros changes as you go.

Now tick Shuffle the squares. All squares move to new places, by one fixed random rule, and you can no longer read the digit. A plain network from B1 and B2 is not bothered. It reads a digit as a list of 784 inputs and gives all of them separate weights. It has no idea which squares are neighbours. A network with 784 inputs, 128 ReLU units and 10 outputs was trained for 5 passes over the 60,000 training digits, once on normal digits and once on shuffled ones. On the 10,000 test digits it scored 96.9% and 97.0%. That is one run per case, and the 0.1 point gap is within the noise of a single run.

Common belief. A network that reads pictures must know which squares are neighbours.

Why it fails. A plain network learns shuffled digits as well as normal ones. It gives a square its own weights, so the order of the squares never enters its arithmetic.

Better account. The layout is information that a plain network throws away. A convolution builds the layout in: the same few weights look at small patches all over the picture. The rest of this chapter shows how.

Debug tip. Arrays are indexed image[row, column], which is [y, x]. If a digit looks mirrored along its diagonal, you swapped the two. Also check the scale: a model trained on values from 0 to 1 that receives values from 0 to 255 sees inputs 255 times too big. Print image.max() before you feed an image to a model.

Quick quiz

A digit has 668 zeros out of 784 values. What share is that, and what does it say about the picture?

The share is 668 / 784, which is 85%. Most of the picture is empty paper, and the information is in a thin band of non-zero values along the pen path.

Why can a plain network learn from shuffled digits when you cannot read them?

Its first layer multiplies the squares by their own weights and adds the results. That sum does not change if all squares and weights are renamed in the same order, so the network never uses the neighbourhood.

Colour is three grids

Guess first. A photo of a hammerhead shark is 640 pixels wide and 362 high. How many numbers does it need? And when the same scene is photographed 10 m under the surface, which colour do you expect to lose first: red, green or blue?

channel
one grid of numbers for one primary colour; a grey picture has one channel, a colour picture three
RGB
red, green and blue, as three grids with values 0 to 255
bias
one more number added to the result of a filter

A colour photo is three stacked grids, and water removes red light much faster than blue.

At 10 m the red grid of the hammerhead photo is almost empty. Its mean falls from about 51 to about 7, while the mean blue rises from about 153 to about 170. The photo never changes; the depth slider applies the model of C3. Move over any grid to read one pixel as three numbers.

The hammerhead photo has 640 × 362 × 3 = 695,040 numbers. In NumPy the array has size (362, 640, 3), with the channel last. PyTorch wants the channel first: (3, 362, 640). A grey digit is the same thing with one channel. Untick a channel to see what the photo looks like without it. At depth 10 m the green grid also looks brighter, which is the haze of the water and the camera's automatic exposure, not extra green light from the shark.

Common belief. Deeper water makes a photo darker and no other change.

Why it fails. The three grids change at different rates. Red falls to a few percent of its surface value within 10 m, blue stays, and haze lifts the dark parts of all three. The mean values in the boxes show it.

Better account. Water changes the balance between the channels first, then the brightness. A model trained on surface photos meets inputs with a different range in the three channels. C3 follows what that does to a classifier.

A filter that reads a colour photo has a weight for all channels in its window. A 3 by 3 filter on an RGB photo has 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 weights, plus one bias, a number added to the result. The count of numbers in a convolution layer follows one formula.

\[ \text{parameters} = \left(k \cdot k \cdot C_{\text{in}} + 1\right)\, C_{\text{out}} \]

  • \(k\) is the side of the filter in squares, so a window has \(k \cdot k\) squares.
  • \(C_{\text{in}}\) is the number of channels the layer reads (3 for an RGB photo).
  • \(C_{\text{out}}\) is the number of filters, which is also the number of feature maps the layer writes.
  • The \(+1\) is the bias of one filter.

The boxes above the credit line work it out for the sliders. With the default choices, 3 by 3 and 16 filters, the layer has (27 + 1) × 16 = 448 numbers, whatever the size of the photo. The first layer of ResNet-18 (used in C3) reads RGB with 7 by 7 filters and writes 64 maps. It has 7 × 7 × 3 × 64 = 9,408 weights. It has no biases because a batch-norm layer (B6) follows.

Debug tip. If a blue shark looks orange, the channels are in the wrong order. OpenCV reads pictures as blue, green, red, while PIL and PyTorch use red, green, blue. Swap with image[:, :, ::-1] and check one pixel you know.

Quick quiz

How many weights has one 5 by 5 filter that reads an RGB photo, with its bias?

The count is 5 × 5 × 3 = 75 weights, plus 1 bias, so 76 numbers.

The photo doubles in width and height. Does the filter get more numbers?

No. The count depends on the filter size and the channels, not on the picture size. The same 28 numbers slide over a small or a large photo.

A filter slides over the picture

Guess first. A filter is a 3 by 3 grid of weights. At one position it multiplies the nine squares under it by the nine weights and adds up the products. The left-right edge filter has the weights −1, 0, 1 in the top row, −2, 0, 2 in the middle and −1, 0, 1 at the bottom. What does it output over a patch where all nine squares have the same value? And at the left edge of a pen stroke, where the picture goes from paper to ink?

filter (kernel)
a small grid of weights, 3 by 3 here
window
the patch of squares under the filter at one position
feature map
the grid of filter outputs, one per position
dot product
multiply matching numbers and add them up (0.5)

A filter takes a weighted sum of a small window, repeats it at all positions, and the grid of results shows where the pattern in its weights appears.

Input, 28 by 28. Click to move the window.

Feature map, 26 by 26. Coral is below 0, teal above 0.

The left-right edge filter writes teal where ink begins to the right of the window and coral where it ends, and 0 over flat paper and flat ink. Press Play, or click the digit to place the window yourself. The boxes show the nine pixels under the window, the nine weights, the nine products and their sum.

Pick the presets in turn. Edge left-right subtracts the left column from the right column and counts the middle row twice. Its weights add up to 0, so a flat patch gives 0. Edge top-bottom does the same with rows. Blur gives all nine squares the weight 1/9, so the output is their average. Sharpen gives the middle weight 5 and its four neighbours −1, which keeps the middle value and adds the difference from its neighbours. Identity copies the middle square. Then type your own nine numbers. Tick then ReLU to keep only the positive outputs, which is what a network does after a filter (B1).

The feature map is 26 by 26, smaller than the 28 by 28 input, because a window must fit inside the picture. The next section gives the rule. An output is one dot product of the window with the weights. By the dot product of 0.5, the result is large when the window points the same way as the weights. So the map is bright where the picture looks like the pattern in the weights.

\[ O[i,j] \;=\; \sum_{u=0}^{k-1}\sum_{v=0}^{k-1} K[u,v]\; I[i+u,\; j+v] \;+\; b \]

  • \(I[r,c]\) is the input value at row \(r\), column \(c\), and \(O[i,j]\) is the feature-map value at row \(i\), column \(j\).
  • \(K[u,v]\) is the filter weight at row \(u\), column \(v\) inside the window. Its side is \(k\) (3 in the figure). Here \(K\) is short for kernel, the filter. It does not mean the keys of attention (D4).
  • \(b\) is a bias added to all outputs of the filter. The figure uses \(b = 0\).
  • The two sums run over the \(k \cdot k\) squares of the window: nine products, then one total.

Take a window that is dark on the left and bright on the right: all rows are 0, 0, 255. This is a worked example for the edge filter and for the blur.

255top row: −1·0 + 0·0 + 1·255+510middle row: −2·0 + 0·0 + 2·255+255bottom row: −1·0 + 0·0 + 1·255=1020edge filter output
3 × 255the nine squares add up to this÷9squares in the window=85blur output

A flat window with all nine values equal to 128 gives 0 under the edge filter, because the weights add up to 0 and 128 × 0 = 0. Under the blur it gives 128. That answers the guess-first question: 0 on a flat patch, a large number at an edge. The largest possible output of this edge filter is 1020, which is 4 × 255. The colour scale of the map is set by the biggest value on the page, so it changes with the filter.

Analogy. A filter is a stencil that you slide over a page. At any spot you ask how well the page matches the stencil.

Where it breaks. A stencil is cut by hand and answers yes or no. A filter has any weights, answers with a graded number that can be negative, and in a CNN its nine weights are not designed by anyone. They start random and are learned by gradient descent like all weights. A weight used at all positions collects the slopes from all of them (B4, a value used twice). The filters here are hand-made so that you can see what a pattern of weights does.

When it works and when it breaks. One set of nine weights is reused at all positions, so a pattern is found wherever it appears and the filter needs only nine numbers. It does respond to one pattern in one size and orientation. The left-right filter says little about a horizontal stroke (switch to top-bottom and compare). A network therefore uses many filters at once. Libraries call this operation convolution, but strictly it is cross-correlation: the kernel is not flipped. A true convolution flips the kernel first. For the left-right edge filter the flip changes the sign of the output. For a learned filter it makes no difference, because training finds whichever version is needed.

Debug tip. Test a convolution on a tiny picture whose answer you can work out by hand, then compare with a library. A picture with a dark left half and a bright right half should give a column of large values where the halves meet, and 0 elsewhere.

import numpy as np

def conv2d(image, kernel, stride=1, pad=0):
    image = np.pad(image, pad)                      # zeros around the edge
    k = kernel.shape[0]
    n = (image.shape[0] - k) // stride + 1          # output size
    out = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            window = image[i * stride : i * stride + k, j * stride : j * stride + k]
            out[i, j] = np.sum(window * kernel)     # sum of products
    return out

image = np.zeros((6, 6)); image[:, 3:] = 255        # dark left half, bright right half
edge = np.array([[-1, 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]])
mine = conv2d(image, edge)
print(mine)
[[   0. 1020. 1020.    0.]
 [   0. 1020. 1020.    0.]
 [   0. 1020. 1020.    0.]
 [   0. 1020. 1020.    0.]]

The two columns of 1020 are the two windows that straddle the boundary between the dark and bright halves. A check against PyTorch's own convolution (not runnable in the browser) confirms the function:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
ref = F.conv2d(torch.tensor(image)[None, None], torch.tensor(edge, dtype=torch.float64)[None, None])[0, 0].numpy()
print("same as PyTorch:", np.allclose(mine, ref))
same as PyTorch: True

Quick quiz

A sharpen filter has weights 0, −1, 0 / −1, 5, −1 / 0, −1, 0. What does it output on a flat window of value 100?

The output is 5 × 100 − 4 × 100 = 100. The weights add up to 1, so a flat window passes unchanged and only differences from the neighbours are boosted.

Why does the edge filter need weights that add up to 0 to give 0 on flat patches?

On a flat patch all nine values equal one number c, so the output is c times the sum of the weights. That is 0 only if the weights sum to 0.

Stride and padding

Guess first. A 28 by 28 digit goes through a 3 by 3 filter, one square at a time. How big is the feature map? And what could you add around the picture so that the map stays 28 by 28?

stride \(s\)
how many squares the window moves between positions; 1 moves one square at a time
padding \(p\)
rings of zeros added around the picture before sliding
output size \(m\)
the number of window positions along one side of the picture; here \(m\) is not the number of examples

The side of the feature map is \(\lfloor (n + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1\), so padding makes the map bigger and stride makes it smaller.

With n = 7, k = 3, s = 1 and p = 0 the map is 5 by 5. One ring of zeros (p = 1) keeps it 7 by 7, and stride 2 cuts it to 3 by 3. The small dots mark where the window's top-left corner is placed. Click an output square to see its window. Dashed squares are padding.

Play with the four sliders and watch the equation boxes. Padding adds rows and columns on both sides, so it adds \(2p\) to the width of the picture. A window must fit inside the padded picture, so its top-left corner can be at positions 0, \(s\), \(2s\), and so on, up to \(n + 2p - k\). The number of corners is the number of steps of size \(s\) that fit, plus the starting position.

\[ m \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{n + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 \]

  • \(n\) is the side of the input, in squares. \(p\) is the number of rings of zeros added on all four sides.
  • \(k\) is the side of the filter and \(s\) is the stride.
  • \(m\) is the side of the output, not the number of examples. The brackets \(\lfloor\ \rfloor\) round down to a whole number.
  • With a stride the sum reads from \(I[s\,i + u - p,\ s\,j + v - p]\), so a step of \(i\) moves the window by \(s\) squares and the \(-p\) puts the padding rings at negative positions.

Worked examples, all with numbers from this primer. A 28 by 28 digit, \(k = 3\), \(s = 1\), \(p = 0\): \((28 - 3)/1 + 1 = 26\). With \(p = 1\): \((28 + 2 - 3)/1 + 1 = 28\), the same size as the input, usually called “same” padding (\(p = (k-1)/2\) when \(k\) is odd). With \(p = 1\) and \(s = 2\): \(\lfloor 27/2 \rfloor + 1 = 13 + 1 = 14\), where the floor drops the half step. The first layer of ResNet-18 reads a 224 by 224 photo with \(k = 7\), \(s = 2\), \(p = 3\): \(\lfloor 223/2 \rfloor + 1 = 111 + 1 = 112\).

26n = 28, k = 3, s = 1, p = 0|28same, p = 1|14p = 1, s = 2 (27 ÷ 2 floors to 13)|112ResNet-18, n = 224, k = 7, s = 2, p = 3

Tick Count how often a square is read. With \(p = 0\) the corner squares are read once and the centre squares nine times. A pen stroke at the border therefore has less influence than one in the middle. With a ring of padding the corner count rises to 4. And zero padding is a guess: it adds an artificial edge between the picture and the zeros, so edge filters fire along the border.

Common belief. A stride of 2 only makes the map smaller. It still reads all squares of the picture.

Why it fails. Set \(n = 8\), \(k = 3\), \(s = 2\), \(p = 0\) and tick Count how often a square is read. The last row and column are crossed out: 15 squares are never read, because the floor drops the half step. When the stride is larger than the filter, whole columns are skipped.

Better account. The stride has to fit the picture. Choose sizes so that \(s\) divides \(n + 2p - k\), add padding, or accept that a strip at the border is dropped, as in the 11 to 5 pooling example.

Debug tip. The most common error in a first CNN is a size mismatch after Flatten: the dense layer expects a different number of inputs than the last feature map provides. Work the sizes out with the formula, or print x.size() after the layers, before you write the dense layer.

def out_size(n, k, s=1, p=0):
    return (n + 2 * p - k) // s + 1    # // rounds down

print(out_size(28, 3), out_size(28, 3, p=1), out_size(28, 3, s=2, p=1))
print(out_size(224, 7, s=2, p=3), out_size(11, 2, s=2), out_size(100, 7, s=3))

Quick quiz

What padding keeps a 5 by 5 filter at stride 1 from changing the size?

\(p = (k-1)/2 = 2\). Check: \((n + 4 - 5)/1 + 1 = n\).

A 2 by 2 pool with stride 2 reads an 11 by 11 map. How big is the output, and what happens to the last row and column?

\(\lfloor (11 - 2)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 5\). The last row and the last column of the map are never read.

Max pooling

Guess first. You move the digit one square to the right and recompute the feature map. By about what share does the map change? Then you cut the map into 2 by 2 blocks and keep only the largest value in a block. Does that smaller map change by more, the same, or less?

pooling
replace a block of the map by one number
max pooling
keep the largest value in the block
average pooling
keep the mean of the block
pool size \(q\)
the side of the block; stride is the step between blocks

Max pooling keeps the strongest response in a block and forgets where it was inside the block. This shrinks the map and makes it a little less sensitive to small shifts.

Feature map after the filter and ReLU, 26 by 26

After pooling

Pooling halves the width and height of the map and softens, but does not remove, the effect of moving the digit. The two percentages compare the map of the moved digit with the map of the digit in its first place, square by square.

Move the slider to 1 and compare the two percentages. Take 30 MNIST digits and the left-right edge filter. Moving the digit one square changes the feature map by 88% of its own size on average, and the 2 by 2 max-pooled map by 65%. Pooling helps because the strongest value of a block often stays inside the same block when the pattern moves one square. Output sizes follow the formula of the last section, with \(k\) replaced by the pool size \(q\) and \(p = 0\). A 26 by 26 map with \(q = 2\) and \(s = 2\) gives \(\lfloor 24/2 \rfloor + 1 = 13\).

\[ P[i,j] \;=\; \max_{0 \le u,\,v < q}\; F[\,s\,i + u,\; s\,j + v\,] \]

  • \(F\) is the feature map before pooling and \(P\) the pooled map; \(q\) is the block side and \(s\) the stride.
  • Here \(F\) is a feature map. The Fisher information of B7 has the same letter and a different meaning.
  • For average pooling replace the maximum by the mean of the \(q \cdot q\) values. Pooling has no weights to learn.

Common belief. Pooling makes a network recognise a digit wherever it is in the picture.

Why it fails. Set the slider to 3. The pooled map now changes by about 128% (averaged over the same 30 digits), against 139% for the unpooled map. A block is only two squares wide, so pooling tolerates shifts of about that size.

Better account. Pooling gives tolerance to small shifts inside a block. Larger tolerance comes from stacking several conv and pool layers and from training on shifted examples. The chart in C2 measures this for a trained network.

What pooling costs. It throws away the position inside a block. That is fine when you want to know that a stroke exists. It hurts when the position is the answer, for example the distance between a shark's eye and its tail fin. Many modern networks use a stride-2 convolution instead of a pooling layer, so the network learns what to keep. ResNet-18 has one max pool near the start and averages over the whole map at the end.

Debug tip. If you pool an odd-sized map, the last row and column are dropped, as in the 11 to 5 example. When that matters, pad the map first or change the sizes. Also remember that max pooling passes the slope back only to the square that held the maximum (B4).

A tiny map shows both sides of the tolerance. The 9 and the 8 are strong responses of a filter.

import numpy as np

def maxpool(m, q=2, s=2):
    n = (m.shape[0] - q) // s + 1
    return np.array([[m[i*s:i*s+q, j*s:j*s+q].max()
                      for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)])

m = np.zeros((4, 4), int); m[0, 1] = 9; m[2, 3] = 8   # two strong responses
for shift in (0, -1, 1):          # no move, one square left, one right
    pooled = maxpool(np.roll(m, shift, axis=1))
    same = bool((pooled == maxpool(m)).all())
    print(f"shift {shift:2d}:", pooled.tolist(), "same as before:", same)
shift  0: [[9, 0], [0, 8]] same as before: True
shift -1: [[9, 0], [0, 8]] same as before: True
shift  1: [[0, 9], [8, 0]] same as before: False

Moving the map one square left keeps both maxima inside their blocks, so the pooled map is the same. Moving it one square right pushes both across a block boundary, and the pooled map changes.

Quick quiz

A 13 by 13 map goes through 2 by 2 max pooling with stride 2. What size is the result?

\(\lfloor (13 - 2)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 6\). The last row and column are not read.

Why does pooling give no help when the digit moves 8 squares?

The pattern falls in different blocks, and a block only hides moves smaller than its own size. At 8 squares no block contains the same part of the stroke as before.

A neuron from B1 computes \(z = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b\). What is a 3 by 3 filter at one position, and how does it differ from that neuron?

It is the same sum: the nine pixels in the window play the part of \(\mathbf{x}\), the nine filter weights are \(\mathbf{w}\), and \(b\) is the bias. The difference is reuse. The filter applies the same weights at all positions, so nine numbers produce a whole map. A plain neuron would need separate weights for separate positions.

The dive camera takes a photo at 10 m and the red grid is almost empty. In B6, batch norm stores a mean and a variance per channel. Why might those stored numbers be wrong for this photo?

They were measured on training photos taken near the surface, where red is bright. At 10 m the red values, and so the first layer's outputs for them, are much smaller. The stored mean and variance no longer describe the inputs, so the normalised values are off. C3 follows this idea.

A 3 by 3 conv layer with padding 1 reads 12 maps of size 13 by 13 and writes 24 maps. What size are the outputs and how many numbers does the layer learn?

Same size, 13 by 13, because \(p = (k-1)/2\). Parameters: \((3 \cdot 3 \cdot 12 + 1) \cdot 24 = 109 \cdot 24 = 2{,}616\).
