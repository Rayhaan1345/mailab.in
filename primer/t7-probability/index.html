Probability and statistics

How to tell a pattern from luck, and a score from a guess

A model's output is a list of probabilities, its training signal is a loss built from them, and its test score is a number that changes with the test set. This chapter covers the tools for reading all three. They are mean and spread, the normal curve, base rates and Bayes' rule, softmax, cross-entropy and moving averages. The last tools say how far a score can be trusted: resampling, confidence intervals and tests.

It ends with the warning that any data scientist needs early: two columns that move together do not show that one drives the other.

You will need: exponents and logarithms for softmax and cross-entropy, and the idea of a slope from 0.6. Vectors (0.5) appear once, in the correlation section.

Mean and spread

Guess first. Nine spadenose sharks measure between 33 and 48 cm. A typing slip turns one length, 48.3 cm, into 95 cm. Does the mean rise a little or a lot? Does the median, the middle value, move?

mean
add the values up and divide by how many there are
deviation
a value minus the mean: how far it lies from the centre
variance and standard deviation (SD)
the average squared deviation, and its square root, which has the units of the data
outlier
a value far from the rest

The mean says where the values centre. The standard deviation says how far a typical value lies from the mean. One odd value can drag both.

Mean 0 cmMedian 0 cmSD 0 cm

One typing slip lifts the mean from 37.2 to 42.4 cm and raises the SD from 5.1 to 18.9 cm, while the median stays at 35.4 cm. The lengths are from the made-up catch records.

Drag any dot, or focus it and press the arrow keys. The teal line is the mean. The coral segments are deviations. The shaded band reaches one SD to either side of the mean. Squaring the deviations before averaging gives long deviations extra weight, so one far-off value inflates the SD. The median ignores how far the extremes go, which makes it steadier but less informative about spread.

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2 \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} \]

  • \(x_i\) is the \(i\)-th value (a shark's length) and \(n\) the number of values.
  • \(\bar{x}\), read "x bar", is the mean. \(\sigma^2\) is the variance and \(\sigma\) the standard deviation. The sigmoid is also written \(\sigma\), but it always has a bracket, \(\sigma(z)\).

Two checks by hand. Take three lengths: 33.2, 35.4 and 44.1 cm. The mean is 37.57. The deviations are \(-4.37\), \(-2.17\) and 6.53, and their squares are 19.07, 4.69 and 42.68, which add up to 66.45. Dividing by 3 gives a variance of 22.15 cm\(^2\), and the root is an SD of 4.71 cm. Dividing by 2, as for a sample, would give 5.76 cm. The squares also explain why the mean is the centre: it is the value that makes the sum of squared deviations smallest. For the nine lengths that sum is 235.8 about the mean of 37.23, against 266.0 about the median of 35.4 and 304.7 about 40.

The whole catch table shows the same gap between mean and median. Its weights have a mean of 1.185 kg and a median of 0.43 kg. The 57 hammerheads and 3 Ganges sharks, with mean weights of 4.45 and 38 kg, pull the mean up.

Common belief. The mean describes a typical shark.

Why it fails. After the slip the mean is 42.4 cm, and no shark in the group is near that length. A mean mixes all values, outliers included.

Better account. Report the mean with a spread, look at the values, and compare it with the median. A large gap between them signals outliers or a lopsided column.

Debug tip. NumPy's std divides by \(n\) unless you pass ddof=1. Dividing by \(n-1\) is the usual choice when the data is a sample used to estimate a larger group, and the two agree closely for large \(n\). Pandas divides by \(n-1\) by default, so the same column can give two answers in two libraries.

Quick quiz

Why does the SD react more to one outlier than the mean does?

The SD squares the deviations, so a deviation twice as large counts four times as much. The mean only adds the values.

When would you report the median instead of the mean?

When a few extreme values would pull the mean away from where most of the data lies, such as lengths with a typing slip or incomes in a city.

What is the SD of the values 5, 5, 5 and 5?

It is 0. All the deviations are 0, so the variance is 0. A column with an SD of 0 gives a model no information.

The normal curve and z-scores

Guess first. Spadenose lengths average 39 cm with an SD of 8.6 cm. About what share of the sharks lie within 8.6 cm of the average: one half, two thirds or nearly all?

normal distribution
the bell curve, set by a mean \(\mu\) and an SD \(\sigma\)
z-score
how many SDs a value lies above the mean (below, if negative)
standardising
replacing all values by their z-scores, so a column has mean 0 and SD 1
density
the height of the curve: the area under it is a probability

Standardising puts any column on one scale, and for a column that follows a bell curve, about two thirds of the values fall within one SD of the mean.

Column

For lengths, 66% of the sharks fall within one SD, close to the normal curve's 68%. Switch to weight and the curve spills below 0 kg: a bell curve fits weight poorly.

The bars are the share of the 268 spadenose sharks in the made-up catch records that fall in a bin. The coral curve is the normal curve for the \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) you choose, with the darker band for one SD and the lighter for two. "Fit to the data" sets \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) to the column's own mean and SD. Now switch to the standardised view. A value \(x\) becomes \(z = (x-\mu)/\sigma\), and with the fitted numbers the histogram lines up with the same standard curve whether the column is in centimetres or kilograms. Drag the sliders away from the fit and the histogram slides off the curve.

Standardising matters for training. Length runs from 20 to 60 and weight from 0.02 to 0.8, so a model that adds them up mixes very different scales. A2 uses z-scores for feature scaling, and batch norm in B6 standardises numbers inside a network.

\[ z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\;e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} \]

  • \(\mu\) is the mean and \(\sigma\) the SD of the column (for a sample, the sample mean and SD).
  • \(f(x)\) is the height of the normal curve at \(x\). You rarely compute it by hand; the useful facts are the shares: 68.3% of the area within one SD, 95.4% within two, 99.7% within three.

A z-score depends on the group you standardise against. Tag 5519 is an 82.0 cm hammerhead. Among all 600 records (mean 54.0 cm, SD 22.8 cm) it has \(z = (82.0-54.0)/22.8 = 1.23\), so it looks long. Among the 57 hammerheads (mean 82.1 cm, SD 41.4 cm) its z-score is about 0, because it is a typical hammerhead.

The bell curve also gives a quick estimate. A spadenose shark of 50 cm has \(z = (50-39.1)/8.6 = 1.27\), and about 10.2% of a bell curve lies above that. The made-up table has 30 spadenose sharks above 50 cm out of 268, which is 11.2%. The two differ by one percentage point, so the bell curve is a fair shortcut for this column, as the 66% within one SD suggested.

Common belief. Measurements follow a bell curve.

Why it fails. Weight grows with length cubed, so weights pile up at the small end with a long tail to the right. Counts, waiting times and incomes are lopsided as well.

Better account. Look at the histogram first. Standardising works for any column, but the 68-95-99.7 rule applies only to columns that are close to a bell curve.

Debug tip. Compute the mean and SD on the training data only, then reuse those same two numbers on validation and test data. Standardising the validation and test sets with their own numbers lets test information leak into the model and hides shifts in the data (A4).

Quick quiz

A shark is 52 cm long. Lengths have mean 39.1 and SD 8.6. What is its z-score, and is it unusual?

\(z = (52 - 39.1)/8.6 = 1.5\). That is 1.5 SDs above the mean, large but not rare: about 7% of a bell curve lies above it.

After standardising, what are the mean and SD of the column?

The mean is 0 and the SD is 1, whatever the original units.

Spadenose lengths have mean 39.1 and SD 8.6. A shark has \(z = -2\). How long is it?

\(39.1 - 2\times8.6 = 21.9\) cm. Only about 2.3% of a bell curve lies that far below the mean.

Base rates and Bayes' rule

Guess first. A diabetes test flags 90% of the patients who have diabetes and clears 90% of those who do not. In one clinic, 8 patients in 100 have diabetes. A patient tests positive. How likely is it that they have diabetes: about 90%, about 50% or about 10%?

probability
a number from 0 to 1 for how often something happens in the long run
base rate
the share of all patients who have the condition, before any test
conditional probability \(P(A \mid B)\)
the probability of \(A\) among cases where \(B\) is true
Bayes' rule
turns "how often sick patients are flagged" into "how often flagged patients are sick", using the base rate

A positive result is only as trustworthy as the base rate allows: when few patients have the condition, most positive results come from the many who do not.

Example
Show

With 8 in 100 patients sick and a test that is 90% sensitive and 90% specific, the test flags 164 of 1,000 patients. Only 72 of the 164 (44%) have diabetes. Step through the four views, then move the sliders.

A circle is a patient, a thousand in all. Coral circles have diabetes and navy circles do not. A filled circle means the test flagged the patient and a ring means it cleared the patient. Sensitivity is the share of sick patients the test flags, and specificity is the share of healthy patients it clears (A4 grades models with both). Choose "Only the flagged". A flagged patient asks one question: of all the filled circles, how many are coral? With 8% prevalence the answer is 44%, because 92 of the 920 healthy patients are flagged by mistake, which is more than the 72 sick patients the test catches.

Try the second example. It uses a one-line rule on the Pima data: flag any woman whose glucose is 140 mg/dL or higher. The Pima data is a published dataset of 768 women of Pima heritage, measured in the United States and not in Mumbai. Among the 763 women with a glucose reading, the rule flags 51% of those with diabetes and clears 88% of those without. The Pima sample has a high base rate (268 of 768 women, 34.9%), so 69% of flagged women have diabetes. The same rule in a population where few have diabetes would be much less reliable. The third example shows a test that is 99% sensitive and 95% specific, with 1% prevalence. A flagged patient has only a 17% chance of having the condition.

\[ P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid \text{not } D)\,P(\text{not } D)} \]

  • \(D\) means the patient has diabetes and \(+\) means the test flags them. \(P(D \mid +)\) reads "the probability of \(D\) given \(+\)".
  • \(P(D)\) is the base rate. \(P(+ \mid D)\) is the sensitivity. \(P(+ \mid \text{not } D)\) is 1 minus the specificity.
  • Counting 1,000 patients, as above, is the same calculation in whole numbers. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) argued that counts like these make Bayesian reasoning easier than percentages do.

The rule is counting done twice. The share of patients who are sick and flagged can be written as \(P(D)\,P(+\mid D)\) or as \(P(+)\,P(D\mid +)\). Setting these equal and dividing by \(P(+)\) gives \(P(D\mid +)\). The flagged group has two sources, the sick who are flagged and the healthy who are flagged, which is why \(P(+)\) is the sum in the denominator.

Now the Pima rule, with a base rate of 0.349, a sensitivity of 0.508 and a specificity of 0.875. Sick and flagged: \(0.349\times0.508 = 0.177\). Healthy and flagged: \(0.651\times0.125 = 0.081\). So \(P(D\mid +) = 0.177/(0.177+0.081) = 0.685\), the 69% above. Use the same rule where only 5 patients in 100 are sick. The two products become \(0.0254\) and \(0.1188\), and the chance falls to \(0.0254/0.1442 = 0.176\). The rule is the same and the answer has changed, because the base rate changed.

Common belief. A test that is right 90% of the time makes a positive result 90% likely to be right.

Why it fails. The 90% answers a different question: of the sick, how many are flagged. A flagged patient needs the answer to: of the flagged, how many are sick. The two numbers differ whenever the base rate is low.

Better account. Count a population, split it by the base rate, apply the test, and look only at the flagged group.

Debug tip. If your answer to "how likely is the disease after a positive result" equals the test's sensitivity, you have swapped \(P(+ \mid D)\) and \(P(D \mid +)\). Redo the problem with counts and check that your four groups add up to the total.

Quick quiz

The same 90%/90% test is used where 35% of patients are sick. Does a flagged patient's chance of being sick rise or fall, and to what?

It rises, to about 83%: \(0.9 \times 0.35 = 0.315\) flagged and sick, against \(0.1 \times 0.65 = 0.065\) flagged and healthy, so \(0.315/0.380 = 0.83\).

Why can a test with 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity still give mostly false alarms?

If only 1% are sick, the 95% specificity still flags 5% of the 99% who are healthy. That is about 50 false alarms for 10 true cases.

A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. One patient in 1,000 has the condition. What is the chance that a flagged patient has it?

Sick and flagged: \(0.001\times0.99 = 0.00099\). Healthy and flagged: \(0.999\times0.01 = 0.00999\). The chance is \(0.00099/0.01098 = 9\%\).

Softmax and temperature

Guess first. A classifier gives a photo the scores 2, 1 and −1 for three species. Scores can be negative, but probabilities cannot, and the probabilities must add up to 1. How would you turn the scores into probabilities?

score (logit) \(z_k\)
the raw number a model gives to class \(k\) before any probabilities
softmax
raises \(e\) to the power of the scores and divides by the total, so the results add up to 1
temperature \(T\)
a number that divides the scores first: low makes the top class dominate, high flattens the result (D3 uses it to sample)
probability distribution
non-negative numbers, one per class, that add up to 1

Softmax turns any list of scores into a probability distribution, and the temperature sets how sharply the top score wins.

At T = 1 the top species gets 66%. At T = 0.3 it gets 98%. At T = 5 all five species get between 12% and 29%. The scores are made up, for one photo.

The scores never change in this figure. Only \(T\) does. Click a species to see the working for its probability. Press "Draw 100" to pick species at random from the probabilities: the outline shows how often the species came up, and it settles towards the bar as you draw more. At a low temperature, nearly all draws are the top species. At a high temperature, even Ganges sharks, the least likely choice, appear regularly. Language models use this dial when they pick the next word (D3).

\[ \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k = \frac{e^{z_k/T}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j/T}} \]

  • \(z_k\) is the score of class \(k\), and \(\sum_j\) adds over all classes.
  • \(T\) is the temperature. \(T = 1\) is plain softmax. As \(T \to 0\) the top class gets probability near 1, and as \(T\) grows all classes approach \(1/\text{(number of classes)}\).
  • Adding the same number to all scores leaves the probabilities unchanged, because \(e^{c}\) cancels in the fraction. Only differences between scores matter.

By hand, for the scores 2, 1 and \(-1\) of the guess. The exponentials are \(e^2 = 7.389\), \(e^1 = 2.718\) and \(e^{-1} = 0.368\), and they add up to 10.475. The probabilities are 0.705, 0.259 and 0.035. At \(T=2\) the scores become 1, 0.5 and \(-0.5\), the exponentials 2.718, 1.649 and 0.607, their total 4.974, and the probabilities 0.547, 0.331 and 0.122.

The first two probabilities have a ratio of \(0.705/0.259 = 2.72 = e^1\). In general the ratio of two probabilities is \(e\) raised to the gap between their scores. A gap of 1 makes one class 2.7 times as likely, and a gap of 2 makes it 7.4 times as likely. Temperature divides the gaps: at \(T=2\) the same gap of 1 gives a ratio of \(e^{0.5} = 1.65\).

Common belief. A softmax probability of 0.98 means the model is 98% sure it is right.

Why it fails. Softmax always produces numbers that add up to 1, even for a photo of a boat, and a model trained to be confident can give 0.98 to a wrong answer.

Better account. Treat the outputs as relative preferences. Whether 0.98 matches how often the model is right is tested on held-out data (A4).

Debug tip. Computing \(e^{z}\) for a large score overflows and returns nan. Subtract the largest score first: the probabilities are identical and no number gets large. Serious libraries do this.

import numpy as np

z = np.array([2.4, 1.2, 0.5, -0.4, -1.8])   # spadenose, milk, grey bamboo, hammerhead, Ganges

def softmax(z, T=1.0):
    e = np.exp((z - z.max()) / T)           # subtract the largest score: same answer, no overflow
    return e / e.sum()

for T in [0.3, 1.0, 5.0]:

Quick quiz

Scores of 3, 3 and 3 go into softmax. What probabilities come out?

One third for all three. Only the differences between scores matter, and here they are all zero.

You want a model to pick more adventurous answers. Raise or lower T?

Raise it. A higher temperature flattens the probabilities, so lower-scored answers get picked more often.

Two classes have scores 0 and \(\ln 3\) (about 1.1). What probabilities does softmax give?

\(e^0 = 1\) and \(e^{\ln 3} = 3\), so the probabilities are \(1/4\) and \(3/4\). A gap of \(\ln 3\) makes one class three times as likely.

Log probability and cross-entropy

Guess first. A model gives the true species a probability of 0.9 on one photo, 0.5 on another and 0.01 on a third. A good loss should charge a little, a moderate amount and a huge amount. Does a straight line, \(1 - p\), do that?

log probability
\(\ln p\), a number at or below 0 that is very negative for small \(p\)
cross-entropy loss
\(-\ln p\) for the probability \(p\) the model gave the true answer, averaged over examples
surprise
another name for \(-\ln p\): large when a likely-looking event does not happen

Cross-entropy charges \(-\ln p\) for the probability \(p\) given to the true answer. It is small when \(p\) is near 1 and climbs steeply as \(p\) falls towards 0.

True species

Moving p from 0.9 to 0.5 costs 0.59 extra loss, and moving it from 0.1 to 0.01 costs 2.30 extra. The loss grows without limit as p goes to 0.

Pick the true species and slide the score the model gives it. The bars show the softmax probabilities of the section above, and the dot shows where the loss lies on the \(-\ln p\) curve. The right-hand curve answers the guess: a straight line would charge only 1 for a confident wrong answer, while \(-\ln p\) charges 4.6 at \(p = 0.01\) and keeps climbing. Try "Make it confidently wrong". Accuracy only notes that the top pick changed. Cross-entropy also reacts to a model that is right with a shaky 0.34 instead of a firm 0.90.

Why a logarithm? The probability that a model gives to all of its training examples is a product of the individual probabilities, and a product of hundreds of small numbers underflows to zero. Taking logs turns the product into a sum, which is stable and which gradients handle well. Averaging the per-example loss over \(m\) examples gives the cross-entropy loss \(L\).

\[ \ell = -\ln p_{\text{true}} \qquad L = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \ln p^{(i)}_{\text{true}} \qquad \text{perplexity} = e^{L} \]

  • \(p_{\text{true}}\) is the probability the model gave the correct class, usually from a softmax. \(\ell\) is the loss on one example and \(L\) the average over a batch or data set.
  • If all \(K\) classes get equal probability, \(p = 1/K\) and \(\ell = \ln K\). With five species that is 1.609, a useful number to expect at the start of training (B8).
  • The slope of \(-\ln p\) is \(-1/p\) (0.6), so a confident mistake receives a very strong push to change.

The three photos of the guess cost \(-\ln 0.9 = 0.105\), \(-\ln 0.5 = 0.693\) and \(-\ln 0.01 = 4.605\). The straight line \(1-p\) would charge 0.1, 0.5 and 0.99. The log losses add up to 5.404, an average of 1.80, and the third photo supplies 4.605 of that total, which is 85%. One confident mistake dominates the loss, and that is the intended behaviour.

With softmax, the push on a score is simple. For the true class \(t\), the loss is \(-\ln p_t = -z_t + \ln\sum_j e^{z_j}\). The slope with respect to \(z_k\) is \(-1\) for \(k=t\) and 0 otherwise, from the first term, plus \(e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j} = p_k\) from the second. So the slope is \(p_k - y_k\), where \(y_k\) is 1 for the true class and 0 for the others. For the five scores of the last section with a milk shark as the truth, the slopes are 0.655, \(-0.803\), 0.098, 0.040 and 0.010. The milk score is pushed up with strength 0.80 and the others are pushed down in proportion to their probability. The code checks this by nudging.

Common belief. Cross-entropy is a fancy way to count mistakes. Accuracy would do.

Why it fails. Two models can have the same accuracy and very different losses, as the code below shows. Accuracy also has a slope of zero at nearly all points, so it cannot tell training which way to move.

Better account. Accuracy counts right and wrong. Cross-entropy grades the probabilities, rewards well-judged uncertainty and punishes a confident mistake, and it has a slope at all points.

Debug tip. A probability of 0 gives \(-\ln 0 = \infty\). Libraries compute the loss from the scores directly, or clip \(p\) to a tiny positive number. If your loss turns to inf or nan, check for a zero probability first.

Running averages

Guess first. Daily water temperature readings jump around by about half a degree. You want a steady estimate that still reacts when the monsoon cools the sea. Would you average all readings so far, only the last 5, or something else?

running mean
the mean of all readings so far
exponential moving average (EMA)
an average that moves a fixed fraction of the way towards a new reading
decay \(\beta\)
the share of the old average that is kept, between 0 and 1

An exponential moving average follows noisy readings smoothly: a decay near 1 smooths a lot and reacts slowly, and a decay near 0 smooths little and reacts quickly.

After the sea cools on day 60, the running mean of all days drifts down slowly. An EMA with \(\beta = 0.9\) is back near the new level within about 20 days. The readings are made up.

The sand line is the running mean. It treats a reading from day 3 the same as one from yesterday, so after day 60 it keeps remembering the warm weeks. The coral line is the EMA. A new reading pulls it a fraction \(1-\beta\) of the way. Slide \(\beta\) towards 0 and the coral line hugs the readings. Slide it towards 0.99 and it becomes smooth and sluggish. The lower plot shows the weights: a reading \(k\) days old counts \((1-\beta)\beta^k\), which shrinks by the same factor per day. The weights add up to 1, and the effective window is about \(1/(1-\beta)\) days.

\[ \bar{x}_t = \beta\,\bar{x}_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\,x_t \qquad \bar{x}_t = (1-\beta)\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\beta^{k}\,x_{t-k} \]

  • \(x_t\) is the reading on day \(t\) and \(\bar{x}_t\) the moving average after day \(t\). \(\beta\) is the decay.
  • The second form shows that the EMA is a weighted mean of all past readings, with weights \((1-\beta)\beta^k\) for a reading \(k\) days old.
  • Momentum and Adam (B5) use this update on gradients, with \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\).

By hand with \(\beta=0.9\), starting from 0. After the first reading, \(\bar{x}_1 = 0.9\times0 + 0.1\times28.4 = 2.84\). After the second, \(\bar{x}_2 = 0.9\times2.84 + 0.1\times28.9 = 2.556 + 2.89 = 5.446\). Dividing by \(1-\beta^t\) repairs the start: \(2.84/0.1 = 28.4\) and \(5.446/0.19 = 28.66\). The weights on today's reading and the two before it are 0.1, 0.09 and 0.081.

How fast does an EMA react? After a jump in the readings it closes the share \(1-\beta^k\) of the gap in \(k\) days. For \(\beta=0.9\), 10 days close 65% and 22 days close 90%. For \(\beta=0.99\) the 90% mark takes \(\ln 0.1/\ln 0.99 = 229\) days. A slow average smooths well and also reports old news.

Analogy. A leaky bucket. Day by day you pour in a small share of today's water, and a share of the old water leaks out.

Where it breaks. A bucket eventually forgets old water completely. An EMA never does: its weights shrink but never reach zero. A moving average of the last \(N\) readings, by contrast, drops a reading entirely on day \(N+1\).

Debug tip. If you start an EMA at 0, its first values are biased towards 0. Choose the "Start at 0" option in the figure. Adam divides by \(1-\beta^t\) to fix this, which is why that factor appears in B5.

Expectation and sampling noise

Guess first. SharkCam sees 0, 1, 2 or 3 sharks on a dive, and the long-run average is 0.75. After 10 dives the average could be 0.4, 0.75 or 1.2. After 1,000 dives, how close do you expect it to be?

expectation \(E[X]\)
the long-run average: outcomes times their probabilities, added up
sampling noise
the random gap between an average from a few cases and the long-run value
standard error
the typical size of that gap for an average of \(n\) cases: \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\)

An average from \(n\) cases wanders around the expectation by about \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), so four times as many cases halve the wobble.

0 × 0.50no sharks, half the dives+1 × 0.301 shark+2 × 0.152 sharks+3 × 0.053 sharks=0.75E[X], sharks per dive

After 10 dives an average is typically within ±0.55 of 0.75. After 1,000 dives it is within ±0.055. The dive counts are simulated from the made-up probabilities above.

The eight lines are simulated runs: the average sightings per dive after 1, 2, 3 and more dives. Early averages swing widely because a single dive counts for a lot. Later averages settle near the expectation. The dotted funnel marks where 95% of averages fall. The funnel narrows like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), which is slow: to halve the wobble you need four times the data. Press "New runs" to see that no single run is special. The same arithmetic applies to a model's test score, which is an average of right-or-wrong results over the test set.

\[ E[X] = \sum_k p_k\,x_k \qquad \mathrm{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

  • \(p_k\) is the probability of outcome \(x_k\). The sum runs over all outcomes.
  • \(\sigma\) is the SD of one case, here \(\sqrt{0.7875} = 0.887\) sharks. SE is the standard error, and about 95% of averages fall within 1.96 SE of the expectation.

The numbers behind the funnel. First \(E[X^2] = 0\times0.5 + 1\times0.3 + 4\times0.15 + 9\times0.05 = 1.35\). The variance is \(1.35 - 0.75^2 = 0.7875\) and the SD is 0.887. Then \(\mathrm{SE} = 0.887/\sqrt{10} = 0.28\) for 10 dives, so 95% of averages fall within \(1.96\times0.28 = 0.55\) of 0.75. For 1,000 dives the SE is 0.028 and the range is 0.055.

Why \(\sqrt{n}\)? The variance of a sum of independent cases is the sum of their variances, \(n\sigma^2\). The mean divides the sum by \(n\), which divides the variance by \(n^2\), so the variance of the mean is \(\sigma^2/n\) and its SD is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

The formula needs independent cases. The 2,400 frames of the made-up dive log come from one dive at one frame per second, and neighbouring frames look nearly alike. They count as far fewer than 2,400 independent cases, so the true wobble of a score on them is larger than \(\sigma/\sqrt{2400}\) says.

Common belief. A model's test score is a fixed property of the model.

Why it fails. The score is an average over one test set. Another set of the same size would give a different score, with a wobble of about \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

Better account. A score is an estimate with noise. Report how large the test set was, and how large the wobble is (the next two sections).

Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

Guess first. A model gets 121 of 154 held-out women right, which is 78.6%. If you tested it on a different 154 women, would it score the same 78.6% again? How far could it drift?

bootstrap
imitating new test sets by drawing cases from your test set with replacement
resample
one such draw, the same size as the original set
95% confidence interval
a range that a method builds so that it catches the true value in about 95 of 100 repeated studies

A test score comes with a range. Resampling the test set shows how far the score would drift, and the middle 95% of the resampled scores is a confidence interval.

A score of 121 right out of 154 has a 95% interval of about 72% to 85%. With 1,500 women the interval narrows to about 76% to 81%. Press "Resample 200" first, then try "Run 100 studies".

The score 121 of 154 is that of the rebuilt XGBoost model in my diabetes screening project. To see how much it could drift, the bootstrap acts as if the 154 women were the whole population. A resample draws 154 women from them with replacement, so some women appear twice and some not at all, and scores the model on that draw. Press "Resample 200" and the histogram builds. The middle 95% of the resampled scores, shown in teal, is the interval. Raise the slider and the interval narrows like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), the same law as in the last section.

What does "95%" mean? Switch to "100 repeated studies". The lines are separate studies, with a new test set, a new score and its own interval. The teal line is the true score, which we know only because this is a simulation. About 95 of the 100 intervals cross it, and about 5 (coral) miss. The 95% describes the method across repeated studies. Any single interval either contains the truth or does not.

\[ \hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\,(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \]

  • \(\hat{p}\), read "p hat", is the observed share right (here 121/154 = 0.786) and \(n\) the test set size.
  • The square root is the standard error of a share. The 1.96 comes from the normal curve: 95% of the area lies within 1.96 SDs. For 121 of 154 the formula gives 72.1% to 85.1%, matching the bootstrap.

The formula by hand. The share is \(\hat{p} = 121/154 = 0.786\). Then \(\hat{p}(1-\hat{p}) = 0.786\times0.214 = 0.168\), and dividing by 154 gives 0.00109. The root is the standard error, 0.033. Times 1.96 this is 0.065, so the interval is \(0.786 \pm 0.065\), from 0.721 to 0.851. With 1,500 women the standard error is 0.0106, the margin 0.021, and the interval runs from 0.765 to 0.806.

For few cases the formula breaks. If a model finds 2 of the 3 Ganges sharks, \(\hat{p} = 0.67\), and the formula gives 0.13 to 1.20. An upper end above 100% is impossible. The bootstrap interval of three cases runs from 0 to 1. Both results say that three cases tell you very little about recall.

Common belief. A 95% interval means there is a 95% chance that the true value is inside this one.

Why it fails. The true value is a fixed number, and a given interval either has it or misses it. What varies from study to study is the interval.

Better account. The method catches the truth in about 95 of 100 repeated studies, and the figure shows it. Treat the interval as the range of values the data does not rule out.

Debug tip. The bootstrap only describes noise from a finite test set. Suppose the test women come from a different population than the people the model will meet, such as Pima women versus Mumbai patients. Then the interval is narrow and still misleading (C3, F1).

Is model B better than model A?

Guess first. SharkCam's model A gets 82.0% of 200 test frames right. A new model B gets 85.5%. Is B better, or could this gap come from luck in which frames were chosen?

null hypothesis
the claim that no special effect exists: here, that the two models are equally good
p-value
how often luck alone would produce a gap at least this large if the null hypothesis were true
paired comparison
grading both models on the same frames, so the frames form small contests

Only the frames where the models disagree say which is better. If B wins most of them, ask how often a fair coin would give a split that lopsided.

When B wins 11 of the 15 frames where the models disagree, a fair coin does as well or better in either direction about 12% of the time. That is too often to call B better. The frame results are made up.

Of the 200 frames, 160 are right for both models and 25 are wrong for both. Those 185 do not show which model is better. The other 15 are the contest: A alone is right on 4 frames and B alone on 11. If the models were equally good, all 15 would be coin flips, and the bars show how likely the different numbers of B wins are. The coral bars mark splits at least as lopsided as 11 to 4, in either direction. They add up to the p-value of 0.118.

Now use the sliders. With 60 frames of disagreement and B winning 40, the p-value falls to 0.013, which is hard to explain by luck. Press the coin button to run 2,000 imaginary worlds of coin flips: the diamonds are the frequencies you get by simulation, and they track the calculated bars. Note what the test does not say. It does not say how large B's edge is, or whether the edge is worth the cost of switching. The confidence interval of the last section addresses size.

\[ p\text{-value} = 2\sum_{k=b}^{d}\binom{d}{k}\frac{1}{2^{d}} \qquad \text{(for } b \ge d/2\text{)} \]

  • \(d\) is the number of frames where only one model is right and \(b\) the number B wins (the larger of the two counts).
  • \(\binom{d}{k}\) counts the ways \(k\) of \(d\) coin flips can come up heads (0.4). Dividing by \(2^d\) turns the count into a probability, and the 2 adds the equally lopsided split in the other direction. This is the sign test, a simple form of McNemar's test.

How many wins are enough? With 15 disagreements, a split of 12 to 3 gives \(2\times(455+105+15+1)/32{,}768 = 0.035\), under 0.05. A split of 11 to 4 gives 0.118. So B needs 12 wins out of 15. The same accuracy gap can also count for more or less. B winning 11 to 4 is a net gain of 7 frames with \(p=0.118\). B winning 17 to 10 is also a net gain of 7, over 27 disagreements, and gives \(p=0.248\). More disagreement means more noise around the same net gain.

With many disagreements the bell curve is a good shortcut. Under coin flips the wins of B have mean \(d/2\) and SD \(\sqrt{d}/2\). For 40 of 60, \(z = (39.5-30)/3.87 = 2.45\), where 39.5 includes a half-step correction. The two-sided p-value is 0.014, close to the 0.013 of the full count.

Common belief. A p-value of 0.12 means a 12% chance that the models are equal, so B is 88% likely to be better.

Why it fails. The p-value assumes the models are equal and asks how surprising the data are. It does not give the probability that the assumption is true.

Better account. A p-value of 0.12 says that luck alone would produce a split this lopsided about 1 time in 8. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement adds that a p-value does not measure the size or importance of an effect. It also says that a decision should not rest only on whether a p-value passes a threshold such as 0.05.

In practice. The test needs disagreements. Two models that are nearly identical produce few of them, and then no test set of a sensible size can separate them. Libraries offer this paired test as McNemar's test.

Quick quiz

Why do the 160 frames that both models get right play no part in the test?

They do not separate the models. Only a frame where one model is right and the other wrong can favour either one.

A p-value is 0.03. Does that prove B is much better?

No. It says luck alone rarely gives a split this lopsided. B's edge could still be tiny, and with enough frames even a tiny edge gives a small p-value.

B wins 9 of the 10 frames where the models disagree. Is the two-sided p-value below 0.05?

Yes. \(2\times(10+1)/1024 = 0.021\). With so few disagreements, a split of 9 to 1 is needed. A split of 8 to 2 gives 0.109.

Correlation and causation

Guess first. On warm days, ice-cream sales at Juhu beach go up, and so do shark sightings. A councillor proposes banning ice-cream sales to cut sightings. Would it work?

correlation \(r\)
a number from −1 to 1 for how closely two columns rise and fall together
confounder
a third quantity that drives both columns
causation
changing one thing would change the other
intervention
setting a value by hand, instead of watching it vary

Two columns can move together because a third drives both. The test of causation is what happens when you set one of them by hand.

Over all 150 made-up days, ice cream and sightings correlate at r = 0.89. Within a band of ±0.4 °C around 27 °C the correlation is −0.10, and banning ice cream leaves sightings unchanged.

A dot is a day: ice-cream sales across, sightings up, colour for water temperature. Warm days (coral) appear up and to the right, cool days (navy) down and to the left, so the cloud slopes upward. Now narrow the window to days with nearly the same temperature. The slope disappears: once the water is held fixed, ice-cream sales say little about sightings. Then try "Ban ice cream". The dots move to zero sales and the sightings stay put, because sightings follow the water. The diagram at the top shows the structure: temperature drives both, and no arrow runs between them. (In small windows, with only a dozen days, r wobbles by chance, as the last section would predict.)

The correlation \(r\) can be computed with the tool from section 2: standardise both columns, multiply the z-scores day by day and average the products. It is +1 when the points lie on a rising line and −1 on a falling line.

\[ r = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_x^{(i)}\, z_y^{(i)} \]

  • \(z_x^{(i)}\) and \(z_y^{(i)}\) are the z-scores of day \(i\) for the two columns, and \(n\) the number of days.
  • Written as vectors of z-scores, \(r = \mathbf{z}_x\cdot\mathbf{z}_y / n\): a dot product (0.5) that equals the cosine of the angle between them.

A small case by hand: five made-up days with water temperatures 27, 28, 29, 30 and 31 and sightings 2, 1, 4, 3 and 5. The z-scores of temperature are \(-1.41\), \(-0.71\), 0, 0.71 and 1.41. Those of sightings are \(-0.71\), \(-1.41\), 0.71, 0 and 1.41. The products are 1, 1, 0, 0 and 2, and their mean is \(r = 0.8\).

Correlation sees only straight-line trends. If \(x\) runs from \(-2\) to 2 and \(y = x^2\), then \(y\) is fixed by \(x\) and still \(r=0\), because the left side falls while the right side rises. Correlation also needs enough days. As a rough rule, the size of \(r\) must exceed about \(1.96/\sqrt{n}\) to differ from zero, which is 0.16 for 150 days and 0.57 for 12 days. That is why \(r\) wobbles in the narrow windows of the figure.

Common belief. If two things move together, one causes the other.

Why it fails. A third quantity may drive both (a confounder, as here), the arrow may run the other way, or a small sample may produce a pattern by chance.

Better account. Ask what happens when you set one of them by hand, which is an experiment, or compare cases that match on the likely confounder, as the narrow window does. Models learn correlations, so a shark detector may pick up "blue water" instead of sharks (B9).

Debug tip. One \(r\) for a pooled data set can mislead. Mixing two groups can create a correlation that neither group has, or hide one that both have. Colour the points by group before trusting a single number.

Quick quiz

Hospitals with more doctors have more deaths. Name a confounder.

Hospital size, or how ill the patients are. Large hospitals treat more and sicker patients, which raises both the number of doctors and the number of deaths.

How would you test whether ice cream affects sightings?

Intervene: set sales high on some days and low on others at random, with the water temperature varying freely, and compare sightings. Or compare days of nearly equal temperature, as the figure does.

Two columns have \(r = 0\). Does that mean they are unrelated?

No. \(r\) measures straight-line trends only. If \(y = x^2\) for \(x\) from \(-2\) to 2, then \(r=0\) while \(y\) depends fully on \(x\).

The loss is \(-\ln p\) and its slope is \(-1/p\) (0.6). Use the slope to estimate how much the loss falls when \(p\) rises from 0.50 to 0.51, then compare with the true change.

The slope at 0.5 is \(-2\), so a rise of 0.01 lowers the loss by about 0.02. The true change is \(\ln 0.51 - \ln 0.50 = 0.0198\) lower. The slope gives a close estimate for a small nudge.

The correlation is \(r = \mathbf{z}_x\cdot\mathbf{z}_y/n\). What is the length of a z-score vector, and what does \(r\) equal in terms of vectors from 0.5?

The squares of the z-scores average to 1 (the SD is 1), so the vector's length is \(\sqrt{n}\). Then \(\mathbf{z}_x\cdot\mathbf{z}_y/n\) is the dot product of two vectors of length \(\sqrt{n}\) divided by \(n\), which is the cosine of the angle between them. So \(r\) is the cosine similarity of the two standardised columns.

A friend says that a p-value of 0.04 means there is a 96% chance model B is better. What is wrong, and what would you say instead?

The p-value assumes the models are equal and gives how often luck would produce a result this lopsided: about 4% of the time. The p-value does not give the probability that either model is better. Say: if the models were equal, a gap this large would be unusual, and then give the size of the gap with an interval.
