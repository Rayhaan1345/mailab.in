Part B · Chapter 9

Explaining a network

Where a photo classifier looks, and why a map that looks right is not yet proof

A network says "hammerhead" with 73%. Why? This chapter covers the main ways to ask. Hide parts of the photo and watch the score (occlusion). Follow the slope of the score back to the pixels (saliency). Colour the last grid of features by how much it matters (Grad-CAM). Then come integrated gradients, and SHAP in brief. The maps are then tested, because a map can look convincing and still mislead.

The figures use a pretrained ResNet-18 and a vision transformer on the SharkCam photos, with credits shown. Both were trained on ImageNet, which has hammerhead, tiger shark and great white shark classes. All results are for these networks and photos. The chapter ends with spurious cues, where the water decides the answer, and with the limits of attention maps.

You will need: the slopes sent back through a network (B4), the gradient (0.6), SHAP values (A6) and the idea of a baseline (A4). A convolutional network is a stack of filters that slide over the image (C1, C2). This chapter needs only that it ends in a small grid of feature maps.

Occlusion: cover and watch

Guess first. You cover part of the hammerhead photo with a grey square. Where must the square go to make the network's "hammerhead" score fall most: over the head, over the fin, or over the plain water?

score
the network's probability for one class, here "hammerhead" (0.7)
occlusion
hiding a patch of the input and measuring how the score changes
patch
the square that is hidden, filled with a grey that is the average colour of the training photos

The score falls most when the patch hides what the network relies on, so sliding the patch over the photo gives a map of that evidence.

A 112 px patch placed at x = 152, y = 48 cuts the hammerhead score from 73% to 2%. The same patch in three of the four corners leaves it at 71% to 80%, and at the lower right corner, by the tail, at 62%. The bar shows the score, and the black tick the score of the whole photo. Computed offline: a grid of 15 by 35 scores for the 112 px patch, one forward pass per position (steps of 8 px).

The answer to the guess is "the head and body", not the fin or the water. The lowest scores come when the patch covers the shark's head and front body. The 72 px patch at best gives 39%, and the 112 px patch gives 2%. Press "map shown" to see the whole grid at once: the coral glow is where hiding the patch costs the most. Zeiler and Fergus (2014, section 4.2) used a grey square for this. They asked whether a network locates the object or relies on its surroundings. The correct-class probability fell when the object was covered.

Switch to the dolphin photo, where the network's score for the class "great white shark" is 41% (its top choice is "grey whale", 52%). Hiding a 72 px patch on the animal's back cuts the shark score to 6%. Even a patch in the top-right corner, which contains only water and its reflection, cuts it to 20%. The plain-water patch matters here, and the next sections show how far it matters.

0.725whole photo−0.39072 px patch at the head=0.335drop, 34 points|0.725whole photo−0.69672 px patch in the water corner=0.029drop, 3 points

\[ \Delta(r) \;=\; p_c(x) \;-\; p_c\big(x \text{ with region } r \text{ hidden}\big) \]

  • \(p_c(x)\) is the network's probability for class \(c\) on the photo \(x\). \(r\) is a square region of the photo, hidden by replacing its pixels with the mean colour.
  • \(\Delta(r)\) is the drop in the score. It is large where \(r\) hides evidence for \(c\). The occlusion map is \(\Delta(r)\) for all positions of \(r\).
import numpy as np

def occlusion_map(score, img, patch=3, stride=1, fill=0.0):
    """Score of the image with a patch hidden, for patch positions on a grid."""
    h, w = img.shape
    rows = range(0, h - patch + 1, stride)
    cols = range(0, w - patch + 1, stride)
    out = np.zeros((len(rows), len(cols)))
    for i, y in enumerate(rows):
        for j, x in enumerate(cols):
            hidden = img.copy()
            hidden[y:y + patch, x:x + patch] = fill        # a grey patch
            out[i, j] = score(hidden)
    return out

# a made-up 8 by 8 "photo": a bright 2 by 3 "fin" on a dark background
img = np.zeros((8, 8)); img[2:4, 3:6] = 1.0
score = lambda im: im[1:5, 2:7].mean()                    # a made-up scorer: brightness in the middle
base = score(img)
m = occlusion_map(score, img, patch=3)
print("score of the whole image: %.3f" % base)
print("score with the patch hidden at each position (rows = top edge, columns = left edge):")
print(np.round(m, 2))
y, x = np.unravel_index(m.argmin(), m.shape)
print("lowest score %.2f when the patch covers rows %d-%d and columns %d-%d" % (m.min(), y, y + 2, x, x + 2))

The code is the whole method in a dozen lines. It works on any scorer, so it needs no access to the inside of the network, which is its main strength. The made-up scorer reads the brightness in the middle, and the map says what to expect. The score falls to 0 when the 3 by 3 patch covers all the bright cells of the fin (rows 1 to 3, columns 3 to 5). It falls partly when the patch covers some of them.

Where it misleads. The map depends on three choices: the patch size, the step and the fill. A small patch (40 px) never hides enough of a large object to move the score. The lowest score of any 40 px patch on the hammerhead is 54%. A patch as large as the shark hides it all. A grey fill is not "no information": it is an odd colour block that the network never saw. In the dolphin photo a grey patch over the animal cuts the shark score to 6%. As the next sections show, painting the same area with water raises it to 93%. The fill changes the answer. The cost is also high: the grid of the 72 px patch needs 800 forward passes, one at all of the 40 by 20 positions. In practice, use a step of a quarter to a half of the patch size and try two patch sizes. Treat the map as a test of one question: what if this region were missing?

Debug tip. Check the score with no patch (the black tick) before reading the map. If it is already low, as for the market photo (the hammerhead class has 19%), differences between positions are small and noisy, and the map explains very little.

Quick quiz

A 384 by 224 photo, a 72 px patch and a step of 24 px. How many forward passes does the occlusion map need?

Positions across: \(\lfloor(384-72)/24\rfloor + 1 = 14\). Positions down: \(\lfloor(224-72)/24\rfloor + 1 = 7\). That is \(14 \times 7 = 98\) passes, against 800 for a step of 8.

Why can the same patch give a large drop in one photo and a tiny drop in another?

The drop depends on how much of the evidence the patch hides, and on how the network combines evidence. The network looked at the shark's body in the first photo and at the water around the animal in the other. A patch of the same size hides different amounts of what the score relies on.

Saliency: the slope as a map

Guess first. Backpropagation (B4) passes slopes back to the first layer, and one more step gives the slope of the score with respect to the input numbers. If you draw those slopes as a picture, will the picture show the shark, the water or noise? And would the same network with random weights draw a similar picture?

saliency map
for one photo and one class, the size of the slope of the class score with respect to the pixels (0.6)
logit
the score before the softmax; saliency uses it, because the softmax squeezes large scores together
gradient × input
the slope times the pixel value, which says how much of the score this pixel supplies in a linear approximation
sanity check
a test that a method must pass, such as giving a different map when the weights are random

The slope of the class score with respect to a pixel says how fast the score moves if the pixel changes. Large slopes point at pixels the score is sensitive to.

For the trained network the slopes gather on the shark's body, with grain in the water. With the same architecture and random weights the map is spread evenly like static, and the rank correlation between the two maps is 0.20. Slope of the class score with respect to the pixels, largest over the three colours, as a coral overlay; ResNet-18 trained on ImageNet, with the class named in the readout.

Choose the photos in turn. The answer to the guess is "the shark and grain, and no, not similar". In the trained network the slope map concentrates on the animal. The random network has no preference, and its map is uniform noise with a faint frame. That is the point of the sanity check of Adebayo and colleagues (2018). A method that gives the same map for random weights shows the image's own edges and not the model. Their abstract reports that some popular saliency methods are independent of the model. They found that plain gradients and Grad-CAM pass the check and that Guided Backpropagation does not. Plain gradient saliency also passes in these four photos, with rank correlations between 0.14 and 0.20. This page does not repeat the check for Grad-CAM. Simonyan, Vedaldi and Zisserman (2014) introduced this map for convolutional networks.

The tiny network of B4 gives the calculation. Its score \(z_o\) depends on the two inputs \(x_1\) (length) and \(x_2\) (girth). Saliency is the slope of \(z_o\) with respect to the inputs, found with the same chain rule: \(\partial z_o/\partial x_i = \sum_j v_j\,(1-h_j^2)\,W_{ji}\) for the tanh units. For Tag 3305 with \(x = (0.22, 0.21)\) the hidden values are \(h = (0.149, -0.140)\), so \(1-h_j^2 = (0.978, 0.980)\).

0.9 × 0.978 × 0.8 + (−0.8) × 0.980 × (−0.4)∂z/∂x₁ = 0.704 + 0.314=1.018length raises the score|0.9 × 0.978 × (−0.6) + (−0.8) × 0.980 × 0.7∂z/∂x₂ = −0.528 − 0.549=−1.077girth lowers it|(0.224, −0.226)gradient × input

Both inputs matter about equally, and in opposite directions: more length raises the score for "adult", more girth (at the same length) lowers it. This is the slope that B4's backward pass already held, as \(\partial L/\partial x = (-0.670, 0.709)\). That slope of the loss is the slope of the score times the loss factor \(p - y = -0.658\), and \(-0.658 \times 1.018 = -0.670\). The gradient times the input, \((0.224, -0.226)\), says how much the inputs supply of the score in a straight-line approximation.

\[ S_i \;=\; \Big|\,\frac{\partial\, y_c}{\partial x_i}\Big| \qquad\quad \text{gradient}\times\text{input:}\;\; x_i\,\frac{\partial\, y_c}{\partial x_i} \]

  • \(y_c\) is the score (logit) of class \(c\) for the photo and \(x_i\) is one input number, here one colour value of one pixel. \(S_i\) is the saliency of that input. The picture takes the largest \(S_i\) over the three colours of a pixel.
  • The slope is the derivative of 0.6: the change of \(y_c\) for a small change of \(x_i\), with all other inputs fixed.

Where it misleads. A slope is local. It says what happens for a tiny change of a pixel, not what happens if the pixel were removed. A pixel can be vital and have slope 0, if a ReLU or a saturated unit just downstream is flat there. A pixel can also have a large slope that matters little for the score. A ReLU network is a patchwork of straight pieces, so the map is grainy: the pictures above are noisy even when the trend is clear. Smoothing the map over several noisy copies of the photo (SmoothGrad) is a common remedy. In practice, use saliency to find where to look, and test the claim with a method that changes the photo, such as occlusion or the deletion test below.

Debug tip. A map explains the class you ask for, not the class the network chose. For the dolphin photo the top choice is "grey whale" and the map above is for "great white shark". Print the class and the score next to the map.

Quick quiz

Why does saliency use the score before the softmax and not the probability?

The probability saturates near 1 for a confident class, so its slope with respect to the pixels is close to 0 although the network relies on the pixels. The logit has no such flat region.

For the B4 network at Tag 3305, which input has the larger saliency, and which pushes the score up?

Girth has the slightly larger size (1.077 against 1.018), and length pushes the score up while girth pushes it down.

Grad-CAM and four maps side by side

Guess first. The last block of ResNet-18 turns the 384 by 224 photo into a grid of 12 by 7 cells, with 512 numbers in a cell (the 512 feature maps). To colour the 12 by 7 grid for "hammerhead", you need one weight per map, which is 512 numbers to find. Where could such weights come from?

feature map
one grid of numbers from one filter, with one value per region of the photo (C1)
Grad-CAM
a coarse map made by weighting the feature maps of the last convolution block by the mean slope of the class score
channel weight \(\alpha_k\)
the mean slope of the score with respect to feature map \(k\)

A feature map the score increases with gets a positive weight, and the weighted sum of the maps, with negative values cut off, shows which regions supply the evidence.

For the hammerhead in Costa Rica all four maps put the weight on the shark's body and head. Grad-CAM is coarse (a cell is 32 by 32 pixels), the gradient and integrated-gradient maps are grainy, and the occlusion map is smooth. ResNet-18, class "hammerhead" (73%). The earlier block gives a finer 14 by 24 grid and a less class-specific picture.

The answer to the guess is "the slopes". For channel \(k\), the weight \(\alpha_k\) is the mean over all cells of the slope \(\partial y_c/\partial A^k_{ij}\). This is the slope of the class score with respect to that channel's value in the cell. A channel that raises the score gets a positive weight, one that lowers it a negative weight. The map is the weighted sum of the 512 channel grids. A ReLU removes the negative total, which keeps only the cells that increase the score (Selvaraju and colleagues, 2017). Numbers from the hammerhead photo: of the 512 weights, 209 are positive; the map has \(7 \times 12 = 84\) cells.

0.5 × [[1, 0], [2, 1]]channel 1, weight +0.5+−0.2 × [[0, 3], [1, 0]]channel 2, weight −0.2=[[0.5, −0.6], [0.8, 0.5]]weighted sum→[[0.5, 0], [0.8, 0.5]]after ReLU: the map

The box is a made-up toy with two channels and a 2 by 2 grid (the numbers are not from a network). Channel 2 has a large value in the top-right cell, but its weight is negative, so that cell ends below 0 and is cut. Channel 1 has the largest value (2) in the bottom-left cell and a positive weight, so that cell wins, with 0.8. The same steps, with 512 channels and 84 cells, give the map of the figure.

\[ \alpha_k \;=\; \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial\, y_c}{\partial A^{k}_{ij}} \qquad\quad L_c \;=\; \mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\sum_{k}\alpha_k\,A^{k}\Big) \]

  • \(A^k_{ij}\) is the value of feature map \(k\) in cell \((i,j)\) of the last convolution block, and \(y_c\) the score of class \(c\). \(Z\) is the number of cells (84 here), so \(\alpha_k\) is the average slope of the score with respect to that map.
  • \(L_c\) is the Grad-CAM map: a weighted sum of the maps with weights \(\alpha_k\), with negative values set to 0. In words: add up the feature maps, counting a map by how much it raises the score.
import numpy as np, torch
from PIL import Image
from torchvision.models import resnet18, ResNet18_Weights

net = resnet18(weights=ResNet18_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1).eval()
mean = torch.tensor([0.485, 0.456, 0.406]).view(1, 3, 1, 1)
std = torch.tensor([0.229, 0.224, 0.225]).view(1, 3, 1, 1)
img = np.asarray(Image.open("photos/h2.jpg").convert("RGB"))     # 224 high, 384 wide
x = ((torch.tensor(img).permute(2, 0, 1).float() / 255).unsqueeze(0) - mean) / std
HAMMERHEAD = 4                                                    # ImageNet class index

# saliency: the slope of the class score with respect to every pixel value
x1 = x.clone().requires_grad_(True)
net(x1)[0, HAMMERHEAD].backward()
sal = x1.grad.abs().max(1).values[0]                              # largest over R, G, B
print("score before softmax: %.2f" % net(x)[0, HAMMERHEAD].item(),
      "| saliency map", tuple(sal.shape), "max %.4f" % sal.max())

# Grad-CAM: weight each channel of the last conv block by its mean slope
acts = {}
net.layer4.register_forward_hook(lambda m, i, o: acts.update(a=o))
score = net(x)[0, HAMMERHEAD]
grads = torch.autograd.grad(score, acts["a"])[0]
alpha = grads.mean((2, 3), keepdim=True)                          # one weight per channel
cam = torch.relu((alpha * acts["a"]).sum(1))[0].detach()
cam = cam / cam.max()
print("Grad-CAM map", tuple(cam.shape),
      "weights: %d channels, %d positive" % (alpha.numel(), (alpha > 0).sum()))
print(np.round(cam.numpy(), 1))

Where it misleads. Grad-CAM is coarse, since one cell covers a 32 by 32 pixel block, and it needs a network that ends in a spatial grid of features. The earlier block's map is finer but less tied to the class. Choose the market photo: the network gives "hammerhead" only 19% (its first choice is "sturgeon", 44%), so these maps explain a weak class and should not be over-read. A map shows where the network's evidence for a class lies, assuming the class is the right question. In practice, show maps for the top choice and for the class you care about, with the scores, and never show a single map as the explanation.

Quick quiz

Why is a ReLU applied to the weighted sum in Grad-CAM?

Cells with a negative total supply evidence against the class. The map is meant to show evidence for it, so those cells are set to 0.

In the toy example, change the weight of channel 2 to +0.2. What is the map?

The sum is \(0.5\,[[1,0],[2,1]] + 0.2\,[[0,3],[1,0]] = [[0.5, 0.6],[1.2, 0.5]]\), all positive, so the ReLU leaves it as it is, and the top-right cell is now bright.

Integrated gradients, SHAP, and a deletion test

Guess first. Four methods give four different maps of one photo, and you cannot ask the network which is right. How could you decide? Think of a test that uses the photo and the score, and not the map's own logic.

integrated gradients
the sum of the slopes along a straight path from a baseline image to the photo, times the change in the pixel
baseline
the reference image that means "no information", here a black image
completeness
the attributions add up to the difference between the score of the photo and the score of the baseline
deletion test
remove the pixels a map ranks highest first, and watch how fast the score falls

A map should be trusted only after a test that does not rely on the map's own logic, and deleting what the map ranks highest is the simplest such test.

After removing 10% of the Costa Rica photo, the score is 11% when blocks are ranked by integrated gradients, 14% by Grad-CAM, 10% by occlusion and 45% by saliency. It is 78% when blocks are removed in random order. Blocks of 8 by 8 pixels set to the mean colour; one network; the random curve is the mean of 20 random orders. Only the two hammerhead photos are used: the shark scores of the others are too fragile for this test.

The answer to the guess is the deletion test, a faithfulness check in the spirit of Zeiler and Fergus's occlusion. Petsiuk, Das and Saenko (2018) use it, with an insertion test, to compare maps. If a map ranks the evidence correctly, deleting the top 10% of blocks should cut the score much faster than deleting 10% at random. Three methods do: integrated gradients, Grad-CAM and the occlusion map all reach about 10 to 14% of the starting score at 10% removed, against 78% for random order. The plain saliency map is much weaker (45%): it finds the shark but not the pixels that the score depends on most. For the Jarvis Island photo only integrated gradients (35% left at 10% removed) and Grad-CAM (45%) beat random order (71%). Saliency (82%) and the occlusion map (83%) do not, so the best method depends on the photo. Note also that removing a few random blocks first raises the Costa Rica score from 73% to 96% at 5% removed (on average): grey blocks are not neutral.

Integrated gradients (Sundararajan and others, 2017) fix a weakness of the plain slope. The slope at the photo can be 0 where the score is flat (saturated), although the score depends on the pixel. Integrated gradients walk from a baseline \(x'\) (a black image) to the photo \(x\) in \(m\) small steps. They add up the slopes met on the way and multiply by the change of the pixel. Their selling point is completeness: the attributions of all pixels sum to the score of the photo minus the score of the baseline. For the hammerhead photo, with \(m = 64\) steps, they sum to 13.99, against \(14.78 - 0.91 = 13.86\) for the two scores, an error of 1%.

F(x) = x²x = 3, baseline 0→3 × ∫ 2(3α) dα = 9the true value: F(3) − F(0)|3 × mean(1.5, 3, 4.5, 6) = 11.254 steps|3 × 3(65 ÷ 64) = 9.1464 steps

\[ \mathrm{IG}_i(x) \;=\; (x_i - x'_i)\int_0^1 \frac{\partial F\big(x' + \alpha\,(x - x')\big)}{\partial x_i}\,d\alpha \;\approx\; (x_i - x'_i)\,\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\partial F\big(x' + \tfrac{k}{m}(x - x')\big)}{\partial x_i} \]

  • \(F\) is the class score, \(x\) the photo and \(x'\) the baseline. \(\alpha\) runs from 0 (the baseline) to 1 (the photo), and \(m\) is the number of steps used to approximate the integral.
  • Summing \(\mathrm{IG}_i\) over all \(i\) gives \(F(x) - F(x')\). The toy shows it: the true value is 9, 4 steps overshoot to 11.25, and 64 steps give 9.14.

SHAP for networks, in brief. A6 shared a score among the inputs by averaging over all orders in which the inputs could be revealed. A photo has 258,048 input numbers, so the sum over orders is out of reach. Implementations group pixels into larger regions, or use approximations, such as Deep SHAP, that reuse the network's slopes (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). The idea is the same as integrated gradients: share the difference between the photo's score and a reference score among the inputs so that the shares add up. Both depend on the reference. A black baseline treats black as "no information", and a different baseline gives a different map.

Where it misleads. Deletion has its own flaws: grey blocks are strange images, so the score may change because the picture looks odd and not because evidence was lost. Another family of checks inserts the top pixels into a blank image. Agreement of several methods and tests is better than a single test. Adebayo and colleagues (2018) also found that maps from integrated gradients and gradient times input keep much of the photo's own structure when the weights are randomised. The randomised-weights check matters for them too. In practice, run a randomised-weights check and a deletion test before you show any map to a colleague, and report which photo, class, baseline and fill you used.

Quick quiz

For \(F(x) = x^3\), baseline 0 and \(x = 2\), what do the true integrated gradients and the 2-step approximation give?

True value: \(F(2) - F(0) = 8\). With 2 steps, the slopes \(3t^2\) at \(t = 1\) and \(t = 2\) are 3 and 12. The mean is 7.5, and times \(x - x' = 2\) the result is 15. More steps move it toward 8.

Why compare a map's deletion curve with random order?

Removing many blocks lowers any score. The curve is informative only relative to the curve for random blocks: a good map falls faster.

Spurious cues: the water decides

Guess first. The network gives the dolphin photo a 41% score for "great white shark". You paint the dolphin out and leave only the sea. Does the great white shark score fall, stay or rise?

spurious cue
a feature that goes with the answer in the training data but is not what defines it, such as blue water for sharks (also called a shortcut)
context
all of the photo except the object, such as the water, the light and the other animals
control
an edit that changes one thing, to test one explanation

A network can reach an answer through a cue that came with the answer in its training photos, and edits that remove the object but keep the cue reveal it.

With the dolphin painted out, the great white shark score rises from 41% to 93%. A strip of water alone gets 63%, and the dolphin on grey gets under 4%. Pasted on green water, the hammerhead is called a great white shark (79%); on dark sand it stays a hammerhead (75%), and on a white card 88%. ResNet-18; the edits are our own, made by hand from the credited photos. Click a row or a chip.

The answer to the guess is "rise". Take the water away and the dolphin on a grey field is a grey whale (57%), with a shark score below 4%. Put the water back without the animal and the shark score is 93%. For this network and this photo, the grey-green sea surface drives the shark call, and the dolphin's body is not what gives it. The hammerhead shows the other side. On a neutral background (a white card, dark sand) the hammerhead's head outline is enough for 75 to 88%, so the outline is a cue the network does use. On green water the network changes its answer from hammerhead to great white shark. It uses both cues, and the water can override the outline.

This is a case of what Geirhos and colleagues (2020) call shortcut learning. Networks can adopt decision rules that score well on the usual test sets and fail under other conditions. If most training photos of sharks show water, a rule built on the colour of the water lowers the training loss. SharkCam meets this problem. If all training photos of sharks come from clear blue water, a detector may learn the water. It can then fail in the murky water of C3, or call a dolphin a shark.

Limits of this demonstration. It is one network and two photos, and the edits are crude. The pasted shark has hand-made edges, the "covered" photo has a visible seam, and the grey-green strip came from the same photo. The numbers are for these files only. Two JPEG versions of the same resized dolphin photo, saved at quality 90 and 88, scored 41% and 52% for great white shark and swapped the top two labels. Re-saving the shipped file at quality 75 gives 18% (exercise 3 repeats this). A single score is fragile. A controlled test would use many photos of an animal on many backgrounds, with the background and animal varied independently. In practice: look at wrong answers (error analysis). Use controls like these for any claim about what a network uses. Collect training photos where the background and the animal vary independently, and report results on groups, such as clear water and murky water (A4).

Debug tip. If a map highlights the background and not the object, do not assume the map is wrong. Run the control: remove the object and keep the background, then remove the background and keep the object. If the score follows the background, the network uses it.

Common belief. A network that scores well on a test set has learned the concept: a shark detector detects sharks.

Why it fails. A test set drawn like the training set rewards any cue that goes with the label in both. The water goes with sharks in both, so it counts as correct.

Better account. A network has learned whatever predicts the labels in its training data. Controls and groups outside the training pattern show which cue that was.

Quick quiz

The dolphin on grey scores under 4% for the great white shark and the sea alone scores 63%. What do the two numbers say together?

For this network the sea region supplies the evidence for that class. The animal alone does not support it, and the animal is not needed.

The hammerhead pasted on green water is called a great white shark. Is the network wrong to look at the water?

It is a reasonable rule in the training data, where sharks are in water, and a wrong one for telling species. What counts is whether the training data let it separate the two cues, and here the water outweighs the head outline.

Attention maps and their limits

Guess first. A vision transformer looks at a photo through 12 attention heads. In its last layer, you replace the attention weights by equal weights for all 196 patches, so that no patch is preferred. Does the hammerhead score collapse, drop a little, or stay?

attention weights
numbers that say how much one token reads from the others (D4)
head
one of the parallel attention computations in a layer, with its own weights
class token
a special token whose final value is used for the prediction (E1); its attention to the 196 image patches is the usual map

An attention map is easy to draw and shows where a token read from, which need not be where the evidence for the answer lies.

With equal weights in all 12 heads of the last layer the hammerhead score goes from 88% to 81%, and with the weights shuffled across the photo to 77%. The maps of single heads differ, with rank agreement with the gradient map between 0.37 and 0.61. Only 5.5% of the mean attention falls on the shark, which covers 11% of the photo. Torchvision ViT-B/16 (ImageNet), photo squashed to 224 by 224, attention of the class token in the last layer. The same network calls the dolphin photo a great white shark (86%).

The answer to the guess is "drop a little". The last layer's attention can be flattened and the network still says "hammerhead", at 81%. Choose the dolphin photo: the shark score goes from 86% to 70% with equal weights and 62% with shuffled weights. This fits the finding of Jain and Wallace (2019) that attention weights are often uncorrelated with gradient-based measures of importance. They also found that very different weights can give the same prediction. Wiegreffe and Pinter (2019) reply that whether attention is an explanation depends on what you mean by one, and that such experiments do not disprove its use. Both papers study text models, so they motivate the test here and do not settle the vision case.

Look at the map of single heads. The heads differ. In the hammerhead photo the agreement with the gradient map is 0.61 for head 12, 0.55 for head 3 and 0.37 for head 4. The mean over heads gives 0.54 (0.23 for the dolphin photo). Where does the attention go? The shark's outline (a mask made by colour thresholding, with a threshold we set by hand) covers 11% of the Costa Rica photo. Another threshold would change the 11% and the 5.5% a little. In the mean over heads, only 5.5% of the attention falls on the outline, which is less than a uniform spread would give. The bright patches in the figure are in the water. Head 9 puts 15% on the shark and head 1 puts 1%. For comparison, 20% of the ResNet's saliency and 28% of its Grad-CAM weight fall inside the outline. There are three reasons for the gap between attention and evidence. First, the output of a head is a mix of value vectors, and a patch with a large weight but a small, uninformative value adds little. Second, the residual connections (B6) pass the class token's own earlier state forward, so a layer can change the answer less than its weights suggest. Third, this experiment changed only the last of 12 layers, so the evidence has already been gathered by the earlier ones.

Where it misleads. Flattening attention is one test of one layer. It shows that this layer's weights do not decide the answer for these photos. It does not show that attention elsewhere is irrelevant. The map for a head is not a map for a class: it is the same for all classes. In practice, treat an attention map as a description of how the model moves information, and use occlusion, deletion or gradient-based maps, with controls, for claims about evidence. If you must show attention, show several heads and layers, and say what a flat-attention test did to the score.

Common belief. Where a transformer puts its attention is what it looks at, so an attention map explains the answer.

Why it fails. In the figure, flattening all 12 heads of the last layer moves the hammerhead score only from 88% to 81%, and only 5.5% of the attention falls on the shark. Jain and Wallace (2019) found a weak link between attention and gradient importance in text models too.

Better account. An attention map shows where one token read from in one layer. Treat it as a description of how information moves, and test claims about evidence with occlusion, deletion or controls.

Quick quiz

The score barely changes when one layer's attention is made uniform. Does that prove that attention is useless in the model?

No. Only one layer was changed in two photos. Earlier layers may have moved the evidence into the class token already, and the other layers' attention was untouched.

Why can two different sets of attention weights give the same prediction?

The output depends on weights, value vectors and the layers after them. Different mixes can add up to similar vectors, and the residual path takes information around the attention step.

Come back later

(B4, B9) Backpropagation gives the slope of the loss with respect to the weights. What one extra step gives a saliency map, and why is the logit used?

Continue the backward pass to the input: the slope of the class score with respect to the input values. The logit is used because the softmax probability saturates, so its slope is near 0 for a confident class.

(B7, B9) After fine-tuning SharkCam on murky water, how could an explanation method help you check for forgetting, and what would you still need to run?

Compare the maps on clear-water photos before and after fine-tuning: if the map moves from the shark to the water, the cue has changed. You still need the old water's held-out accuracy (B7) and a deletion test, because a map alone is not evidence.

(A4, B9) A shark detector scores 95% on a test set of 200 photos, all in blue water. What does the spurious-cue figure suggest you also do?

Test by group (blue water, murky water, no animal, other animals), with controls like painting out the animal, as the subgroup analysis of A4 does for the clinic data.
